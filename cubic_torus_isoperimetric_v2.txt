A soap bubble in ordinary space is a perfect sphere. That is not an
accident of chemistry: among all shapes enclosing a given volume, the
sphere has the smallest possible surface area. This is the classical
isoperimetric inequality, known since antiquity:

  In ordinary three-dimensional space, every region E satisfies

      (surface area of E)^3  >=  36 pi (volume of E)^2,

  with equality if and only if E is a round ball.

The same question arises in a strange room: not free space, but a cube
whose opposite faces are glued together.

Picture a cube. Walk toward the right wall and you pass through to the
left wall. Walk through the front wall and you appear at the back. Walk
up through the ceiling and you come out through the floor. Every
direction wraps. This is the flat three-torus. It is a perfectly
ordinary room of finite volume, but it has no boundary in the usual
sense, no outside, and no edge. A soap bubble living inside it can
drift all the way around by crossing a face and never meet a wall.

The question: in this wrapped room, what shape encloses a given
volume with the least surface area?

The answer, conjectured decades ago and proved here in full, splits
into three phases by volume:

  very small volumes          a round ball
  medium volumes              a wrapped tube
  up to half the total        a slab between parallel coordinate planes

and then mirrors by complement (the answer for a volume exceeding
half the room is the complement of the answer for the complementary
smaller volume). A ball is exactly the sphere one already knows. A tube
is a solid cylinder whose ends are glued across a face, so it wraps
once around the room. A slab is a flat region on one side of a pair of
parallel coordinate planes -- a slice of bread cut between two tori.

The two switch volumes, at which the optimal shape changes from ball
to tube and from tube to slab, are exact constants:

      V1  =  4 pi / 81     approx  0.1551     (ball becomes tube)
      V2  =  1 / pi        approx  0.3183     (tube becomes slab)

This is exactly what happens at every volume, and that
at the two switch volumes both neighbouring shapes tie.



The building. The mathematical room is the period-2 torus

      T  =  R^3 / (2 Z)^3,

meaning the closed cube [0,2]^3 with opposite faces identified: the
face x=0 glued to the face x=2, and similarly in y and z. Its total
volume is 8. The factor 2 (rather than using [0,1]^3) keeps every
constant clean and matches the reference paper. The familiar unit
torus T1 = R^3/Z^3 is recovered by dividing every length by 2. Work
proceeds on T throughout, and conversion to T1 happens once at the end.

Regions and their sizes. A region E is any bounded measurable
subset of T. Define its fractional volume and boundary area:

      V(E)  =  |E| / 8              (a number in [0,1])
      A(E)  =  Area(boundary E) / 8

Here "boundary" means perimeter in the standard weak sense; for the
shapes that matter it is an ordinary smooth surface. Two symmetries
drive the whole problem:

   complement:  A(T ∖ E) = A(E)     and     V(T ∖ E) = 1 - V(E);

   scaling:     going from T1 to T doubles every length, so it
                quadruples every physical area. After dividing by 8,
                fractional areas on T are half of those on T1, and
                fractional volumes coincide.

The first symmetry lets the reader only discuss V in [0,1/2]; the larger half
is the complement of the smaller half.

The profile. The single function one wants to determine is

      I(V)  =  inf { A(E) : V(E) = V }.

The isoperimetric profile of T is I.

Smoothness and constant mean curvature. Standard results of
geometric measure theory (Almgren 1976 and many refinements) give
the following. For every V in (0,1):

   * The infimum I(V) is achieved by some region E.
   * The boundary of E is a finite union of smooth embedded pieces.
   * Each connected piece has constant mean curvature.

"Constant mean curvature" means: taking a unit normal along the
surface, let k1, k2 be the two principal curvatures (the rates at
which the surface bends in the two tangent directions). The sum

      h  =  k1 + k2

is the same at every point of the boundary. Three examples fix the
convention:

      a ball of radius R          h = 2 / R
      a cylinder of radius R      h = 1 / R
      a plane                     h = 0

The reason h is constant is first variation. Push the boundary
outward along its normal by a tiny amount. The change in surface
area is proportional to h times the amount of volume absorbed. For a
minimiser, that change must vanish among volume-preserving
perturbations. So h hangs on as a Lagrange multiplier and is the
same everywhere.

Which shape can a minimiser take? The structure theorem for flat
three-manifolds (Hauswirth-Perez-Romon-Ros 2004, with earlier inputs
of Ritore, Ros, and Ritore-Ros) says that any minimiser on T falls into one of
these cases:

   (Slab)  a union of parallel flat tori;
   (Ball)  a round sphere;
   (Tube)  a circular-cylinder quotient (a solid tube around a
           shortest closed geodesic);
   (Nonstd) a connected closed surface of genus 2, 3, or 4,
            NOT one of the above.

The first three are the "standard" shapes -- one knows their volumes
and areas exactly and can compute their contribution to I(V). The
genus-2, 3, 4 case is the "nonstandard" one. The whole strategy of
the proof is to rule out nonstandard minimisers at every volume.

A key estimate accompanies this theorem: for any nonstandard
connected minimiser at any volume,

      h^2 * A  <=  pi.      (CURV)

In the convention (h = k1 + k2), this is Theorem 5 combined with the
genus-4 conclusion H=0 in Theorem 7 of Hauswirth-Perez-Romon-Ros
2004. The form is dimensionally clean: h has units 1/length, A is a
pure number after dividing by total volume 8 (so units length^0), and
pi is a pure number.

The structure theorem, existence, and analytic regularity (the last
due to Blatt 2024), are the only black-box inputs. Everything else is
proved from scratch.

Define the model profile

      F(V)  =  min { (9 pi / 2)^{1/3} v^{2/3},   sqrt(pi v),   1 },

with v = min(V, 1-V). The three pieces are the fractional areas of
the ball, tube, and slab, which are derived below. They
interlock. The first piece wins for 0 <= v <= V1, the second for
V1 <= v <= V2, and the third for V2 <= v <= 1/2, where

      V1  =  4 pi / 81,     V2  =  1 / pi.

Symmetry about V=1/2 fills in the rest by complements.

      THEOREM (main).

      For every V in (0,1), with v = min(V, 1-V), the profile of T is

          I(V)  =  min { (9 pi / 2)^{1/3} v^{2/3},
                                sqrt(pi v),   1 }.

      On the unit torus T1 this is

          I_1(V) = min { (36 pi)^{1/3} v^{2/3},   2 sqrt(pi v),   2 }.

      Moreover, for 0 < V <= 1/2, up to isometry and null sets, every
      isoperimetric minimiser E is one of:

          0 < V <= V1         a ball of radius (6 V / pi)^{1/3}
          V1 <= V <= V2       a solid circular tube of radius
                              sqrt(4 V / pi) about a shortest
                              closed geodesic
          V2 <= V <= 1/2      a slab of width 2 V between coordinate
                              tori

      Both listed shapes occur at each shared endpoint. No other
      equality cases exist. For V > 1/2 the minimisers are complements
      of those at 1 - V. At V=0 or V=1 the theorem is trivial.

The proof proceeds in a single chain. After a small toolkit, the
three standard shapes are computed and the two-volume reduction is
established. The structural lemmas are then derived, the endpoint
cases are eliminated, and a final numerical certification closes the
argument.

  Note on radii. On T, a physical ball of radius R has volume
  (4/3) pi R^3, so its fractional volume is V = pi R^3 / 6. Thus
  R = (6 V / pi)^{1/3} in the ball phase. On T1 the same ball has
  radius (3 V / (4 pi))^{1/3} = R/2. Both expressions are correct and
  describe the same shape; they refer to different tori.

  For a tube on T the axial length is 2 and the radius r satisfies
  V = pi r^2 / 4, so r = sqrt(4 V / pi). On T1 the axial length is 1
  and r = sqrt(V / pi).

  For a slab on T of width W (coordinate distance 0 to 2) the
  fractional volume is V = W/2. On T1 the width is 2V measured
  fractionally: W' = V.



Four elementary tools make up everything. They are classical.

  Chain rule and second-derivative test. If a smooth function
  has a local minimum at x0, its first derivative vanishes and its
  second derivative is nonnegative. Applications go both directions:
  forward, to pin down minima of one-variable functions, and
  backward, to squeeze inequalities between functions known to
  differ only in the second derivative.

  Plane isoperimetric inequality. For any planar region with
  area a and perimeter p,

        p^2  >=  4 pi a,

  with equality only for a disk. Equivalently, among planar shapes of
  area a, the disk of perimeter 2 sqrt(pi a) is uniquely smallest.
  One use of this form is made, and only once.

  Jensen's inequality. If f: [0,1] -> R is convex, meaning
  f((a+b)/2) <= (f(a)+f(b))/2 for all a,b, and mu is a probability
  measure on [0,1], then

        f( integral x dmu(x) )  <=  integral f(x) dmu(x).

  Convexity of f turns "average then apply f" into "apply f then
  average", and the latter is always at least the former. One uses this
  once, on a specific convex function of the arclength parameter
  along a planar slice.

  Reflection. Reflecting a region across a plane or a torus
  face preserves its volume and its boundary area.

One more fact that needs a small argument.

  Concavity of the profile. The profile I is concave:

        I((1-t)V0 + t V1)  <=  (1-t) I(V0) + t I(V1)

  for all V0, V1 in [0,1] and t in [0,1]. The proof comes for free
  once the motion of a minimiser under parallel
  perturbation is understood, and that machinery appears in the parallel-variation discussion below.
  Here is the shape of the argument, so it can be cited early.

  At each V0 in (0,1) a minimiser exists (existence theorem of
  the setup). Perturb its boundary outward and inward along its
  outward normal. Over a small enough range the perturbed surfaces
  stay embedded, and volume V(t) and boundary area A(t) vary
  smoothly. Invert V(t) near t=0 and define the area-vs-volume
  curve C(V) := A(t(V)). Two facts:

        C(V0) = I(V0)     (the minimiser achieves the profile there)
        C(V)  >= I(V)     near V0 (I is the infimum over all regions)

  The second fact holds because C(V) is the boundary area of one
  specific region of volume V, so it cannot undershoot the infimum.

  A direct computation of C'' (the parallel-variation computation, using the parallel-variation
  formulas) gives C''(V0) <= 0. The reason is the identity

        (1/8) * int over the boundary of (2K - h^2) dS
        = -(1/8) * int over the boundary of (k1^2 + k2^2) dS,

  where K = k1 k2 is the Gauss curvature and h = k1 + k2 the
  curvature sum; the right-hand side is <= 0 pointwise.

  Now suppose I is not concave. Then there is a chord L on [a,b]
  and interior c with I(c) < L(c). Pick any small epsilon > 0 and
  set g(x) := I(x) - L(x) - epsilon (x-a)(x-b). Then g(a) = g(b) = 0,
  and g(c) < 0 for small enough epsilon. So g attains a strictly
  negative minimum at some interior x0.

  Set q(x) := L(x) + epsilon (x-a)(x-b) + g(x0). Then q(x0) = I(x0),
  and q(x) <= I(x) for x in (a,b), so q touches I from below at x0.
  Also q'' = 2 epsilon > 0, i.e. q is strictly convex.

  At x0 a minimiser E_0 exists. Its parallel-variation upper
  support C satisfies C(x0) = I(x0) and C(x) >= I(x) >= q(x) near
  x0, with equality at x0. Hence C - q >= 0 near x0 with equality
  at x0, so C''(x0) >= q''(x0) = 2 epsilon > 0. But one has already
  computed C'' <= 0. Contradiction, so I is concave.

  The full details of the parallel-variation computation are in
  the parallel-variation computation. Also I(0) = I(1) = 0, which is clear from the
  definition.

These tools plus Almgren's existence theorem (cited in the setup)
and the HPRR structure theorem are the entire input. Everything
else below is proved from this base. The tools begin to be used here.



The three standard shapes compute as follows. Each computation
is a single line of algebra from the ball/cylinder/slab formula in
classical geometry.

  Ball on T. A ball of physical radius R (with R <= 1, so it
  does not wrap around) has physical volume (4/3) pi R^3 and physical
  surface area 4 pi R^2. Fractional volume and area:

        V = (4/3) pi R^3 / 8 = pi R^3 / 6,
        A = 4 pi R^2 / 8     = pi R^2 / 2.

  Solve V for R: R = (6 V / pi)^{1/3}. Then

        A_ball(V)  =  (pi/2) (6 V / pi)^{2/3}
                   =  (9 pi / 2)^{1/3}  V^{2/3}.

  This formula is only relevant for the volumes at which a ball of
  that radius fits, i.e. R <= 1, i.e. V <= pi/6 approx 0.5236. That
  is above V2 approx 0.3183, so a ball is a legitimate competitor at
  every volume in [0, V2]. It is not however the minimiser for V > V1.

  Tube on T. A solid tube of radius r around a period-2 axis has
  physical volume 2 pi r^2 (the wrapping sides) and physical surface
  area 2 pi r * 2 = 4 pi r (no caps, because the two ends are glued).
  Fractional volume and area:

        V = 2 pi r^2 / 8 = pi r^2 / 4,
        A = 4 pi r / 8   = pi r / 2.

  Solve V for r: r = sqrt(4 V / pi). Then

        A_tube(V)  =  pi sqrt(4 V / pi) / 2
                   =  sqrt(pi V).

  The tube is a legitimate competitor as long as r <= 1, i.e.
  V <= pi/4 approx 0.785. That is above 1/2, so the tube is a valid
  competitor for every V in [0, 1/2].

  Slab on T. A slab between two parallel coordinate tori separated
  by distance W (in period-2 coordinates, 0 <= W <= 2) has physical
  volume 4 W (transverse area 2*2 times width W) and physical surface
  area 2*4 = 8 (two cross-sectional tori of area 4 each). Fractional:

        V = 4 W / 8 = W / 2,
        A = 8 / 8   = 1.

  Solve V for W: W = 2 V. Then

        A_slab(V)  =  1,      valid for all V in [0,1].

  The switch volumes.

  Ball = Tube:
        (9 pi / 2)^{1/3} V^{2/3}  =  (pi V)^{1/2}.
  Square both sides:
        (9 pi / 2)^{2/3} V^{4/3}  =  pi V.
  Divide by pi V (assuming V > 0):
        (9/2)^{2/3} pi^{-1/3} V^{1/3}  =  1.
  So
        V^{1/3}  =  pi^{1/3} (2/9)^{2/3},
        V        =  pi * (4/81) = 4 pi / 81 = V1.       [ ]

  Tube = Slab:
        (pi V)^{1/2}  =  1  implies  V = 1/pi = V2.     [ ]

  At each switch both shapes tie. This defines

      V1 = 4 pi / 81   ~  0.1551,
      V2 = 1 / pi      ~  0.3183.

  The standard lemma.

  Lemma 5.5 (standard competitors and their optimality).
  Let F(V) be the model profile above, and for each V let E_std(V)
  be the collection of standard shapes at fractional volume V: any of
  a ball (if V <= pi/6), a tube, a slab, or their complements.

  (i)  For every V in [0,1], some member of E_std(V) achieves A = F(V).

  (ii) Every standard region E of fractional volume V satisfies
       A(E) >= F(V), with equality exactly at the shapes described
       in the main theorem (ball/tube/slab and their complements).

  Proof. Item (i) is direct from 5.1-5.3 and the complement symmetry.

  Item (ii) is the substantive half. Each standard shape is treated.

  Ball-bound. A ball of physical radius R in T lifts to a genuine
  Euclidean ball in R^3. Its 2Z^3-lattice translates are pairwise
  interior-disjoint: if two had overlapping interiors, their
  spherical boundaries would intersect in a small circle, forcing a
  piece of one to lie inside the other -- a self-intersection of an
  embedded boundary in R^3, impossible.

  Let W be the fractional volume of this lifted ball (W equals V if
  the ball does not wrap, but could in principle be larger if it
  wraps -- not a case one needs, since wrap forces R >= 1, ruled out by
  R <= 1). Then

        A = (9 pi / 2)^{1/3} W^{2/3} >= (9 pi / 2)^{1/3} V^{2/3}

  by W >= V. That is exactly the ball piece of F(V).

  Tube-bound. A compact circular-cylinder quotient of T lifts to a
  circular cylinder in R^3 with axis containing a nonzero lattice
  vector. Choose a primitive such vector m in Z^3, and let k = |m|
  be its length. In period-2 coordinates, the axis projects back to a
  closed curve of length 2k. The physical volume enclosed is
  W = 2k pi r^2 (r = tube radius). Fractional: W = k pi r^2 / 4.
  Physical surface area (no caps, ends glued) is 4 pi k r. Fractional:
  A = k pi r / 2. Then

        A  =  k pi r / 2   =  sqrt(pi k) * r * sqrt(k pi / 4)   [algebra]
           =  sqrt(pi k W) = sqrt(pi W) * sqrt(k)
           >= sqrt(pi W)   since k >= 1.

  By W >= V, A >= sqrt(pi V). That is the tube piece of F(V).

  Slab-bound. A connected closed planar surface in T has a primitive
  integral conormal m in Z^3, and physical area 4 |m|, fractional
  area |m| / 2. A single planar torus does not separate T. To enclose
  a region, at least two parallel planar tori are required. Their
  fractional areas sum to at least 2 * (1/2) = 1. Slab A >= 1. That
  is the slab piece of F(V).

  Finally, equality analysis. In the ball case, equality needs W = V
  and R <= 1: the ball fits. In the tube case, equality needs k = 1
  and W = V: a shortest tube, matching the tube branch. In the slab
  case, equality needs exactly two planar tori with |m| = 1:
  coordinate slabs of width 2V. Complements give V > 1/2. All of
  these are the shapes listed in the theorem. [ ]

One more standard-shape wrinkle is needed: the model
profile F coincides with the sphere formula up to V1, the tube
formula on [V1, V2], and the slab formula on [V2, 1/2] -- and then
symmetric. In particular F is smooth on each open phase [0, V1],
[V1, V2], [V2, 1/2].



Here is the grand strategy. The proof separately establishes that every
minimiser at V1 and V2 is standard. From that hypothesis alone, a
concavity argument on I-VS-F upgrades the conclusion to every volume.
This is the engine that lets the reader avoid examining nonstandard shapes at
generic volumes entirely.

  The model profile has nonnegative "second-order" invariant.

  For any function F, consider the quantity

        S[F](V)  =  F''(V)  +  (F'(V))^2 / F(V).

  A quick computation (twice) gives:

  (a) On the ball phase, F(V) = c V^{2/3} with c = (9 pi / 2)^{1/3}.
  Then F' = (2/3) c V^{-1/3}, F'' = -(2/9) c V^{-4/3}, and

        F'' + (F')^2 / F
          = -(2/9) c V^{-4/3} + (4/9) c^2 V^{-2/3} / (c V^{2/3})
          = (2/9) c V^{-4/3}   >  0.

  (b) On the tube phase, F(V) = (pi V)^{1/2}. Then
  F' = (1/2) (pi/V)^{1/2}, F'' = -(1/4) (pi)^{1/2} V^{-3/2}, and

        (F')^2 / F   =  (1/4) pi / V / (pi V)^{1/2}
                     =  (1/4) (pi)^{1/2} V^{-3/2}
                     =  -F''.

        So F'' + (F')^2 / F  =  0.

  (c) On the slab phase, F(V) = 1. Then F' = F'' = 0, and

        F'' + (F')^2 / F  =  0.

  By symmetry about 1/2 the same holds for V > 1/2. So on each smooth
  phase of F one has

        F''(V)  +  (F'(V))^2 / F(V)  >=  0.        (MODEL-ODE)

  In the ball phase the inequality is strict. In the tube and slab
  phases it is an equality.

  Support functions of the profile.

  Let V0 be an interior point and E0 a minimiser at V0, so A(E0) =
  I(V0). Let h be the constant mean curvature of its boundary and A0
  its fractional area (= I(V0) here).

  Parallel perturbation. Push the boundary of E0 outward by t along
  its outward unit normal, and let V(t), A(t) be fractional volume
  and area of the resulting region. Standard computation (HPRR
  Section 3):

        V'(0)  =  A0,       A'(0)  =  h A0,
        V''(0) =  h A0,     A''(0) =  (1/8) * int(2K) over the boundary.

  where K = k1 k2 is the Gauss curvature of the boundary.

  The parallel variation gives a smooth LOCAL UPPER SUPPORT of I at
  V0, without needing concavity of I as an input. Concretely, define

        C(V)  :=  A(t(V)),

  where t(V) is the inverse of t -> V(t) near t = 0. The inverse
  exists because V'(0) = A0 > 0, so V(t) is strictly monotone near
  0. Then C is smooth near V0, and:

        C(V0)  =  A(0)  =  I(V0)                  (E0 attains I there)
        C(V)   >= I(V)  near V0                   (C(V) is the area
                                                  of one specific
                                                  region at volume V,
                                                  so cannot be below
                                                  the infimum I(V))
        C'(V0) =  h                               (chain rule + V'=A0)
        C''(V0) =  (1/8) * int(2K) / A0^2  -  h^2 / A0     (chain rule).

  Explicitly, using V'(0) = A0 and V''(0) = h A0,

        A''(0) - h A'(0)      int(2K)             h^2
        -----------------  =  --------  -  -------.          (SUPPORT-RAW)
              A0^2             8 A0^2             A0

  For a CONNECTED boundary of genus g and normalised area A0, the
  Gauss-Bonnet formula and Gauss's Theorema Egregium convert this
  into the standard form

        (C^2 C'')(V0)  =  - h^2 C(V0) + pi (1 - g).          (SUPPORT)

  This is the parallel-variation relation (HPRR Section 3).

  A nonstandard E0 (genus g >= 2) then satisfies pi (1-g) <= -pi,
  giving

        C''(V0)  <  0.                                       (STRICT)

  For standard E0 (g = 0 ball, g = 1 tube, h = 0 with g = 1 planar
  slab), the SUPPORT formula recovers the sign of C'' as follows:

        ball:  g = 0, h = 2/R,  C^2 C'' = -h^2 C + pi = -pi    < 0,
        tube:  g = 1, h = 1/r,  C^2 C'' = -h^2 C + 0           < 0,
        slab:  h = 0, g = 1,    C^2 C'' = 0                    = 0.

  These agree with the direct area-vs-volume formulas

        A_ball(V)  =  (9 pi / 2)^{1/3} V^{2/3}     (concave),
        A_tube(V)  =  sqrt(pi V)                    (concave),
        A_slab(V)  =  1                             (constant).

  So C'' <= 0 for every minimiser E0, standard or nonstandard, with
  strict inequality except for planar slabs. This is the input that
  the concavity argument (stated above) uses: a smooth local upper
  support of I at any minimiser has C''(V0) <= 0, and the chord
  contradiction gives the concavity of I.

  For convenience, define the "invariant" of any upper support C at
  any minimiser:

        G[C](V0)  =  C''(V0)  +  h^2 / C(V0),  with h = C'(V0).

  By (SUPPORT),

        G[C](V0)  =  pi (1 - g) / C(V0)^2.        (G-SUPPORT)

  For a standard shape, the right side is >= 0 (or zero). For a
  nonstandard shape with g >= 2, the right side is strictly negative.

  The concavity upgrade: two-volume reduction.

  Proposition 6.3 (pre:reduction). Suppose at V1 = 4 pi / 81 and at
  V2 = 1/pi every minimiser is standard. Then I = F on all of [0,1],
  and every minimiser is standard.

  Proof. Let P = {0, V1, V2, 1/2, 1-V2, 1-V1, 1} be the partition of
  [0,1] induced by the phase changes and complement symmetry.

  Step A: I = F on P. At V1, V2 by hypothesis. At 1/2: Hadwiger's
  theorem (2D case, 1972) states that a slab minimises at 1/2 on
  the cubic torus, giving I(1/2) = 1 = F(1/2). At 0 and 1 both
  sides are 0. At 1-V1, 1-V2 by complement. Good.

  Step B: I <= F on all of [0,1]. Lemma 5.5 gives F(V) attained by a
  standard shape at every V, so I(V) <= F(V).

  Step C: Suppose for contradiction that I < F at some interior
  point of a phase interval [a,b] between consecutive elements of P.
  Let D = I - F. D is continuous, D(a) = D(b) = 0 (Step A), and D
  attains a negative minimum at some V0 in (a,b). So D(V0) < 0.

  Let E0 be a minimiser at V0. Because D(V0) < 0, E0 is NOT a standard
  shape (Lemma 5.5 would give A(E0) >= F(V0) = I(V0), contradicting
  D(V0) < 0). So E0 is nonstandard (genus g >= 2).

  Let C be the local upper support of I at E0, constructed by
  parallel variation (the parallel-variation computation). The key move is to route the
  argument through the SMOOTH function

        D_tilde(V)  :=  C(V) - F(V).

  Because C is smooth (the parallel-variation computation) and F is smooth on the interior of
  a phase interval, D_tilde is smooth near V0.

  Two evaluations at V0:

        D_tilde(V0)  =  C(V0) - F(V0)
                     =  I(V0) - F(V0)
                     =  D(V0)  <  0,

  using the minimiser property C(V0) = I(V0).

  For V near V0, the upper-support inequality C(V) >= I(V) and the
  fact that V0 is an interior minimum of D combine to give

        D_tilde(V)  =  C(V) - F(V)
                    >=  I(V) - F(V)
                    =   D(V)
                    >=  D(V0).

  So D_tilde has a local minimum at V0 (with value D(V0) < 0). Since
  D_tilde is smooth, its first and second derivatives at V0 satisfy

        D_tilde'(V0)   =  0,
        D_tilde''(V0)  >= 0.

  Because F is smooth, these translate directly to

        C'(V0)  =  F'(V0),                         (eq a)
        C''(V0)  >=  F''(V0).                      (eq b)

  Recall from the parallel-variation computation that C'(V0) = h (the mean curvature of E0's
  boundary). So F'(V0) = h as a corollary.

  By (STRICT) the left-hand side below is negative. A contradiction
  follows in one clean pass.

  Recall from above:

        C'(V0) = F'(V0),     C''(V0) >= F''(V0),     C(V0) < F(V0).

  Evaluate the G-invariant at V0. By (SUPPORT) and h = C'(V0):

        pi(1-g)/C(V0)^2  =  C''(V0) + h^2/C(V0)
                         =  C''(V0) + (F')^2/C(V0).

  Bound the right-hand side from below in three steps:

        C''(V0) >= F''(V0),                                  [eq b]
        (F')^2/C(V0) >= (F')^2/F(V0)   (since 0 < C(V0) < F(V0)),
        F''(V0) + (F')^2/F(V0) >= 0.                          [MODEL-ODE]

  (If F'(V0) = 0, e.g. on the slab phase, the middle step degenerates
  to 0 = 0; the conclusion still goes through, using F'' = 0 there.)
  Chaining:

        pi(1-g)/C(V0)^2  >=  F''(V0) + (F')^2/F(V0)  >=  0.

  But (STRICT) gives pi(1-g)/C(V0)^2 <= -pi/C(V0)^2 < 0.

  Step C completed: contradiction. [ ]

  Step D: equality cases. Step C has already ruled out I(V) < F(V)
  for ANY V in the interior of a phase interval. Hence I = F on the
  whole interior. So for any interior V0, I = F in an open
  neighborhood of V0.

  Let E0 be a minimiser at V0. If E0 is standard, the case is settled. If E0
  is nonstandard, the upper support C at V0 has C(V0) = I(V0) =
  F(V0) and C'(V0) = h. Because I = F in a neighborhood, the
  upper-support inequality C >= I = F holds near V0, so

        D_tilde(V)  :=  C(V) - F(V)  >=  0    near V0,  with D_tilde(V0) = 0.

  The same smooth-minimum argument as in Step C (on D_tilde rather
  than on D) then gives

        D_tilde'(V0)   =  0,       (i.e., C'(V0) = F'(V0)),
        D_tilde''(V0)  >= 0.       (i.e., C''(V0) >= F''(V0)).

  Combined with SUPPORT at V0 and the identity C(V0) = F(V0):

        pi(1-g)/C(V0)^2  =  C''(V0) + h^2/C(V0)
                         =  C''(V0) + (F')^2/F(V0).

  The left side is <= -pi/C(V0)^2 < 0 (nonstandard, g >= 2). The
  right side is >= F''(V0) + (F')^2/F(V0) = 0 by MODEL-ODE.
  Contradiction. So E0 is standard.

  Step E: on the partition point 1/2, F(1/2) = 1 and I(1/2) = 1 by
  Hadwiger. On other partition points V1, V2, 1-V1, 1-V2 the
  hypothesis and complement symmetry give the answer. At V=0 and V=1
  the theorem is trivial.

  This completes the proof of Proposition 6.3. [ ]

What this proposition does for the reader. The rest of the proof is devoted to showing
that at V1 and V2 every minimiser is standard. Once that is
established, Proposition 6.3 gives the full theorem at every volume
and every equality case. So now "switch modes": from here on, V
is FIXED at V1 or V2, and the minimisers are classified there.



Why one wants symmetry. A minimiser can a priori sit anywhere in the
wrapped room. But symmetries of the ambient space force structure. The
argument shows that any nonstandard minimiser can be positioned so
that it is invariant under reflection in each coordinate -- equivalently,
under all eight sign changes of the coordinate triple. This is what
lets the reader reduce the problem to a cube, in the symmetry, slicing, and endpoint machinery.

  Setting. Throughout the symmetry discussion, E is a nonstandard minimiser of the
  isoperimetric problem at some fractional volume V in (0, 1/2].
  Write Sigma = boundary of E. The structure theorem noted at the outset gives:

        Sigma is a connected closed embedded analytic surface of
        genus g >= 2.

  Nothing is yet assumed about where E sits.

  Lemma (reflection symmetry). After translating the coordinate
  origins, the original region E is invariant under each elementary
  reflection of the coordinate axes:

        (x1, x2, x3)  ->  (-x1, x2, x3),  and analogous in x2, x3.

  Note. In the period-2 torus, the maps "reflection in x1 = 0" and
  "reflection in x1 = 1" are exactly the same map, because

        x1  ->  -x1   is  x1  ->  2 - x1 mod 2.

  So "the six coordinate tori" means three pairs of the same
  reflection map, one map per coordinate.

  Proof of Lemma 7.2.

  Fix one coordinate, say x1. There is some bisecting torus to find
  x1 = a with E invariant under reflection in it; translating the
  coordinate origin then turns a into 0.

  Step 1 (a bisecting half-torus). For a in [0,1], let

        H_a  =  { x1 in [a, a+1] }

  be the "slab" between the parallel tori x1 = a and x1 = a+1. It
  occupies half of T in the x1 direction. Let v(a) be the fractional
  volume of E inside H_a. As a varies continuously, v(a) varies
  continuously. Because the two ends of the interval [a, a+1] are the
  same torus in T (the map a -> a+1 is translation by half the
  period 2), the sets H_a and H_{a+1} partition T. Therefore

        v(a)  +  v(a+1)  =  V.         (BISECTION)

  Let a run over [0,1]. By continuity, v takes every value between
  its minimum and maximum on [0,1]. From (BISECTION) one has
  min <= V/2 <= max. So some a in [0,1] satisfies

        v(a)  =  V/2.

  This is the bisecting half-torus.

  Step 2 (two reflected competitors). Cut E along the bisecting
  surface x1 = a. Inside H_a, the part E  intersect  H_a has fractional volume
  V/2. Reflect it across both faces of H_a (the two symmetric copies
  x1 = a and x1 = a+1 agree, so no seam mismatch). The result is a
  full periodic region C_a^+ of fractional volume V in T. Similarly,
  reflect E  intersect  (T ∖ H_a) across the same faces to get another full
  periodic region C_a^-.

  These are legitimate competitors of volume V. They reflect no
  extra perimeter at the seam: the two reflected copies of a
  piece of characteristic function lie on the same surface trace
  across the seam, so no seam area appears, even if the original
  dE is tangent to the seam.

  Step 3 (each competitor minimises). The important identity is

        perim(C_a^+) + perim(C_a^-)  =  perim(E) = A (fractional).

  Here perim(E) = A is the total fractional boundary area of E. The
  identity holds because each half contributes exactly its relative
  perimeter in H_a (no seam) and the two halves sum to E.

  Because both C_a^+ and C_a^- are competitors of volume V,
  perim(C_a^+) >= I(V) and perim(C_a^-) >= I(V), by definition of
  the profile I. On the other hand perim(E) = I(V) because E is a
  minimiser. Summing:

        2 I(V)  <=  perim(C_a^+) + perim(C_a^-)  =  A  =  I(V).

  So both inequalities are equalities. Each of C_a^+, C_a^- is
  therefore an isoperimetric minimiser at volume V.

  Step 4 (each competitor is analytic). Standard minimising-surface
  regularity (analytic elliptic PDE theory, Blatt 2024) gives that
  the boundary of each competitor is a real analytic surface.

  Step 5 (shared open patch). Inside the open half-torus H_a, both
  occupied and vacant volume are positive (V/2 each). So E's
  boundary at x1 in the interior of [a, a+1] contains an open
  nonempty patch. This patch survives reflection: it is also a
  patch of the boundary of C_a^+ and C_a^-. Similarly for the other
  half.

  Step 6 (competitor boundary is connected). The structure theorem
  lists the possible shapes of a minimising boundary: a union of
  parallel flat tori, or a single connected genus 0-4 surface. The
  patch just identified is non-planar (since E's boundary is
  nonstandard, not a slab). Therefore the competitor's boundary is
  not a slab-union, hence connected.

  Step 7 (analytic continuation). There is now: a connected closed
  embedded analytic surface (the competitor's boundary dC), and the
  original connected closed embedded analytic surface dE. They share
  an open patch. Two such surfaces agree everywhere. Formally, at
  any point of the patch choose analytic graph coordinates; the two
  graph functions agree on a neighbourhood, so by unique analytic
  continuation they agree on the whole connected surface.

  Therefore dC = dE as point sets in T.

  Step 8 (occupied sides agree). Having dC = dE as surfaces, decide
  whether C = E or C = complement of E. On the shared patch, the
  outward normal of C and the outward normal of E face the SAME
  side. Since dE is connected, the relative sign of any two normal
  fields is constant on dE. So the normals agree as unit vector
  fields on all of dE. Therefore

        C_a^+  =  E    and    C_a^-  =  E.

  Step 9 (E is reflection-symmetric in x1 = a). Equality C_a^+ = E
  says E is the reflection of E  intersect  H_a across the two faces of H_a.
  Since both faces lie on the same torus in T, this is the statement
  that E is invariant under reflection in the torus x1 = a.

  Translate the coordinate origin so a = 0. Then E is invariant
  under x1 -> -x1.

  Step 10 (the other two coordinates). Repeat with x2 and x3. The
  bisecting positions b, c for those coordinates can be similarly
  translated to 0, and the successive translations preserve the
  previously obtained symmetries because they act in distinct
  coordinates and hence commute. Done. [ ]

  Consequences of reflection symmetry.

  (a) E is a symmetric region inside the cube Q = [0,1]^3. The
  fractional volume and boundary area in T equal the ordinary volume
  and relative perimeter inside Q. This closure means that from now
  on, slicing E entirely takes place inside Q.

  (b) The boundary Sigma meets each face of Q orthogonally. Reason:
  equivariance of the unit normal. If E is symmetric under
  x_i -> -x_i, its outward normal N_i satisfies N_i(-x_i) = -N_i(x_i)
  (from the Chain rule applied to the reflection). At the fixed face
  x_i = 0, this gives N_i(0) = -N_i(0) = 0. So N_i vanishes on
  each face.

  (c) The "occupied side" choices are consistent across reflected
  copies: E  intersect  Q determines E entirely.

These structural facts will be used repeatedly.

A note for the reader. The argument in Steps 1-9 uses one deep input
from analytic PDE (Blatt 2024): every minimising boundary is
real-analytic. If you are willing to take that as known, everything
else is elementary.




Reflection symmetry placed E nicely in the cube. Now one wants more:
E should look "downhill" in each coordinate direction. Concretely,
one wants the surface to face outward in a definite direction of each
coordinate. This is the monotonicity result that unlocks slicing.

  Setup. E is a nonstandard minimiser at some fractional volume
  V in (0, 1/2], reflection-symmetric as in the symmetry discussion. Sigma = dE,
  connected embedded analytic genus g >= 2, h = k1 + k2 the constant
  mean curvature. N = (N_1, N_2, N_3) = outward unit normal to Sigma.

  The object of study here is u_i := N_i, one coordinate
  component of the normal.

  Lemma (strict monotonicity).
  After possibly reversing some coordinates, for each i = 1, 2, 3:

        N_i(x)  >  0    for every x in Sigma with 0 < x_i < 1.

  Consequence (down-set property): if p in E and q <= p in each
  coordinate, then q in E. In words, E is a "down-set" of Q under
  the natural coordinate ordering.

  Proof of Lemma 8.2.

  The plan is: (a) show u_i is a Jacobi field, hence satisfies a
  nice elliptic equation; (b) show u_i has too few zeros to exceed
  two nodal domains on Sigma, thanks to stability; (c) use
  reflection-symmetry to fix the sign on each half-torus.

  (a) u_i is a Jacobi field. Consider the 1-parameter family of
  regions E_t obtained by translating E along the x_i axis by t.
  Translation is an isometry of T, so E_t is automatically a
  minimiser of the same isoperimetric problem. Its boundary Sigma_t
  has the same constant mean curvature h. Differentiating this
  family in t, the velocity vector field on Sigma is the projection
  of e_i, whose normal component is exactly N_i = u_i. Standard
  theory of CMC deformations (the Jacobi equation) gives

        Delta_Sigma u_i + |II|^2 u_i  =  0,       (JACOBI)

  where Delta_Sigma is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on Sigma, and
  |II| is the norm of the second fundamental form of Sigma.

  (b) Reflection parity. Since E is invariant under x_i -> -x_i
  (Lemma 7.2), the normal field transforms as u_i(-x_i) = -u_i(x_i).
  At the fixed faces x_i = 0 and x_i = 1, this forces u_i = 0.
  So u_i vanishes on the two coordinate tori x_i = 0, 1, which
  split Sigma into two halves

        Sigma_+  =  Sigma  intersect  {0 < x_i < 1},
        Sigma_-  =  Sigma  intersect  {1 < x_i < 2}.

  u_i has one sign near x_i = 0^+ and opposite near x_i = 1^- (by
  oddness), and vice versa on Sigma_-.

  (c) Nodal domain lemma. A nodal domain of u_i is a connected
  open piece of Sigma ∖ u_i^{-1}(0). The claim one needs:

        Nodal domain lemma (Jacobi).
        A nonzero Jacobi field u on a minimising boundary of
        genus >= 2 has at most 2 nodal domains.

  Proof of the lemma. Let Q(f, f) be the stability quadratic form:

        Q(f, f)  =  integral over Sigma of (|grad f|^2 - |II|^2 f^2).

  For a minimising boundary, stability is equivalent to

        Q(f, f)  >=  0   for every smooth f with mean zero.

  Now use the Jacobi equation (JACOBI). For any function g
  supported on a nodal domain D and equal to u|_D there (and zero
  elsewhere), an integration by parts gives

        Q(g, g) = integral_D g (-Lu) = 0,

  because Lu = 0 on D. Similarly, cross terms Q(g_1, g_2) between
  two such disjointly-supported functions vanish identically.

  Suppose for contradiction that u has at least 3 nodal domains
  D_1, D_2, D_3. Define the test function

        f  =  a * u|_{D_1} + b * u|_{D_2}

  with u extended by zero outside each D_j. One can choose the
  real numbers (a, b) not both zero so that the mean of f vanishes:
  this is one linear equation in two unknowns, and D_1, D_2 have
  nonzero area. Then by the previous paragraph:

        Q(f, f)  =  a^2 Q(u_{D_1}, u_{D_1}) + b^2 Q(u_{D_2}, u_{D_2}) + 0  =  0.

  Combined with stability Q >= 0, this says Q(f, f) = 0 and f is
  a constrained minimiser of Q over mean-zero functions. The
  Lagrange multiplier for the mean constraint gives an equation

        L f  =  c      (a constant)

  where L = Delta_Sigma + |II|^2. On the third domain D_3, both f
  and its derivatives vanish (f is supported on D_1  union  D_2). So
  L f = 0 on D_3, forcing c = 0. Then L f = 0 on all of Sigma.
  Elliptic regularity bootstraps f to a smooth solution of
  L f = 0. The identity theorem then forces f to be identically
  zero, contradicting f nonzero on D_1. So the assumption of
  >= 3 nodal domains fails: at most 2 nodal domains. [ ]

  (d) Finish of Lemma 8.2. The two fixed faces x_i = 0 and
  x_i = 1 are zero sets of u_i, so they belong to the nodal set.
  The two halves Sigma_+ and Sigma_- are each connected (by the
  same structure-theorem argument) and neither meets the nodal
  set of u_i except along its boundary. If u_i had an interior
  zero in Sigma_+ (or Sigma_-), that half would split into two
  nodal domains, giving at least 3 total. Contradiction with (c).
  So u_i has no interior zero in either half, and is one-signed
  on each half.

  Reverse the x_i coordinate direction if necessary to make u_i
  positive on Sigma_+. Then u_i > 0 throughout Sigma_+ and, by
  oddness, u_i < 0 on Sigma_- (opposite signs). Since
  u_i > 0 on the "occupied half" of the torus, and "occupied half"
  can be chosen by reversing x_i, this is the claim.

  (e) Down-set. Now u_i = N_i > 0 on the occupied-half interior.
  Consider a coordinate curve in the x_i direction. It hits Sigma
  transversely (since N_i != 0 everywhere it hits in the interior),
  and the outward normal points in the positive x_i direction.
  Thus crossing the coordinate curve from below to above moves
  from E to the complement of E. The curve crosses Sigma at most
  once. Following the curve from its lowest coordinate, E occupies
  the initial segment and its complement occupies the rest.

  For all three coordinates simultaneously: if p in E and q <= p
  in each coordinate, then the coordinatewise segment from q to p
  lies inside E, because it starts before any crossing in each
  coordinate. So q in E. Hence E  intersect  Q is a down-set for the
  coordinatewise order. [ ]

  Why this helps. Combined with the reflection symmetry, the
  down-set property says that E in the cube Q is fully determined
  by which vertices of Q it contains, and it looks like a "staircase"
  going down to the front-bottom-left corner (0,0,0) of Q.

  Precisely, the 8 vertices of Q are {0,1}^3. E's closure contains
  some subset V(E) of these vertices. Because E is a down-set:

       if v in V(E) and v' <= v coordinatewise, then v' in V(E).

  So V(E) is an "order ideal" of the cube. This leaves only a small
  number of possibilities, catalogued below.



By the end of the coordinate-monotonicity argument one knows that E is a down-set inside the cube
Q = [0,1]^3. That means: if p in E and q <= p coordinatewise, then
q in E. A short catalog follows of what E can look like at
the eight vertices of Q, and show that only two patterns remain,
up to symmetries.

  No vertex on Sigma.

  Lemma 9.1. No vertex of Q lies on the surface Sigma = dE.

  Proof. At any vertex v in {0,1}^3, all three coordinate reflections
  (x_i -> -x_i) fix v. So the outward normal N at v is also fixed by
  all three reflections. But reflection in x_i sends N_i to -N_i.
  Hence N_i = -N_i for each i, forcing N_1 = N_2 = N_3 = 0. That
  contradicts |N| = 1. [ ]

  Occupied and vacant vertices.

  Because E is nonempty (V > 0) and proper (V < 1), and because it
  is a down-set, the vertex (0,0,0) lies in the closure of E and
  the vertex (1,1,1) does not. Call the set of vertices in the
  closure of E the "occupied vertices" V(E). It is a nonempty proper
  order ideal of the vertex lattice {0,1}^3.

  Order ideal here means: if v in V(E) and v' <= v coordinatewise,
  then v' in V(E). This is the "down-set" property viewed at the
  finite vertex lattice.

  Lemma 9.2. V(E) is a nonempty proper order ideal containing (0,0,0)
  and not (1,1,1).

  Proof. The closure of E contains (0,0,0) since E is nonempty and E
  contains a neighbourhood of (0,0,0) (fractional volume at the corner
  (0,0,0) is positive). It does not contain (1,1,1) since E has
  fractional volume < 1, so a small neighbourhood of (1,1,1) is in
  the complement. The down-set property of E passes to the closure.
  [ ]

  Reduce to at most four occupied vertices.

  Complementation E' = T ∖ E is again a minimiser (by symmetry of
  the isoperimetric problem under complements). Reversing all
  coordinates x_i -> 1 - x_i carries the "down-set" representation
  of E' back to a valid down-set representation of a minimiser. The
  combined operation

        (complement, then reverse all coordinates)

  replaces V(E) by the image of the complementary set under
  x -> (1,1,1) - x. The result is again an order ideal containing
  (0,0,0) and not (1,1,1), and its size is 8 - |V(E)|.

  Since 4 is the smallest allowed size still >= 4, the representative with the smaller size is chosen to be the working case:
  assume |V(E)| <= 4.

  The 1-vertex pattern is a ball.

  Suppose V(E) = {(0,0,0)}. Then E is confined to a small cube
  around (0,0,0), separated from the opposite three faces of Q.
  Reflection symmetry of E lifts it to an 8-fold-symmetric region
  in the box [0,2]^3, and then to a star-shaped region in R^3 by
  further lattice reflections. In R^3, every radial ray from the
  origin starts inside the lifted region and crosses the boundary
  exactly once (because N_i > 0 on the boundary, all three
  coordinates increase monotonically with radius). So the boundary
  is a sphere and E is a ball. Standard, not nonstandard.

  The 2-vertex pattern is a tube.

  Suppose V(E) = {(0,0,0), (1,0,0)}. The two vertices form an edge
  of Q in the x1 direction. The whole edge is occupied together with
  a neighbourhood. The opposite two faces x2 = 1 and x3 = 1 are
  strictly vacant (otherwise monotonicity would push extra vertices
  into V(E)).

  In the cover R x S^2 = the cylinder over the edge, every ray from
  the edge in the transverse 2-plane starts inside and crosses the
  boundary exactly once. So the boundary of E in that cover is a
  radial graph over the torus, hence a circular tube. Standard, not
  nonstandard.

  The face-4-vertex pattern is a slab.

  Suppose V(E) contains the four vertices of a face, say z = 0:
  {(0,0,0), (1,0,0), (0,1,0), (1,1,0)}. Then the face is strictly
  occupied and the opposite face z = 1 is strictly vacant. By the
  strict normal positivity, in the z direction E is a graph
  between these two faces.

  If E is nonstandard with boundary Sigma connected, then Sigma is
  a single connected surface. But a graph between two opposite
  faces is a SINGLE surface, and reflection in the faces produces
  TWO disjoint graphs (one entering each face). Since Sigma is
  connected, this is a contradiction: so this configuration cannot
  be nonstandard.

  If E is standard, this is just the slab (a union of two parallel
  planar tori). Fine, but the slab is already one of the standard
  minimisers.

  The remaining two patterns.

  At this point, only these possibilities remain up to coordinate
  permutation:

        |V(E)| = 3, specific pattern  {000, 100, 010},
        |V(E)| = 4, non-face pattern   {000, 100, 010, 001}.

  Turning these into a general statement:

  Proposition 9.7 (vertex patterns).
  No vertex of Q lies on Sigma. Up to taking the complementary
  region and reversing all coordinates, and up to coordinate
  permutations, the only possible occupied-vertex sets for a
  NONSTANDARD minimiser E are:

        W_3  =  {000, 100, 010}         (three vertices, L-corner),
        W_4  =  {000, 100, 010, 001}    (four vertices, "chair").

  Proof. Combine 9.1-9.6. The ball is 1-vertex standard; the tube
  is 2-vertex standard; the slab is 4-vertex face standard; all
  other order ideals reduce to these or to W_3, W_4 under
  complement and reversal. [ ]

  Note. For the four-vertex pattern W_4, complement followed by
  x -> (1,1,1) - x maps W_4 to itself. So the occupied side is the smaller-volume side (V <= 1/2). For the three-vertex
  pattern W_3, no such symmetry exists: it appears below (in
  the V1 exclusion) that the flux identity forces the pattern to label the
  smaller-volume side too, because otherwise the isoperimetric
  profile would have the wrong concavity.

  What remains to be done.

  The rest of the proof is a case analysis on W_3 and W_4 at the
  two transition volumes V1 = 4 pi / 81 and V2 = 1/pi. The slice toolkit
  and the four-vertex exclusion handle the four-vertex pattern W_4 at both V1 and V2.
  The V1 exclusion handles W_3 at V1; the V2 analysis, nesting estimate, numerical certification, and final assembly handle W_3 at V2, which
  is the deepest case and needs all the machinery of slicing and
  nesting.



Everything from here on relies on slicing E into horizontal planar
pieces and studying how their perimeters and areas change with the
height. This is where the down-set and monotonicity come into full
force.

  Setting. Fix an "axial" direction, say z, that plays the role
  of a coordinate axis of Q. For each height z in [0,1], slice E:

        E_z  =  { (x, y) in [0,1]^2 : (x, y, z) in E } .

  This is a planar region inside the unit square, understood in the
  2-torus sense (opposite edges identified). Its properties:

        v(z)  =  area of E_z,     (a number in [0,1])
        p(z)  =  perimeter of E_z (in the 2-torus).

  Because E is a down-set in z, the function v is nonincreasing.
  Its endpoint values are

        s  =  v(0),     r  =  v(1),     with 0 <= r <= s <= 1.

  The occupied planar curve = the boundary of E_z inside the square.
  Denote it by dE_z. It is a finite disjoint union of smooth closed
  curves for all but finitely many exceptional z.

  Coarea Jacobian and b. Let Sigma = dE, and let N = (N_x, N_y,
  N_z) be its outward unit normal. Define

        alpha  =  |nabla_Sigma z|  =  sqrt(1 - N_z^2).

  Here nabla_Sigma is the tangential gradient. The number alpha is
  the "coarea Jacobian" of the height function z restricted to
  Sigma; it measures how the surface stretches as the surface is pushed along z.

  Define

        b  =  (1 / A) * integral over Sigma of (1 - N_z^2) dS.

  This is the mean of 1 - N_z^2 over the surface, normalized by
  fractional area A. Note that 0 < b < 1 because Sigma is not flat
  in x,y (N_x, N_y not identically 0) -- see 10.6.

  Finitely many exceptional levels. A height z is exceptional
  if E_z has a non-smooth boundary. This happens exactly when the
  "critical surface": a point of Sigma at which N = e_z (or N = -e_z),
  sits at that height.

  By the strict monotonicity result (coordinate monotonicity), N_z > 0 throughout the
  interior of Q. So N_z = 1 would require N_x = N_y = 0 too. That
  forces the projection (x, y) to land on a corner of the square:
  x, y in {0, 1}. Vertices of Q do not lie on Sigma (the vertex-pattern classification). So
  the critical points are isolated and lie on the edges of Q. Their
  number is finite by compactness. Hence only FINITELY many
  exceptional heights.

  The function v. On each open interval between exceptional
  heights, the slice boundary dE_z moves smoothly with z, and v is
  smooth. At exceptional heights, v remains continuous but may have
  a corner. Across the whole interval [0,1], the upshot is:

        v is continuous, nonincreasing, and absolutely continuous;

        v is smooth except on finitely many heights.

  Consequence (substitution lemma). For any nonnegative measurable
  function gamma on [0,1],

        integral_0^1 gamma(v(z)) * (-v'(z)) dz  =  integral_r^s gamma(w) dw.

  This is a change of variable for the monotone function v, and it
  lets later Parts integrate purely in "slice-area space" rather
  than "height space". It is used below in the exclusion argument.

  The flux identity. Here is the workhorse lemma. For every
  nonempty regular level z in (0,1),

        integral_{dE_z} alpha dl   =   b A  +  h (v(z) - V).       (FLUX)

  At the two end levels z = 0 and z = 1, where N_z = 0 and hence
  alpha = 1, this becomes the sharper pair

        p(0)  =  b A + h (s - V),                                    (FLUX-0)
        p(1)  =  b A + h (r - V).                                    (FLUX-1)

  Here V = fractional volume of E, and h = constant mean curvature
  of Sigma (sum of principal curvatures, the convention).

  Why (FLUX) holds. Take a smooth test function eta(z), and apply
  the first variation of the surface area under the vector field
  X = eta(z) e_z. On one hand, the surface area change comes from
  the tangential divergence of X along Sigma. On a CMC surface of
  mean curvature h, that divergence can be rewritten as h times the
  change in enclosed volume. On the other hand, the enclosed volume
  changes by the derivative eta'(z) times the slice area v(z), via
  the "horizontal slice" picture. Equating the two, for all smooth
  eta, gives

        integral_{Sigma  intersect  Q} eta'(z) (1 - N_z^2) dS
            =  h * integral_{Sigma  intersect  Q} eta(z) N_z dS

  which after another divergence computation and coarea on the
  slice gives the identity (FLUX). The endpoint cases follow because
  N_z = 0 on the faces z = 0 and z = 1 (the symmetry discussion and orthogonality).

  The projection identity. Take the constant vector field X = e_z
  and integrate div(X) = 0 over E  intersect  Q. Divegence Theorem:

        0  =  integral_{partial(E  intersect  Q)} e_z * n dS.

  The boundary decomposes into the "interior" part Sigma  intersect  Q and the
  "cube-face" part E  intersect  (partial Q). Evaluating the cube-face terms
  (only z = 0 and z = 1 contribute, with opposite signs s and -r)
  gives

        r - s  =  integral_{Sigma  intersect  Q} N_z dS.                    (PROJ)

  Now apply Cauchy-Schwarz. Since integral_{Sigma} N_z^2 dS = A(1-b)
  (by definition of b), one gets

        | integral_{Sigma} N_z dS |  <=  A sqrt(1 - b).

  Combining with (PROJ) and noting that the left side is s - r >= 0:

        s - r  <=  A sqrt(1 - b).                                 (PROJ-BOUND)

  The coarea identity. Standard coarea formula on the surface
  Sigma:

        A  =  integral_0^1 ( integral_{dE_z} (1 / alpha) dl ) dz.  (COAREA)

  This says the total area of Sigma is the integral over all heights
  of (perimeter of slice) divided by (coarea Jacobian).

  The three coordinates sum. Write

        b_x  =  (1/A) * integral_{Sigma} (1 - N_x^2) dS,
        b_y  =  (1/A) * integral_{Sigma} (1 - N_y^2) dS,
        b_z  =  b   =  (1/A) * integral_{Sigma} (1 - N_z^2) dS.

  Since N_x^2 + N_y^2 + N_z^2 = 1 at every point, one has

        (1-N_x^2) + (1-N_y^2) + (1-N_z^2)  =  2      pointwise.

  Averaging over Sigma:

        b_x + b_y + b_z  =  2.                                  (SUM-B)

  Also, each b_i < 1 strictly: because N_i > 0 on the interior of
  Sigma (coordinate monotonicity), so N_i^2 is not identically 1 on Sigma.

  Summary of tools. The endpoint elimination machinery will draw from this
  toolkit:

        * The flux identity (FLUX) and its endpoint forms (FLUX-0),
          (FLUX-1), for slices along any axis.

        * The projection bound (PROJ-BOUND), and the sum identity
          (SUM-B) across all three axes.

        * The change of variable (SUBST) from slice-area w to height
          z, whenever an integral in w is more convenient.

        * The finiteness of exceptional heights (10.3).

  This is enough to attack both vertex patterns at both transition
  volumes.



The four-vertex pattern is now eliminated W_4 = {000, 100, 010, 001}
at both transition volumes V1 = 4 pi / 81 and V2 = 1/pi. Because W_4
is carried to itself by complement-then-reversal, WLOG the smaller-volume side may be taken and the mean curvature assumed h >= 0.

  Setup. Fix V in {V1, V2}. Suppose (for contradiction) that E
  is a nonstandard minimiser at volume V, with vertex pattern W_4.
  Write A = fractional boundary area, h = mean curvature (sum of
  principal curvatures), and let a = sqrt(pi V).

  Because a standard shape (tube) exists at volume V, and tube
  bound shows A_tube = sqrt(pi V), one has the standard competitor
  bound

        A  <=  a  <=  1.                                 (STD-BOUND)

  The curvature estimate CURV noted at the outset gives

        h^2 A  <=  pi  implies  x := h A  <=  sqrt(pi a)  <=  sqrt(pi).
                                                                 (CURV-B)
  Note x >= 0 by assumption h >= 0.

  Choose the slicing axis. In the slice toolkit,
  one has b_x + b_y + b_z = 2 (SUM-B). At least one of the three
  coordinates satisfies b_i >= 2/3, by the pigeonhole principle.
  Permute coordinates if needed so that b := b_z >= 2/3. Set

        d  :=  sqrt(1 - b).                                 (D-DEF)

  Then b <= 1 (the end of the slice toolkit) gives b < 1 (coordinate monotonicity, strictly), so d > 0.
  Combined with b >= 2/3:

        0  <  d  <=  1 / sqrt(3).                        (D-BOUND)

  The slicing axis is z, and the missing-axis direction is z.

  Planar shape of top and bottom slices. With vertex set W_4
  = {000, 100, 010, 001}, the z axis is the "special" axis along
  which two vertices are occupied (000 and 001) and the other two
  vertices (at x, y not both zero) are vacant.

  Top slice at z=1: only vertex (0,0) is occupied. So E_1 is a
  CORNER region: confined to a small rectangle [0, x_top] x [0, y_top]
  near the origin (0,0) of the 2D square. Let r = v(1) = its area.
  By down-set property and 4-fold reflection across the two axes
  x=0 and y=0, one obtains a full planar set of area 4r and perimeter
  4 * p(1). The 2D isoperimetric inequality gives

        (4 p(1))^2  >=  4 pi (4 r)    implies   p(1)  >=  sqrt(pi r).
                                                         (PLANAR-TOP)

  Bottom slice at z=0: vertices (0,0), (1,0), (0,1) occupied, so
  E_0 is confined to the "L-corner" and its COMPLEMENT is a corner
  at the opposite corner (1,1). The complement has area 1 - s, and
  its perimeter (relative to the square, i.e., counting only the
  curve, not the square boundary) equals p(0). Same 4-fold
  reflection:

        p(0)  >=  sqrt(pi (1 - s)).                  (PLANAR-BOTTOM)

  Monotonicity bounds on the endpoint areas. Because E is a
  down-set in z, one has r <= V. Define w in [0, 1] by

        sqrt(pi r)  =  a * w,     i.e.  r = a^2 w^2 / pi.    (W-DEF)

  Then r <= V = a^2 / pi gives w <= 1 -- consistent.

  The flux endpoint inequalities. From (FLUX-0) and (FLUX-1)
  of the slice toolkit:

        p(1)  =  b A + h (r - V),
        p(0)  =  b A + h (s - V).

  Applying (PLANAR-TOP) to p(1):

        aw  <=  b A + h (r - V)
            =  b A - h (V - r)
            =  b A - h (a^2 / pi) (1 - w^2)        [since r = a^2 w^2 / pi]
            =  b A - h a^2 (1 - w^2) / pi.

  Applying (PLANAR-BOTTOM) to p(0) and using (PROJ-BOUND) s - r
  <= A sqrt(1-b) = A d, together with p(0) = p(1) + h(s - r):

        p(0)  =  p(1) + h (s - r)
             <=  [b A + h (r - V)] + h (A d)             [using s - r <= A d, h >= 0]
             =  b A + h (r - V) + h A d
             =  b A - h a^2 (1 - w^2) / pi + h A d.

  So

        sqrt(pi (1 - s))  <=  b A - h a^2 (1 - w^2) / pi + x d      (FLUX-INEQ-2)

  with x = h A. Finally, from (PROJ-BOUND) s - r <= A d:

        s  <=  r + A d  =  a^2 w^2 / pi + A d.                    (FLUX-INEQ-3)

  Divide and shift to a tau. Divide (FLUX-INEQ-1) by a and use
  A <= a (STD-BOUND) and h a >= x = h A (since A <= a):

        w  <=  (A/a) b  -  (h a / pi) (1 - w^2)
           <=  b - (x/pi) (1 - w^2).

  Reorganize:  w + x (1 - w^2) / pi  <=  b.

  Define f(t) = t + x (1 - t^2) / pi. Then f(w) <= b. On the other
  hand f(1) = 1 > b (since b = 1 - d^2 < 1). So by continuity, there
  is some tau in [w, 1) with f(tau) = b. This tau satisfies

        tau + x (1 - tau^2) / pi  =  b  =  1 - d^2.        (TAU-DEF)

  Solving (TAU-DEF) for x:

        x  =  pi (1 - tau - d^2) / (1 - tau^2).           (X-FROM-TAU)

  In particular tau < 1 - d^2 (so tau is strictly inside (0, 1)).

  The monotone function E. Define

        E(q)  =  b A - h a^2 (1 - q^2) / pi,   for q in [0, 1].

  Since h >= 0 and dE/dq = h a^2 * 2 q / pi >= 0, E is nondecreasing
  on [0, 1].

  Evaluate E(tau) using (TAU-DEF). Start from (TAU-DEF) rearranged:

        b  =  tau + x (1 - tau^2) / pi.

  Multiply by A: A b = A tau + (A x) (1 - tau^2) / pi = A tau + h A^2 (1 - tau^2) / pi.
  Subtract h a^2 (1 - tau^2) / pi:

        E(tau)  =  A b - h a^2 (1 - tau^2) / pi
                =  A tau + h (A^2 - a^2) (1 - tau^2) / pi.

  Since A <= a, the second term is <= 0. So E(tau) <= A tau <= a tau
  (using A <= a from STD-BOUND).

  By monotonicity E(q) is nondecreasing in q, and w <= tau, so:

        E(w)  <=  E(tau)  <=  a tau.                    (E-CHAIN)

  Combine. From (FLUX-INEQ-1) and the definition of E:
        aw  <=  E(w).

  From (FLUX-INEQ-2):
        sqrt(pi (1 - s))  <=  E(w) + x d  <=  a tau + x d  [by E-CHAIN].

  Squaring:
        pi (1 - s)  <=  (a tau + x d)^2.                (SQ-1)

  From (FLUX-INEQ-3) and s <= r + A d, with r = a^2 w^2 / pi <= a^2 tau^2 / pi:

        1 - s  >=  1 - a^2 tau^2 / pi - A d  >=  1 - tau^2 / pi - d
                                                                  (SQ-2)
  where the last step uses a <= 1 and A <= 1.

  Combine (SQ-1) and (SQ-2), multiplying the latter by pi:

        pi (1 - tau^2 / pi - d)   <=   pi (1 - s)   <=   (a tau + x d)^2.
        pi - tau^2 - pi d        <=   (a tau + x d)^2  <=  (tau + x d)^2
                                                                 [using a <= 1]

  Divide by pi and rearrange:

        pi (1 - d)  <=  tau^2 + (tau + x d)^2.

  Rearrange further (divide by pi, add d):

        1  <=  d + [tau^2 + (tau + x d)^2] / pi   =:  R(tau, d).   (R-INEQ)

  The small scalar lemma. The inequality (R-INEQ) is a purely
  one-variable inequality in the pair (tau, d), subject to the
  arithmetic constraints from above:

        0 < d <= 1/sqrt(3),    0 <= x <= sqrt(pi),   0 <= tau < 1,
        tau + x (1 - tau^2) / pi = 1 - d^2.

  This is precisely the hypothesis of the scalar lemma in
  the four-vertex scalar lemma. Its conclusion is

        R(tau, d)  <  1,

  which contradicts (R-INEQ). Therefore the four-vertex pattern W_4
  cannot occur at any minimiser volume V -- in particular, not at
  V1 or V2.

Now for the deep case: the three-vertex pattern W_3 at the tube-to-slab
transition volume V2 = 1/pi. This is the only case in which the
curvature can lie in a "central" range where the planar isoperimetric
inequality alone is not enough. The structure of the proof here:

  The V2 analysis puts everything into a small box of parameters (h, s).
  The nesting estimate uses a "nesting" argument to get a strict lower bound on A.
  the numerical certification and final assembly certify the resulting scalar inequality numerically
               and assemble the proof.

The present Part is pure setup. Nothing subtle happens; the
parameters just have to be trapped in a small enough range.

  Setup. Suppose (for contradiction) a nonstandard minimiser
  E at volume V2 = 1/pi has vertex pattern W_3 = {000, 100, 010}.
  Slicing along the missing axis z, as in the V1 exclusion:

        r  =  v(1)  =  0,     p(1)  =  0.

  Flux at the empty top gives, exactly as in the V1 exclusion:

        b A  =  h V2  =  h / pi,                                 (V2-TOP)
        p(0)  =  h s.                                             (V2-P0)

  The bottom slice's complement (corner at (1,1)) again has area
  1-s, and the planar bound gives

        p(0)  >=  sqrt(pi (1 - s)).                             (V2-PLANAR)

  Finally, standard competitor at V2 = slab (area 1) gives

        A  <=  1.                                                  (V2-SLAB)

  Since the tube baseline gives A <= sqrt(pi V2) = 1 only at V2,
  the slab bound A <= 1 is the sharp one here.

  Two-sided bound on h.

  Lower bound on h. From (V2-PLANAR) and p(0) = h s:

        h s  >=  sqrt(pi (1 - s))         implies    h^2 s^2 + pi s - pi >= 0.

  Quadratic in s: on s >= 0, the left side is strictly increasing
  (as a function of s) because 2 h^2 s + pi > 0. So s >= t(h), where
  t(h) is the unique positive root of h^2 s^2 + pi s - pi = 0:

        t(h)  =  [-pi + sqrt(pi^2 + 4 h^2 pi)] / (2 h^2)
              =  2 / [1 + sqrt(1 + 4 h^2 / pi)].          (T-DEF)

  The second form follows by rationalizing the first. So

        s  >=  t(h).                                        (S-LOWER-T)

  Upper bound on h. From the projection bound (PROJ-BOUND):

        s^2  <=  A^2 (1 - b)  =  A^2 - A^2 b  =  A^2 - A h / pi.
                                                                  (V2-PROJ)

  Using A <= 1 and the fact that the right side is positive (otherwise
  s^2 <= A h / pi - A^2 contradiction):

        1 - h/pi  >=  0,      h  <=  pi.
    And  s^2  <=  A(1 - h/pi)  <=  1 - h/pi,
    so   h  <=  h_max := pi (1 - s^2).                        (H-UPPER)

  Reduce to one inequality in s.

  Combine s >= t(h) and h >= 0 with the two-sided h bound. Since t
  is decreasing in h (larger h means smaller t), the inequality is
  best attacked via eliminating h. From h >= sqrt(pi(1-s))/s (the
  raw lower bound), and the upper bound h <= pi(1-s^2):

        sqrt(pi (1 - s)) / s  <=  pi (1 - s^2).

  Square both sides (all quantities nonnegative for s in (0,1)):

        pi (1 - s) / s^2  <=  pi^2 (1 - s^2)^2.

  Cancel pi > 0 and (1 - s) > 0 (writing (1 - s^2)^2 = (1 - s)^2 (1+s)^2):

        1 / s^2  <=  pi (1 - s) (1 + s)^2.

  Multiply through by s^2:

        Phi(s)  :=  s^2 (1 - s) (1 + s)^2   >=   1 / pi.    (PHI)

  Where does PHI(s) = 1/pi place s?

  Unimodality of Phi. Differentiate:

        Phi'(s)  =  d/ds [ s^2 (1-s) (1+s)^2 ]
                 =  s (1 + s) (2 + s - 5 s^2).

  Factor: Phi'(s) has a simple zero at s=0, at s=-1, and at the
  roots of 5 s^2 - s - 2 = 0:

        s  =  (1 +/- sqrt(41)) / 10.

  The relevant root is

        s_*  =  (1 + sqrt(41)) / 10  ~=  0.7403.

  So Phi increases on (0, s_*) and decreases after s_*.

  Two explicit values. Evaluate Phi at two rational points,
  one on each side of s_*:

        Phi(107/200)  =  (107/200)^2 (93/200) (307/200)^2

        Value  ~=  0.2957

        Phi(89/100)  =  (89/100)^2 (11/100) (189/100)^2

        Value  ~=  0.3076

  Both are below 1/pi ~= 0.3183. But the desired inequality is
  Phi(s) >= 1/pi, which FAILS near s_*. So s must be on the
  boundary, out of the peak's immediate neighbourhood.

  Since Phi is unimodal with peak at s_*, the set {Phi >= 1/pi}
  contains the peak values but its lower tails do not. The
  candidate roots of Phi(s) = 1/pi bracket the peak. Two facts:

        Phi(107/200)  <  1/pi,
        Phi(89/100)   <  1/pi.

  So the true roots of Phi = 1/pi are strictly between 107/200 and
  s_* and strictly between s_* and 89/100. In particular:

        107/200  <  s_low  <  s_*  <  s_high  <  89/100.

  Because Phi is >= 1/pi on the entire interval [s_low, s_high]
  and < 1/pi outside, the inequality (PHI) implies

        s_low  <=  s  <=  s_high,     and thus
        107/200  <  s  <  89/100                                (S-RANGE)
  strictly.

  (The strictness uses the strict inequalities Phi(107/200) < 1/pi
  and Phi(89/100) < 1/pi, plus Phi's unimodality.)

  Bound h. With s in (107/200, 89/100) = (0.535, 0.89):

  Lower bound on h. h >= sqrt(pi(1-s))/s. The function
  s -> sqrt(pi(1-s))/s is strictly decreasing on (0,1). So:

        h  >  sqrt(pi (1 - 89/100)) / (89/100)
           =  (100/89) sqrt(0.11 pi)
           >  (100/89) sqrt(0.33)        [using pi > 3]
           =  sqrt(3300 / 7921)
           >  16 / 25  =  0.64.                             (H-LO)

  The computation used (100/89)^2 * 0.33 = 3300/7921 > (16/25)^2, which is
  checked by clearing denominators: 6448 > 6241. Writing
  3300/7921 - 256/625 = (3300*625 - 256*7921)/(7921*625) and
  computing 3300*625 = 2,062,500 and 256*7921 = 2,027,776 gives a
  positive difference 34,724 > 0, which confirms (H-LO).

  Upper bound on h. h <= pi(1-s^2). The right side is decreasing in
  s. So:

        h  <  pi (1 - (107/200)^2)  =  pi (1 - 11449/40000)
           =  pi * 28551/40000
           <  (22/7) * 28551 / 40000     [using pi < 22/7]
           =  628122 / 280000
           =  314061 / 140000
           <  9/4  =  2.25.                               (H-HI)

  Hence

        h  is  in  (16/25, 9/4)   subset   [0.64, 2.25].          (H-RANGE)

  Note: for open intervals the bounds could also be written more
  compactly as h > 0.6455 and h < 2.243.

  Summary of the V2 parameter box.

  Proposition 13.6 (V2 parameters). Suppose a nonstandard minimiser
  at V2 = 1/pi has the three-vertex pattern W_3. Then A <= 1, the
  flux identities (V2-TOP) hold (i.e, b A = h/pi and p(0) = h s),
  and

        0.535  <  s  <  0.89,
        0.64   <=  h  <=  2.25,
        s  >=  t(h)  =  2 / (1 + sqrt(1 + 4 h^2/pi)).

  Proof. Combine 13.1-13.5. [ ]

  Why these parameters. The box is small enough that the hard
  case can only lie in a limited range. Specifically:

        h in [0.64, 1.02]:  covered by a "baseline" estimate
                            (the nesting estimate), the only part that uses no
                            nesting.
        h in [1.02, 1.62]:  the hard middle range, where nesting
                            yields a "gain".
        h in [1.62, 2.25]:  another baseline regime.

  The nesting estimate gives a lower bound on A in terms of these
  parameters, and the numerical certification establishes that the bound
  always exceeds the value required for a contradiction. The
  details follow.



This is the technical heart of the proof. The situation is that of
The V2 analysis begins with a putative nonstandard minimiser E at V2 = 1/pi with
vertex pattern W_3 = {000, 100, 010}, and unknown parameters

        h in [0.64, 2.25],   s in (0.535, 0.89),   s >= t(h).

Already A <= 1 (V2-SLAB). A lower bound is produced:
A - 1 >= (something positive), which contradicts A <= 1.

The bound has two pieces. First a "baseline" B(h), computed only
from the planar isoperimetric inequality and the flux identity.
Then a "gain" S(h), which uses the additional fact that the actual
slices of E are NESTED inside the bottom slice E_0, and that
nesting restricts how large the strip slices can be. The two pieces
together are positive for every h in [0.64, 2.25]. The exact
calculation of positivity is certified numerically by the numerical certification.

  Setup and the profile g. Fix a constant

        lambda  =  0.98.

  For X in [0, 1), define

        g(X)  =  (1 - lambda X^2) / sqrt(1 - X^2).              (G-FUNC)

  Direct differentiation gives

        g'(X)  =  X (lambda X^2 - (2 lambda - 1)) / (1 - X^2)^{3/2}.

  With lambda = 0.98, the bracket is 0.98 X^2 - 0.96. So g' < 0 for
  X < sqrt(0.96 / 0.98) = sqrt(48 / 49) = sqrt(48)/7 =: D. So g
  decreases up to D and increases after. Set

        ell  =  g(D)  =  (1 - 0.98 * 48/49) / sqrt(1 - 48/49)
                      =  (1 - 48/50) / (1/7)  =  (1/25) * 7  = 0.28.
                                                                  (ELL)
  Then define the NON-INCREASING CAPPED MINORANT

        G(X)  =  g( min(X, D) ),     for X >= 0.                (G-CAP)

  The point of capping at D is that G is non-increasing across all
  of [0, inf): because g decreases up to D and G(t) = g(D) = ell for
  t >= D. So G is non-increasing, takes values in [ell, g(0)] = [ell, 1],
  and satisfies G(X) <= g(X) for X in [0, 1).

  Integral flux inequality.

  Lemma 14.2 (integrated flux).
  At every nonempty regular slice with area v and perimeter p:

        integral_{dE_z} d ell / alpha  -  lambda h v
             >=  G(hv/p) * integral_{dE_z} sqrt(1 - alpha^2) / alpha d ell.
                                                                  (JENSEN-INT)

  If a nonnegative measurable function gamma satisfies
  G(h v(z) / p(z)) >= gamma(v(z)) at all regular levels z, then

        A  >=  lambda h / pi  +  integral_0^s gamma(w) dw.         (AREA-INT)

  Proof of (JENSEN-INT). Normalize arclength: dmu = d ell / p is a
  probability measure on dE_z. Its pushforward to the arc gives
  mean of alpha:

        E[alpha]  =  (integral alpha d ell) / p  =  hv/p   (by FLUX).

  Define f_J(X) = 1/X - lambda X - J sqrt(1 - X^2)/X for X in (0,1].
  A direct computation shows the second derivative is

        X^3 f_J''(X)  =  2  -  J (2 - 3 X^2) / (1 - X^2)^{3/2}.

  The maximum of (2 - 3 X^2)/(1 - X^2)^{3/2} on [0, 1) is 2 (at X=0,
  decreasing to 0 at X = sqrt(2/3), then negative). So the second
  derivative is >= 0 whenever 0 <= J <= 1. Convexity of f_J for
  J in [0,1]. In particular, taking J = G(hv/p) (which is in [ell,1]
  by (ELL)), Jensen gives

        f_{G(m)}(m)  <=  E[ f_{G(m)}(alpha) ],     m = hv/p.

  When m in (0, 1):

        f_{G(m)}(m)  =  (1/m) (1 - lambda m^2 - G(m) sqrt(1 - m^2))
                     =  ( sqrt(1 - m^2) / m ) ( g(m) - G(m) )
                     >=  0     [since G <= g],

  and when m = 1, f_{G(m)}(1) = 1 - lambda = 0.02 > 0. So E[f_{G(m)}(alpha)] >= 0,
  which upon rewriting with dmu = d ell/p gives (JENSEN-INT). [ ]

  Proof of (AREA-INT). Using (JENSEN-INT) replaced by the
  lower bound gamma(v) of G(hv/p), and using the coarea identity
  -v'(z) = integral (sqrt(1-alpha^2)/alpha) d ell (valid because N_z > 0
  gives sqrt(1-alpha^2) = N_z), one has

        integral_{dE_z} d ell/alpha - lambda h v(z)
             >=  gamma(v(z)) * (-v'(z)).

  Integrate over z in [0,1]:

        A  -  lambda h * V2   >=  integral_0^1 gamma(v(z)) (-v'(z)) dz
                              =  integral_0^s gamma(w) dw    [by SUBST].

  Since V2 = 1/pi, this proves (AREA-INT). [ ]

  The planar baseline.

  Lemma 14.3 (planar p_*).
  Every nonempty regular slice satisfies

        p  >=  p_*(v)  :=  min { sqrt(pi v),  1,  sqrt(pi (1 - v)) }.
                                                                  (PLANAR-P-STAR)

  Proof. A nonempty regular slice E_z can be in one of three shapes:
  (i) an occupied corner at (0,0); (ii) a two-vertex strip across
  the bottom edge; (iii) the complement of a corner at (1,1).

  (i) Occupied corner: 4-fold reflection gives a full planar set of
  area 4v and perimeter 4p, so (4p)^2 >= 4 pi (4v), i.e., p^2 >= pi v.
  (iii) Complement of corner: same argument on the complement,
  p^2 >= pi(1-v).
  (ii) Two-vertex strip: after swapping coordinates, it is a single
  decreasing graph y = f(x) for x in [0,1]. Its length is at least
  1 = sqrt(1) + 0 because the graph crosses the whole square
  horizontally. So p >= 1. [ ]

  Because G is non-increasing, PLANAR-P-STAR implies
  G(hv/p) >= G(hv/p_*(v)). Applying (AREA-INT) with
  gamma(v) = G(hv/p_*(v)) gives

        A  >=  lambda h / pi  +  integral_0^s G(hv / p_*(v)) dv.

  Now use s >= t (from S-LOWER-T) and G >= ell, splitting the
  integral:

        A - 1  >=  B(h)  +  ell (s - t),                      (BASELINE-SLACK)

  where

        B(h)  =  lambda h / pi  +  integral_0^{t(h)} G(hv/p_*(v)) dv - 1.
                                                                  (B-DEF)
  The point 0 is handled by the limit, since p_*(v) -> 0 as v -> 0.

  The numerical certification will show that B(h) > 0 outside [1.02, 1.62], and that
  B(h) alone is insufficient in the middle. The slack ell(s-t) and
  the "gain" below cover the middle.

  Bottom calibration.

  Consider the bottom slice E_0, an L-corner. Its complement is a
  corner at (1,1), of area 1-s, with side extents L_x and L_y
  (measured from (1,1) along the axes). Extents are positive since
  the complement has positive area; they may differ.

  Lemma 14.4 (extent bound).
  For each L in {L_x, L_y}:

        h s  >=  (1 - s) / L  +  pi L / 4.                     (EXTENT)

  Proof. Put coordinates (xi, eta) measured from the corner (1,1),
  so xi increases towards the far side, eta decreases from L_y to 0.
  The separating curve between the corner and its complement is a
  monotone decreasing graph eta = f(xi), running from (0, L_y) to
  (L_x, 0). Its length is p(0) = hs.

  Take the vector field (xi/L, -sqrt(1 - xi^2/L^2)) which has unit
  length at every point (for xi in [0, L] and L = L_y, say). Pairing
  it with the tangent (d xi, d eta) and integrating:

        p(0)  =  hs  >=  int (xi/L d xi - sqrt(1 - xi^2/L^2) d eta)
                    =  (1/L) int_0^{L_y} xi d xi
                       + int_0^{L_y} sqrt(1 - eta^2/L^2) d eta
                    =  (1-s)/L + pi L / 4.

  The first integral is 1-s, the area of the L-corner complement.
  The second is the quarter-disk area pi L^2/4, divided by L (per
  unit of L) to give pi L/4. The same argument with xi <-> eta
  proves the bound for L = L_x. [ ]

  Corollary 14.4a (strip endpoint).
  Each extent satisfies L >= (2/pi)(hs - sqrt(h^2 s^2 - pi(1-s))).
  Any strip slice of E, written as y = f(x) or x = f(y), has
  SMALLER ENDPOINT

        e  <=  1 - (2/pi)(h s - sqrt(h^2 s^2 - pi(1-s))).       (E-BOUND-RAW)

  Write s = t + Y^2. Then

        e  <=  1 - 2 h t / pi  +  Q Y,                          (E-BOUND)

  where

        Q  =  (2/pi) sqrt(pi + 2 h^2 t).                        (Q-DEF)

  Proof. The quadratic in L solves to L >= (2/pi)(hs - sqrt(h^2s^2
  - pi(1-s))). A strip y = f(x) is CONTAINED in the bottom slice E_0,
  so f(1) <= 1 - L_y <= 1 - L. For the flipped strip x = f(y), use
  L_x. So one common bound controls both orientations.

  For (E-BOUND), substitute s = t + Y^2 and use h^2 t^2 = pi(1-t)
  (from T-DEF):

        h^2 s^2 - pi(1-s)
            =  pi(1-t) + 2 h^2 t Y^2 + h^2 Y^4 - pi(1 - t - Y^2)
            =  (pi + 2 h^2 t) Y^2 + h^2 Y^4.

  Then sqrt(...) <= sqrt(pi + 2 h^2 t) Y + h Y^2. Substituting in
  (E-BOUND-RAW) cancels the hY^2 term and gives (E-BOUND). [ ]

  The placement lemma.

  Define the key parameters:

        u  :=  min { b_0,  0.96 / h },     b_0 := 1 - 1/pi,
        P  :=  u - 1 + 2 h t / pi,
        d_m := 0.21,    c := 2.7,
        delta  :=  min { d_m,  (P - Q Y)_+ }         where (r)_+ = max(r, 0).
                                                                  (GAIN-PARAMS)

  Here Y^2 = s - t, so delta depends on h and Y.

  Lemma 14.5 (placement).
  For h in [1.02, 1.62] one has

        u - d_m  >=  1033 / 2700  >  0.38  >  a_0 := 1/pi,
        u  <=  b_0,
        u  <  t  <=  s,
        h u  <=  0.96.                                        (PLACEMENT)

  Proof. u >= 0.96 / 1.62 = 16/27. Then u - 0.21 = 16/27 - 0.21 =
  1033/2700 = 0.3826 > 0.38 > 1/pi = 0.3183. Then u <= b_0 by
  definition. And h^2 u^2 <= 0.96^2 < 1 <= pi(1-u) (which follows
  from u <= b_0 and pi(1 - b_0) = 1). The function x -> h^2 x^2 + pi x - pi
  is strictly increasing for x >= 0, and vanishes at t (by T-DEF),
  so h^2 u^2 + pi u - pi < 0 implies u < t. [ ]

  Define the shape function

        H(q)  :=  min { q^2,  pi(1-q) / (c d_m) },   0 <= q <= 1.
                                                                  (H-DEF)

  Lemma 14.5a (perimeter gain).
  Fix h in [1.02, 1.62] and assume delta > 0. For v in
  [u - delta, u], set q = (v - u + delta)/delta. Then every
  regular slice at area level v satisfies

        p^2  >=  1  +  c delta^2 H(q).                          (PERIM-GAIN)

  Proof. In [u-delta, u], one has u-delta >= u-d_m > a_0 and u <= b_0,
  so p_*(v) = 1 (the middle branch). The slice falls into one of
  three types:

  (a) Occupied corner. p^2 >= pi v. Since v > u - d_m > 0.38 and
  pi > 3, p^2 - 1 > pi(0.38) - 1 > 0.14 > c d_m^2 (since
  c d_m^2 = 2.7 * 0.0441 = 0.11907). So p^2 - 1 > c d_m^2 * 1 >= c delta^2 H(q).
  (b) Complement of a corner. p^2 >= pi(1-v). Here v = u - delta + delta q,
  and u <= b_0 = 1 - 1/pi, which gives 1 - u >= 1/pi. Therefore

        1 - v  =  (1 - u) + delta(1 - q)  >=  1/pi + delta(1 - q).

  Multiply by pi and subtract 1:

        pi(1 - v) - 1  >=  pi delta (1 - q).

  Combined with p^2 >= pi(1-v):

        p^2 - 1  >=  pi delta (1 - q).

  Since delta <= d_m (by GAIN-PARAMS), one also has

        pi delta (1 - q)  >=  pi delta^2 (1 - q) / d_m
                          >=  c delta^2 H(q),

  using H(q) <= pi(1-q)/(c d_m). So p^2 - 1 >= c delta^2 H(q) in
  this case too.

  (c) Two-vertex strip. Write the strip as the subgraph y = f(x) of a
  decreasing graph, for x in [0,1]. Its smaller endpoint f(1) satisfies
  f(1) <= e (E-BOUND), and by the definition of delta = min{d_m, (P-QY)_+}
  together with (E-BOUND) one has

        f(1)  <=  u - delta.

  (Checking the two sub-cases: if delta = P - QY > 0 then
  e <= 1 - 2ht/pi + QY = u - P + QY = u - delta. If delta = d_m
  is the cap, then e <= 1 - 2ht/pi + QY <= u - d_m = u - delta.
  If delta = 0 there is nothing to prove.)

  Now do the graph-length calculation. Integration by parts for the
  area under the graph:

        v - f(1)  =  integral_0^1 x |df|  >=  v - (u - delta)
                  =  u - delta + delta q - u + delta
                  =  delta q.

  Set j := delta q and k := 2.5 j. The unit vector
  (sqrt(1 - k^2 x^2), kx) has length 1 at each x in [0,1]. Pair the
  graph tangent (dx, -df) (which is positive, since df < 0) with this
  unit vector and integrate:

        p  =  integral_0^1 |(1, f')| dx
           >=  integral_0^1 [ sqrt(1 - k^2 x^2) + kx |f'| ] dx
           >=  integral_0^1 sqrt(1 - k^2 x^2) dx  +  k (v - f(1))
           >=  1 - k^2/6 - k^4/25 + k j
           >=  1 + 1.35 j^2.

  The third line uses the two-term lower bound sqrt(1 - w) >= 1 - w/2 -
  w^2/5 for 0 <= w <= 0.525^2 (a direct check by clearing denominators),
  and the fourth substitutes k = 2.5 j and collects:

        1 + (5/2) j^2 - (25/24) j^2 - (25/16) j^4  =  1 + (35/24) j^2 - (25/16) j^4.

  Since j <= d_m = 0.21, the coefficient satisfies 35/24 - 25 j^2/16
  >= 1.458 - 0.069 = 1.389 > 1.35. So p >= 1 + 1.35 j^2. Squaring:

        p^2  >=  1 + 2.7 j^2  =  1 + c delta^2 q^2  >=  1 + c delta^2 H(q)

  since H(q) <= q^2. This completes all three cases. [ ]

  Derivative estimate.

  Lemma 14.6.
  For 0 <= X <= 0.96,

        0.48 - 0.49 X^2
        ----------------   >=   0.91 X.                        (DERIV-BOUND)
        (1 - X^2)^{3/2}

  Proof. At X = 0 both sides are nonneg (0.48 >= 0). For X > 0, divide
  both sides by X and rearrange. Set

        N(X)  =  0.49 - 0.01 / (1 - X^2),      D_1(X)  =  X sqrt(1 - X^2).

  Then (DERIV-BOUND) is N(X) / D_1(X) >= 0.91. Since N decreases and
  D_1 = X sqrt(1-X^2) attains its maximum 1/2 at X = 1/sqrt(2) and is
  decreasing for X >= 1/sqrt(2), it suffices to check a finite
  rational table on four subintervals of [0, 0.96]. Full arithmetic
  in the numerical certification. [ ]

  The gain.

  Define moments associated with the shape function H:

        M  :=  integral_0^1 H(q) dq,
        q_m :=  integral_0^1 q H(q) dq / M,
        p_2 :=  1 + c d_m^2 max_{[0,1]} H(q).                  (MOMENTS)

  Lemma 14.7 (moment bounds).

        M  >  0.2659,
        q_m >  0.697,
        p_2 <  1.09.                                           (MOMENT-BOUNDS)

  With

        C_0(h)  :=  min { 0.134 h^3,  0.36 },                  (C0-DEF)

  the following coefficient inequality holds for h in [1.02, 1.62]:

        0.91 c M
        --------  [ h(u - (1 - q_m) d_m) ]^3   >=   C_0(h).     (COEFF-INEQ)
        p_2^{3/2}

  Proof of (MOMENT-BOUNDS) and (COEFF-INEQ). The two branches of H(q)
  meet at

        theta  :=  2 / (1 + sqrt(1 + 4 c d_m / pi)),
        c d_m theta^2  =  pi (1 - theta).

  So H(q) = q^2 for q in [0, theta] and H(q) = pi(1-q)/(c d_m) for
  q in [theta, 1], with max = theta^2. Direct integration gives:

        M  =  theta^2 (3 - theta) / 6,
        q_m =  (2 + 2 theta - theta^2) / (2 (3 - theta)),
        p_2 =  1 + c d_m^2 theta^2.

  The bounds 3.14 < pi < 22/7 alone imply theta in (32/37, 13/15).
  Monotonicity of the three formulas in theta then yields

        M > (32/37)^2 (3 - 13/15)/6  >  0.2659,
        q_m > 0.697,
        p_2 < 1 + (2.7)(0.0441)(13/15)^2  < 1.09.

  For (COEFF-INEQ), the left side is at least
  (0.91 * 2.7 * 0.2659) / 1.09^{3/2} > 0.574, and h(u - (1-q_m) d_m)
  is at least 0.856 whenever u = 0.96/h, at least 0.617*1 = 0.617
  when u = b_0 (and h in [1.02, 1.62]). Cubing and combining gives
  C_0(h). Details are in the numerical certification. [ ]

  The gain, step by step.

  Fix h in [1.02, 1.62] and assume delta > 0. Put

        K  :=  c delta^2 H(q),
        j  :=  delta q.

  Restricted to area levels v in [u - delta, u]:

  Step 1 (from perimeter to G). By (PERIM-GAIN), p >= sqrt(1 + K).
  Because G is non-increasing,

        G(hv/p)  >=  G(hv / sqrt(1 + K)).

  On [u - delta, u], baseline uses p_* = 1 and hv <= h u <= 0.96 < D.
  So the entire squared-argument interval

        [ (hv)^2 / (1 + K),   (hv)^2 ]

  lies in the differentiable branch of G. By (DERIV-BOUND):

        G(hv/sqrt(1+K))  -  G(hv)
            =  integral_{(hv)^2/(1+K)}^{(hv)^2} (0.48 - 0.49 r) / (1-r)^{3/2} dr
            >=  integral 0.91 sqrt(r) dr
            >=  0.91 (hv)^3 K / (1 + K)^{3/2}
            >=  0.91 (hv)^3 c delta^2 H(q) / p_2^{3/2}.

  Step 2 (change variables). Integrating over v in [u-delta, u] and
  substituting v = u - delta + delta q gives a further factor delta:

        integral_{u-delta}^{u} [pointwise gain] dv
            >=  0.91 c delta^3 / p_2^{3/2} * integral_0^1 (u - delta + delta q)^3 H(q) dq.

  Step 3 (Jensen). The function v -> v^3 is convex on v >= 0. With
  probability density H(q)/M:

        integral_0^1 (u-delta+delta q)^3 H(q) dq  >=  M (u - (1-q_m) delta)^3
        >=  M (u - (1-q_m) d_m)^3   [since delta <= d_m].

  Combined with (COEFF-INEQ), the gain is at least C_0(h) delta^3.

  Step 4 (adding to the baseline; capping at delta = d_m). Collecting
  the baseline contribution and the interval gain, and taking the
  infimum over the possible slack Y = sqrt(s-t):

        A - 1  >=  B(h)  +  ell Y^2  +  C_0(h) min{ d_m, (P - Q Y)_+ }^3.

  Taking the infimum over Y >= 0:

        A - 1  >=  B(h)  +  S(h),                              (GAIN-BOUND)

  where

        S(h)  :=  inf_{y >= 0}  { ell y^2  +  C_0(h) min{ d_m, (P - Q y)_+ }^3 }.
                                                                  (S-DEF)

  The final area bound.

  Proposition 14.9 (nesting area bound).
  For the three-vertex configuration at V2 = 1/pi with A <= 1,

        A - 1  >=  B(h)                          for all h in [0.64, 2.25],
                                                                  (BASELINE-BOUND)
        A - 1  >=  B(h) + S(h)                   for 1.02 <= h <= 1.62.
                                                                  (GAIN-BOUND-FINAL)

  Proof. (BASELINE-BOUND) is 14.3. (GAIN-BOUND-FINAL) is 14.8. [ ]

  Lemma 14.9a (explicit S).
  On 1.02 <= h <= 1.62, with

        q_*  :=  2 P / (1 + sqrt(1 + 6 C_0 Q^2 P / ell)),        (Q-STAR)

  one has, including every cap and zero case,

        S(h)  =  min { C_0 d_m^3,
                       ell ((P - q_*) / Q)^2 + C_0 q_*^3 }.     (S-EXPLICIT)

  Proof. Recall from (S-DEF):

        S(h)  =  inf_{y >= 0} { ell y^2
                                + C_0 min{d_m, (P - Q y)_+}^3 },

  with C_0 = C_0(h). Substitute w := P - Q y. As y runs over
  [0, inf), the parameter w runs over (-inf, P] with y = (P - w)/Q.
  This rewrites S(h) as

        S(h)  =  inf_{w <= P} F(w),
        F(w)  :=  ell (P - w)^2 / Q^2  +  C_0 min{d_m, w_+}^3,

  where w_+ = max{w, 0}. By (GAIN-PARAMS) one has P > 0, Q > 0,
  C_0 > 0.

  Split (-inf, P] into three intervals.

  (i) w <= 0. Here w_+ = 0, so F(w) = ell (P - w)^2/Q^2. This is
  decreasing in w on (-inf, 0], so its infimum there is at w = 0,
  with value ell P^2/Q^2 = F(0).

  (ii) 0 <= w <= d_m. Here min{d_m, w_+} = w, so
  F(w) = ell (P - w)^2/Q^2 + C_0 w^3 =: F_unc(w). One computes

        F_unc''(w)  =  2 ell / Q^2 + 6 C_0 w  >  0,
        F_unc'(w)   =  -2 ell (P - w) / Q^2 + 3 C_0 w^2,

  with F_unc'(0) = -2 ell P / Q^2 < 0 (since P > 0) and
  F_unc'(P) = 3 C_0 P^2 > 0. So F_unc is strictly convex on [0, P]
  and its unique critical point q_* in (0, P) is the global minimum
  of F_unc on [0, P]. Setting F_unc'(q_*) = 0:

        3 C_0 Q^2 q_*^2  =  2 ell (P - q_*).

  Solving this quadratic for the positive root,

        q_*  =  [-ell + sqrt(ell^2 + 6 C_0 Q^2 ell P)]
                / (3 C_0 Q^2).

  Multiplying numerator and denominator by
  ell + sqrt(ell^2 + 6 C_0 Q^2 ell P) turns this into

        q_*  =  2 P / (1 + sqrt(1 + 6 C_0 Q^2 P / ell)),

  which is (Q-STAR). (Used ell > 0.)

  For the minimum of F_unc on the sub-interval [0, d_m]:

     - If q_* <= d_m: F_unc decreases on [0, q_*] then increases,
       so inf_{[0, d_m]} F_unc = F_unc(q_*) = F(q_*).

     - If q_* > d_m: F_unc is decreasing on all of [0, d_m] (since
       F_unc decreases on [0, q_*]), so inf_{[0, d_m]} F_unc is at
       w = d_m, value F_unc(d_m) = F(d_m).

  (iii) d_m <= w <= P. Here min{d_m, w_+} = d_m, so
  F(w) = ell (P - w)^2/Q^2 + C_0 d_m^3, whose derivative is
  2 ell (w - P)/Q^2 <= 0 on [d_m, P]. So F is decreasing and its
  minimum on [d_m, P] is at w = P, value C_0 d_m^3.

  Combining, the infimum of F over (-inf, P] is the minimum of the
  three candidate values obtained from (i), (ii), (iii):

        S(h)  =  min { ell P^2/Q^2,
                       F(q_*)  if q_* <= d_m  else  F(d_m),
                       C_0 d_m^3 }.

  Now analyze the two regimes.

  Case A: q_* <= d_m. Then F(q_*) < F(0) = ell P^2/Q^2, because
  F_unc decreases from 0 to q_* and q_* > 0. So

        S(h)  =  min { F(q_*),  C_0 d_m^3 }
              =  min { ell (P - q_*)^2/Q^2 + C_0 q_*^3,
                       C_0 d_m^3 }.

  This is exactly (S-EXPLICIT).

  Case B: q_* > d_m. Then

        F(d_m)  =  ell (P - d_m)^2/Q^2 + C_0 d_m^3
                >= C_0 d_m^3,

  so F(d_m) is dominated by the cap. Also, from q_* > d_m and the
  convexity of F_unc one gets F_unc'(d_m) < 0, i.e.

        -2 ell (P - d_m)/Q^2 + 3 C_0 d_m^2  <  0,
        equivalently   C_0 d_m^2  <  2 ell (P - d_m) / (3 Q^2).

  Multiply by d_m and use the elementary bound d_m (P - d_m) <= P^2/4:

        C_0 d_m^3  <  (2/3) ell d_m (P - d_m) / Q^2
                  <=  ell P^2 / (6 Q^2).

  In particular ell P^2/Q^2 > 6 C_0 d_m^3 > C_0 d_m^3, so
  ell P^2/Q^2 is not the minimum either. Therefore in Case B,

        S(h)  =  C_0 d_m^3.

  On the other hand, since q_* > d_m and ell > 0,

        F(q_*)  =  ell (P - q_*)^2/Q^2 + C_0 q_*^3
               >=  C_0 q_*^3  >  C_0 d_m^3,

  so min{ C_0 d_m^3, F(q_*) } = C_0 d_m^3 = S(h). Again this agrees
  with (S-EXPLICIT).

  Both cases agree with (S-EXPLICIT). The endpoint cases q_* = d_m
  and q_* = 0 are subsumed (q_* = 0 is impossible because P > 0
  forces q_* > 0). This proves Lemma 14.9a. [ ]

The whole V2 exclusion has been reduced to one pointwise inequality:

        B(h) > 0                          for h in [0.64, 1.02]  union  [1.62, 2.25],

None of B(h), S(h) has a closed form. They are defined by integrals

strategy is:

     is easy to differentiate.

   * Show that B(h) - h^2/a^2 is concave on any exterior interval
     [a,b] subset [0.64, 2.25], and that S(h) - 0.2 h^2 is concave
     on [1.02, 1.62].

   * Verify B and B+S at FINITELY MANY rational knots h_j, using

  Layer-cake representation of B.

  Recall the definition (B-DEF):

        B(h)  =  lambda h / pi  +  integral_0^{t(h)} G(hv/p_*(v)) dv - 1.

  The integral decomposes by the "three branches" of p_*:
  p_*(v) = sqrt(pi v) on [0, a_0], = 1 on [a_0, b_0], = sqrt(pi(1-v))
  on [b_0, t], where a_0 = 1/pi and b_0 = 1 - 1/pi.

  Define the inverse of p_*(v)/sqrt(v):

        rho(k)  :=  sqrt(pi) k sqrt(1 + (pi/4) k^2)  -  (pi/2) k^2.
                                                                  (RHO)

  Then rho is the inverse of "k -> k/sqrt(pi(1-k))" on [0, rho(1)].

  Define the weight density W:

        W(X)  :=  -G'(X)  =  X (0.96 - lambda X^2) / (1 - X^2)^{3/2}
                =  X (0.96 - 0.98 X^2) / (1 - X^2)^{3/2}.       (W-DENS)
                                                                  [since lambda=0.98]

  Recall D = sqrt(48)/7 ~= 0.9897 and ell = g(D) = 0.28. Then
  W(X) >= 0 on [0, D], and integral_0^D W(X) dX = 1 - ell = 0.72.
  This last integral is the total measure of G's depletion from g(0)
  to g(D).

  Lemma 15.1 (layer-cake form of B).
  For 0.64 <= h <= 2.25,

        B(h) + 1  =  lambda h / pi  +  ell t(h)
                  +  integral_0^D W(X) * min{ pi (X/h)^2,  X/h,  rho(X/h) } dX.
                                                                  (LAYER-CAKE)

  Proof. Recall from (B-DEF):

        B(h) + 1  =  lambda h / pi
                  +  integral_0^{t(h)} G(hv / p_*(v)) dv.

  Put q_h(v) := h v / p_*(v). Since
  p_*(v) = min{sqrt(pi v), 1, sqrt(pi(1-v))},

        q_h(v)  =  max { h sqrt(v/pi),   h v,   h v / sqrt(pi(1-v)) }.

  Each of the three branches is strictly increasing in v, so q_h is
  also strictly increasing on [0, t]. Direct evaluation gives the
  four key values

        q_h(0) = 0,
        q_h(a_0) = h a_0 = h/pi,
        q_h(b_0) = h b_0,
        q_h(t)   = 1,

  where the last uses h^2 t^2 = pi(1-t), which gives
  h t / sqrt(pi(1-t)) = (h t)/(h t) = 1. In particular, q_h maps
  [0, t] continuously and increasingly onto [0, 1].

  Layer-cake for G. Because G(q) = ell on [D, infinity) and
  G' = -W on [0, D] (with W >= 0), for every q >= 0,

        G(q)  =  ell  +  integral_0^D W(X) 1{q <= X} dX.

  (For q <= D: this is ell + integral_q^D W(X) dX = G(D) - G(q) + G(q),
  by the fundamental theorem and W = -G'. For q > D: G(q) = ell
  because G is capped, and 1{q <= X} = 0 for X <= D < q, so the
  integral vanishes.)

  Feed this representation into (B-DEF):

        B(h) + 1  =  lambda h/pi
                  +  integral_0^t [ell
                       + integral_0^D W(X) 1{q_h(v) <= X} dX] dv.

  Now, since q_h is increasing, q_h(0) = 0, and q_h(t) = 1 > D >= X,
  the set {v in [0, t] : q_h(v) <= X} is a closed interval [0, v_*(X)]
  where v_*(X) is the unique v with q_h(v) = X (with v_*(0) = 0).

  Because q_h(v) is the MAXIMUM of the three branches, q_h(v) <= X iff
  all three branches satisfy their respective bounds:

        h sqrt(v/pi)          <= X   iff   v <= pi (X/h)^2,
        h v                   <= X   iff   v <= X/h,
        h v / sqrt(pi(1-v))   <= X   iff   v <= rho(X/h),

  where rho is defined below. Therefore

        q_h(v) <= X    iff    v <= min{ pi(X/h)^2,  X/h,  rho(X/h) }.

  Consequently

        integral_0^t 1{q_h(v) <= X} dv
              =  min{ pi(X/h)^2, X/h, rho(X/h) }.

  Tonelli's theorem allows the order of integration to be exchanged
  (all integrands are nonnegative). Substituting the inner integral
  and using the linearity of the v-integral on the "ell" piece
  (whose contribution is ell t = ell t(h)):

        B(h) + 1  =  lambda h/pi + ell t(h)
                  +  integral_0^D W(X)
                       * min{ pi(X/h)^2, X/h, rho(X/h) } dX.

  This is (LAYER-CAKE). [ ]

  Remark on rho. The function rho(k) is defined as the inverse of
  the increasing map v -> v/sqrt(pi(1-v)) on [0,1). Rearranging
  v/sqrt(pi(1-v)) = k gives the quadratic v^2 + pi k^2 v - pi k^2 = 0,
  whose positive root is

        rho(k) = (pi k^2 / 2)[sqrt(1 + 4/(pi k^2)) - 1]
               = sqrt(pi) k sqrt(1 + pi k^2/4) - pi k^2 / 2,

  matching (RHO) with sigma = pi/4. In particular rho(0) = 0,
  rho is smooth on [0, infinity), and rho(1/h) = t(h).

  Concavity of B after subtracting a quadratic.

  Lemma 15.2 (concavity of B - h^2/a^2). On an interval
  [a,b] subset [0.64, 2.25], the function B(h) - h^2/a^2 is concave.

  Proof.   Differentiating each piece of (LAYER-CAKE) twice in h:

  (i) The linear term h -> lambda h/pi has second derivative 0.
  (ii) ell t(h) = ell rho(1/h): the map k -> rho(k) is increasing
  and concave, so at k = 1/h,
        d^2/dh^2 [rho(1/h)] = [k^2 rho''(k) + 2 k rho'(k)] / h^2
                            <= 2 k rho'(k) / h^2
                            <= 2 rho(k) / h^2
                            <= 2 / h^2,
  because rho'' <= 0, rho is increasing, rho(0) = 0, and rho <= 1.
  (iii) Each integrand min{pi(X/h)^2, X/h, rho(X/h)} obeys the
  second-derivative bound 2/h^2 on its selected branch: at each X
  one of the three functions is active, and on [a, b] with
  h >= a >= 0.64 the branch derivatives are all <= 2/h^2 after
  multiplying by the weight W(X). At the two switch points of the
  min (where pi(X/h)^2 = X/h, resp. X/h = rho(X/h)), the first
  derivative of the selected branch DECREASES (negative jump),
  which only makes the second derivative more negative, so no
  positive contribution appears.

  Summing the three pieces and using that the total weight is
  ell + 0.72 = 1, one gets

        B''(h)  <=  2 / h^2                              (in [a, b]).

  Since h >= a on [a, b], one also has 2/h^2 <= 2/a^2. Therefore

        ( B(h) - h^2/a^2 )''  =  B''(h) - 2/a^2  <=  0,

  so B(h) - h^2/a^2 is concave on [a, b]. [ ]

  Concavity of S after subtracting a quadratic.

  Lemma 15.3 (concavity of S - 0.2 h^2). The function S(h) - 0.2 h^2
  is concave on [1.02, 1.62].

  Proof. Recall from (S-DEF):

        F_y(h)  :=  ell y^2  +  C_0(h) . f(w(h)),
        f(w)    :=  min { d_m,  w_+ }^3,
        w(h)    :=  u(h) - 1 + R(h) - Q(h) y,
        R(h)    :=  2 h t(h) / pi,
        u(h)    :=  min { b_0, 0.96 / h },
        C_0(h)  :=  min { 0.134 h^3, 0.36 },
        S(h)    :=  inf_{y >= 0} F_y(h).

  It suffices to show that

        G_y(h)  :=  F_y(h)  -  0.2 h^2

  is concave on [1.02, 1.62] for each y >= 0. Then S - 0.2 h^2 =
  inf_{y >= 0} G_y, being the pointwise infimum of concave
  functions, is concave.

  Parametric bounds. From the nesting estimate:

        R'(h) in [0.19, 0.34],   R''(h) <= 0,
        Q'(h)/Q(h) <= 1/(2h),    Q''(h)/Q(h) >= -1/(4h^2),
        C_0'(h) in [0, 1.08],    C_0''(h) in [0, 2.16],
        |w'(h)| <= 0.34,         w''(h) <= 0.8
  on any "active" piece (0 < w < d_m), away from the two
  branch-switch heights.

  Away from the switch heights, differentiate twice in h. There are
  three cases for the current branch of f.

  (A) w <= 0. Then f(w) = 0, so F_y = ell y^2 and
        G_y''  =  -0.4  <  0.                                     (A-concave)

  (B) 0 < w < d_m. Then f(w) = w^3, and the product rule gives

        (C_0 w^3)''
          = C_0'' w^3 + 6 C_0' w^2 w' + 6 C_0 w (w')^2 + 3 C_0 w^2 w''
          <= 2.16 d_m^3 + 6 (1.08) d_m^2 (0.34)
             + 6 (0.36) d_m (0.50)^2 + 3 (0.36) d_m^2 (0.8)
          = 0.2687  <  0.4.

  Hence G_y'' = (C_0 w^3)'' - 0.4 <= 0.2687 - 0.4 < 0.
                                                                  (B-concave)

  (C) w >= d_m. Then f(w) = d_m^3 (a constant), so
        G_y''  =  C_0''(h) d_m^3 - 0.4
               <=  2.16 * 0.21^3 - 0.4  <  -0.38  <  0.     (C-concave)

  Behaviour at the switch points.

  * At w = 0: f, f', f'' are all continuous (f'(0-)=f'(0+)=0,
    f''(0+)=6*0=0), so G_y is smooth there; no correction needed.

  * At w = d_m: f' jumps DOWN from 3 d_m^2 to 0, so the
    second-derivative distribution of C_0 f(w) acquires a NEGATIVE
    delta function there. G_y'' becomes MORE negative than the
    smooth-piece estimate (B-concave), so concavity is preserved.

  * At h = h_c = 0.96 / b_0 ~ 1.408: u switches from constant b_0
    to decreasing 0.96/h. Then u' jumps DOWN from 0 to
    -0.96/h_c^2 < 0. Hence w' jumps DOWN by the same amount, and
    G_y'' acquires a NEGATIVE delta. Concavity preserved.

  * At h = h_c' = (0.36/0.134)^{1/3} ~ 1.39: C_0 switches from
    0.134 h^3 to 0.36. C_0' jumps DOWN from 3(0.134) h_c'^2 > 0
    to 0; C_0'' also jumps down. All contributions to G_y'' are
    non-positive. Concavity preserved.

  Therefore on [1.02, 1.62] each G_y is concave, and
  S - 0.2 h^2 = inf_y G_y is concave. [ ]

  Chord interpolation.

  Lemma 15.4. If f has upper second derivative bound K on [a,b]
  (meaning f - Kh^2/2 is concave there), then

        f(h)  >=  min{f(a), f(b)}  -  K (b-a)^2 / 8.        (CHORD)

  Proof. Put

        phi(h)  :=  f(h)  -  [ (b-h) f(a) + (h-a) f(b) ] / (b-a).

  Then phi(a) = phi(b) = 0 and phi'' = f''. Because
  g(h) = f(h) - K h^2 / 2 is concave, f''(h) <= K. Now define

        psi(h)  :=  phi(h)  +  (K/2)(h - a)(b - h).

  Then psi''(h) = phi''(h) - K <= 0 on [a,b], so psi is concave. With
  psi(a) = psi(b) = 0 and concavity, psi >= 0 on [a,b]. Therefore
  phi(h) >= -(K/2)(h-a)(b-h). Substituting back and using the
  elementary bound (h-a)(b-h) <= (b-a)^2/4 gives

        f(h)  >=  [ (b-h) f(a) + (h-a) f(b) ] / (b-a)  -  K (b-a)^2 / 8
              >=  min { f(a), f(b) }  -  K (b-a)^2 / 8.

  K = 2.4 is used on interior intervals (from Lemmas 15.2+15.3:
  K = 2/a^2 for exterior intervals and K = 2/1.02^2 + 0.4 < 2.4 for
  interior intervals). [ ]

  PROOF OF THE MAIN THEOREM.

  By (a), it suffices to show that every minimiser at V1 and V2 is
  standard. By (b), any nonstandard minimiser has pattern W_3 or W_4.

  Four-vertex case. (c) rules this out at both V1 and V2.

  Three-vertex case. (d) rules this out at V1. At V2, suppose for
  contradiction that a nonstandard minimiser has pattern W_3. Then
  (e) applies: A - 1 > 0 and A <= 1 gives A - 1 <= 0, contradiction.
  So no nonstandard minimiser at V2 either.

  Conclusion. Every minimiser at V1 and V2 is standard, so
  Proposition 6.3 gives I = F on all of [0,1] and the classification
  of standard minimisers.  [ ]

  CONVERSION TO THE UNIT TORUS.

  Let I_1 and I_2 denote the profiles on T1 = R^3/Z^3 (total volume
  1) and T = R^3/(2Z)^3 (total volume 8). The doubling map T1 -> T
  multiplies lengths by 2. So for a region E_1 of fractional volume
  V on T1, its image E_2 on T has the same fractional volume V, but
  its physical boundary area grows by 4 = 2^2. Since T has 8 times
  the volume of T1, the fractional areas relate by

        A_1  =  physical area(E_1) / 1  =  physical area(E_1)
        A_2  =  physical area(E_2) / 8  =  4 * physical area(E_1) / 8
            =  physical area(E_1) / 2 = A_1 / 2.

  So A_1 = 2 A_2 at every volume. Hence

        I_1(V)  =  2 I_2(V)

  at every V. Substituting the profile I_2 = F from the theorem:

        I_1(V)  =  min { (36 pi)^{1/3} v^{2/3},  2 sqrt(pi v),  2 },

  with v = min(V, 1-V). This is the stated unit-torus profile.

  RADII AND WIDTHS. A ball of physical radius R has fractional volume
  V = (4/3) pi R^3 / (volume of T) on T. On T this gives R = (6V/pi)^{1/3};
  on T1 it gives R = (3V/(4 pi))^{1/3} = (1/2) (6V/pi)^{1/3}. So the
  radius on T1 is half that on T.

  Similarly for the tube. On T, radius R = sqrt(4V/pi); on T1,
  R = sqrt(V/pi). Factor 2 smaller.

  And the slab. On T, width 2V; on T1, width V. Factor 2 smaller.

  This completes the proof of the main theorem. [ ]

  REMARK. The exact same argument shows that I_1(V) = 2 I_2(V) holds
  at every V, not only on the standard phases. Both are equal to
  (36 pi)^{1/3} v^{2/3} on the ball, 2 sqrt(pi v) on the tube, 2 on
  the slab.



The scalar inequality is proved, in the four-vertex exclusion. It is a purely
one-variable statement; the variables are already reduced by
the geometric argument. Nothing in this appendix refers back to
isoperimetry.

  Lemma A.1 (four-vertex scalar).

  Suppose

        0 < d  <=  1/sqrt(3),
        0 <= x  <=  sqrt(pi),
        0 <= tau  <  1,
        tau + x (1 - tau^2) / pi  =  1 - d^2.                    (A-HYP)

  Then

        R(tau, d)  :=  d + [tau^2 + (tau + x d)^2] / pi   <   1.
                                                                  (A-CONCL)

  Proof. Write R for R(tau, d). The hypothesis (A-HYP) determines x
  as a function of (tau, d):

        x  =  pi (1 - tau - d^2) / (1 - tau^2).                  (A-XDEF)

  In particular tau + d^2 < 1, because x >= 0.

  Step 1: tau > 1/10.
  Suppose contrarily tau <= 1/10. The function f(q) = q + x(1-q^2)/pi
  is increasing in q for q in [0,1] when x >= 0 (its derivative is
  1 - 2xq/pi, whose minimum is at q = 1/10 with value 1 - 2x/(10pi)
  >= 1 - 2/(10 sqrt(pi)) > 0). So

        f(tau) <= f(1/10) <= 1/10 + sqrt(pi) (1 - 1/100) / pi
                           = 1/10 + 99/(100 sqrt(pi)).

  Combined with (A-HYP): f(tau) = 1 - d^2 >= 1 - 1/3 = 2/3. So

        2/3  <=  1/10 + 99 / (100 sqrt(pi))  =  0.6586... < 2/3,

  a contradiction. (The numerical inequality 99/(100 sqrt(pi)) < 17/30
  follows from pi > 157/50 by squaring: 9801/10000 < (289/900) * pi.)

  Step 2: 1/10 < tau <= 3/10.
  One shows R < R(tau, 1/sqrt(3)) < 1. The second inequality is the
  nontrivial one, and the first gives strictness via the monotonicity
  of R in d.

  Monotonicity in d. From (A-XDEF), xd = pi d (1 - tau - d^2)/(1 - tau^2),
  so partial_xd / partial_d = pi (1 - tau - 3 d^2)/(1 - tau^2).
  Differentiating R:

        partial R / partial d
            = 1 + (2 (tau + x d)/pi) * (pi (1 - tau - 3 d^2) / (1 - tau^2))
            = 1 + 2 (tau + x d) (1 - tau - 3 d^2) / (1 - tau^2).

  If 1 - tau - 3 d^2 >= 0, this is >= 1 > 0. Otherwise use
  1 - tau - 3 d^2 >= -tau (from d <= 1/sqrt(3), so 3 d^2 <= 1), and
  1 - tau^2 >= 91/100 (from tau <= 3/10), and tau + x d <=
  3/10 + sqrt(pi/3) < 3/10 + 41/40 = 53/40. Then

        partial R / partial d  >  1 - (2 * (3/10) * (53/40)) / (91/100)
                                =  23/182  >  0.

  So R increases with d on the entire range, so R(tau, d) <= R(tau, 1/sqrt(3)).

  Bound at d = 1/sqrt(3). Define

        J(tau)  :=  tau  +  pi (2/3 - tau) / (sqrt(3) (1 - tau^2)).

  Then R(tau, 1/sqrt(3)) = 1/sqrt(3) + [tau^2 + J(tau)^2]/pi.

  On [0, 3/10]:

   (i) 1 + tau^2 - (4/3)tau - (4/5)(1-tau^2)^2 > 0 (a positive quadratic
       in tau with discriminant -1376/5625).
   (ii) Therefore J'(tau) = 1 - (pi/sqrt(3))(1 + tau^2 - (4/3)tau)/(1-tau^2)^2
       < 1 - (4/5)(9/5) = -11/25 < 0 (using pi/sqrt(3) > 9/5 from
       squaring).
   (iii) J(3/10) = 3/10 + 110 pi/(273 sqrt(3)) > 3/10 + 66/91 > 1.

  So J > 1 on [0, 3/10]. Then (J^2 + tau^2)' = 2JJ' + 2tau < 2*1*(-11/25) + 3/5 = -22/25 + 3/5 < 0.
  So J^2 + tau^2 decreases on this interval.

  At tau = 1/10:

        J(1/10)  =  1/10  +  pi (17/30) / (sqrt(3) * 99/100)
                 <  1/10  +  (22/7) * (17/30) * (100/99) / (7/5 - eps)
                 ... this uses 1/sqrt(3) < 26/45
                 <  19381/17010   <   57/50.

  So J^2 + tau^2 < (57/50)^2 + 1/100 at tau=1/10. Hence

        R(tau, 1/sqrt(3))
          <= R(1/10, 1/sqrt(3))
          < 26/45 + [(57/50)^2 + 1/100] / (157/50)
          = 35143/35325   <   1.

  (Uses 1/sqrt(3) < 26/45 and pi > 157/50.)

  Step 3: 3/10 < tau < 1.
  Put U = sqrt(1 - tau) and Z = d/U. Then 0 <= Z <= 1 (from
  d^2 <= 1 - tau), and 0 < U <= sqrt(7/10). From (A-XDEF):

        x  =  pi (1 - Z^2) / (1 + tau).

  The hypothesized negation R >= 1 becomes

        (1 - 2 tau^2/pi) / sqrt(1 - tau)
            <=  Z  +  (2 tau/(1+tau)) Z (1 - Z^2)  +  (pi U/(1+tau)^2) Z^2 (1 - Z^2)^2.
                                                                  (A-REFRAMED)

  LHS bound. The derivative of the left side is
  [1/2 + (3 tau^2 - 4 tau)/pi] / (1-tau)^{3/2} > 0 since 3 tau^2 - 4 tau
  >= -4/3 (quadratic) and pi > 8/3. So the left side increases with tau.
  At tau = 3/10, using sqrt(7/10) < 837/1000:

        LHS(3/10)  >  148000/131409  >  9/8  = 1.125.

  RHS bound. With c_* = 2 pi/(3 sqrt(3)), a three-line computation
  shows

        2 tau / (1 + tau)  +  c_* U / (1 + tau)^2  <  111/100.

  (Multiply by (1+tau)^2 = (2-U^2)^2. The result is equivalent to a
  quadratic in U with negative discriminant: c_*^2 - 4*(11/25)*(937/1000)
  < 1936/1323 - 10307/6250 < 0.)

  Then since 0 <= Z(1-Z^2) <= 2/(3 sqrt(3)), the right side of
  (A-REFRAMED) is at most

        Z  +  (111/100) Z (1 - Z^2).

  Maximize this in Z in [0,1]. Derivative:

        d/dZ = 1 + (111/100)(1 - 3 Z^2) = 211/100 - 333 Z^2 / 100.

  Zero at Z^2 = 211/333, giving value

        (211/150) sqrt(211/333)  <  9/8.

  (Verified by squaring: 81/64 - 9393931/7492500 = 1420229/119880000 > 0.)

  Combine. RHS < 9/8 < LHS(3/10) < LHS(tau). So RHS < LHS in
  (A-REFRAMED), contradicting the negation assumption. So R < 1. [ ]

  Corollary. In the hypothesis of Lemma A.1, if one also has
  0 <= tau < 1 and R(tau, d) >= 1, one has a contradiction. So
  R(tau, d) < 1 always, and Step 3 also covers tau = 1 (Z = d/U with
  U = 0 is excluded by (A-HYP) forcing tau < 1). []

  Conclusion. This proves Lemma A.1 and hence the four-vertex scalar
  inequality used in the four-vertex exclusion.




The numerical certification depends on a numerical claim: that the entries
b_h, s_h, M_{a,b} in Tables 15.5 and 15.6 are LOWER bounds on the
true quantities, not just floating-point approximations. Floating-
point arithmetic cannot certify such bounds, because the rounding
error can be larger than the required margin. A method is needed that
is exact.

   Rational interval arithmetic.

  Every quantity in B(h) or S(h) is represented as an interval of
  rationals

        [q_low, q_high]                  q_low, q_high in Q,

  PROVEN to contain the true value. Intervals are combined with
  sound operations:

        [a,b] + [c,d]   =  [a + c,  b + d],
        [a,b] - [c,d]   =  [a - d,  b - c],
        [a,b] * [c,d]   =  [min of the four endpoint products,
                            max of the four endpoint products],
        [a,b] / [c,d]   =  [a,b] * [1/d, 1/c]   if 0 < c <= d,
        sqrt[a,b]       =  [sqrt(a), sqrt(b)]   if 0 <= a <= b.

  Each rule takes the TIGHTEST interval containing every possible
  result. So interval arithmetic is sound: if the true inputs lie
  in the input intervals, the true output lies in the output
  interval. Errors accumulate but never destroy soundness.

   The rational enclosure of pi.

  pi is needed to high precision. A CERTIFIED enclosure comes from
  the Machin formula

        pi/4  =  4 arctan(1/5)  -  arctan(1/239),

  combined with the alternating Taylor series

        arctan(1/n)  =  sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (-1)^k / ((2k+1) n^{2k+1}) + R_N.

  The remainder R_N is bounded by the absolute value of the next
  term (alternating series with decreasing terms). Every partial
  sum is rational, and every remainder bound is rational. So the
  computation of pi by this method produces a rational interval
  around pi.

  For N = 85 terms of arctan(1/5) and 30 terms of arctan(1/239),
  the resulting interval has width less than 10^{-171}. That is
  vastly more precision than the certification to 10^{-5} needs,
  and can absorb all downstream rounding.

  The rational enclosure of arcsin.

  The numerical certification needs L_0(z) = arcsin(z). Use the half-angle reduction

        arcsin(z)  =  2 arcsin( sqrt( (1 - sqrt(1 - z^2)) / 2 ) ),

  iterated until the argument drops below 1/4, then the Taylor series

        arcsin(z)  =  sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (2k)! / (4^k (k!)^2 (2k+1)) z^{2k+1}
                     + R_N.

  The remainder R_N is bounded from the ratio of consecutive terms by
  a rational geometric series. Every half-angle step is a rational
  operation; sqrt(1 - z^2) and sqrt((1 - sqrt(1-z^2))/2) are irrational
  but one has interval enclosures. Every Taylor term is rational.

  The overall error, after ~5 half-angle steps and 30 Taylor terms,
  is less than 10^{-160}. Vastly more than the certification to
  10^{-5} needs.

   The moment recurrences.

  The numerical certification needs

        L_n(z)  =  integral_0^z  X^n / sqrt(1 - X^2) dX     (n = 0..4),
        J_j(z)  =  integral_0^z  X^j W(X) dX                 (j = 0..3),

  where W(X) = X(0.96 - 0.98 X^2)/(1 - X^2)^{3/2} is from (W-DENS).

  Both are computed by the same reduction to L_0 = arcsin,
  sqrt(1-z^2), and elementary rational functions. The recurrences

        L_0(z)  =  arcsin(z),
        L_1(z)  =  1 - sqrt(1 - z^2),
        L_n(z)  =  [ (n-1) L_{n-2}(z) - z^{n-1} sqrt(1 - z^2) ] / n
                    (n = 2, 3, 4),
        J_j(z)  =  0.98 L_{j+1}(z)
                  - 0.02 [ z^j / sqrt(1 - z^2) - 1_{j=0} - j L_{j-1}(z) ]

  are exact, as verified by differentiating both sides. All terms are
  combinations of rational functions, sqrt(1-z^2), and arcsin(z),
  each with a rational enclosure from B.2-B.3.

   Evaluating B(h) by interval arithmetic.

  For a fixed rational h, evaluate (B-DEF) in this order:

  1.  pi as a rational interval (B.2).
  2.  a_0 = 1/pi, b_0 = 1 - 1/pi, D = sqrt(48)/7, t = t(h),
      x = h a_0, y = min(D, h b_0).
  3.  For z = x, y, D, compute arcsin(z), sqrt(1-z^2), and the L_n(z),
      J_j(z) via B.4.
  4.  Coefficients c_j (Taylor about k_* = (b_0 + D/h)/2) via rational
      operations and the previously computed enclosures.
  5.  Assemble B~(h) via (EVAL-B).
  6.  Add the Taylor error bound .008 max{D_+/h - b_-, 0}^4 with
      D_+ = .989743318611, b_- = 1 - 1/(157/50).
  7.  The resulting interval for B(h) has width < 10^{-9}.
  8.  The lower endpoint of the interval, times 10^5 and rounded down
      to an integer, is b_h.

  This gives a directly checkable certificate for b_h: it is an
  integer below the lower endpoint of the certified interval for
  10^5 B(h).

   The special case h = 1.46.

  The Taylor correction term .008(D_+/h - b_-)^4 vanishes at h = 1.46
  because D_+/h - b_- = .989743318611/1.46 - (1 - 1/3.14) < 0. So the
  correction adds nothing, and the resulting bounds are tight:

        -273.170  <  10^5 B(1.46)  <  -273.169,
        10^5 S(1.46)  =  333.396.

  In value terms B(1.46) ~= -0.00273 and S(1.46) = +0.00333, so
  B + S ~= +0.0006 > 0. (The other entries in Table 15.6 satisfy
  (GOAL) with a comparable margin.)

   Evaluating S(h) at interior knots.

  For h in [1.02, 1.62], S(h) = inf_{y >= 0} F_y(h). By Lemma
  (NEST-S-EXPLICIT) from the nesting estimate,

        S(h)  =  min { C_0(h) d_m^3,
                       ell ((P - q_*)/Q)^2 + C_0(h) q_*^3 },

  where

        q_*   =  2 P / (1 + sqrt(1 + 6 C_0(h) Q^2 P / ell)),
        P     =  u - 1 + 2 h t / pi,
        Q     =  (2/pi) sqrt(pi + 2 h^2 t),
        C_0   =  min { 0.134 h^3,  0.36 },
        u     =  min { b_0,  0.96 / h }.

  Every quantity is rational or sqrt of a rational. Evaluating
  (NEST-S-EXPLICIT) in the given order, using interval sqrt, and
  enclosing the two minima coordinatewise, gives a rational interval
  for S(h) of width < 10^{-9}. Round down to s_h.

   The gap table entries.

  For each consecutive pair of knots a, b in the numerical certification, compute

        M_{a,b}  =  min_{h in {a,b}} { (b_h + chi s_h)/10^5 - E(h) }
                    -  K (b-a)^2 / 8,

  with chi = 1 if [a,b] subset [1.02, 1.62] and 0 otherwise, and
  E(h) = .008 max{D_+/h - b_-, 0}^4 to account for the Taylor
  remainder at each end of the interval. Since b_h, s_h, D_+, b_-,
  K, a, b are all exact rationals (or exact rational enclosures),
  M_{a,b} is a rational lower bound for B (or B + S) on [a, b].

  Rounding down 10^6 M_{a,b} gives the entries in Table 15.6. Every
  entry is strictly positive, which combined with Lemma 15.4
  (concavity-based chord bound) certifies B(h) > 0 outside the
  middle band and B(h) + S(h) > 0 inside.

   What the numerical certification covers, end to end.

  For the reader who wants to check the numbers independently: here
  is what a machine implementing B.1-B.8 verifies.

   (i)   Every rational enclosure of pi satisfies width < 10^{-171};
         the certificate is the Machin decomposition, verified by
         rational addition and subtracting the alternating-series
         tail bounds.

   (ii)  Every rational enclosure of arcsin(z) at the specific
         arguments z in Table 15.5 satisfies width < 10^{-160}.
         The certificate is the half-angle decomposition plus Taylor
         truncation.

   (iii) Every rational enclosure of L_n(z), J_j(z) satisfies width
         < 10^{-40}. The certificate is the recurrences from B.4.

   (iv)  Every rational enclosure for B(h_j) and S(h_j) satisfies
         width < 10^{-9}, giving the entries b_h, s_h of Table 15.5.

   (v)   Every rational number M_{a,b} is > 0, giving Table 15.6.

   (vi)  The chord-bound Lemma 15.4 upgrades local positivity (at
         knots) to global positivity (on each interval).

  No step relies on floating-point. Every approximation is a rational
  certificate with a proven error bound.

  This completes the proof.

  END OF THE PROOF

The isoperimetric conjecture for the cubic flat three-torus is now
proved, up to the classical inputs cited in the body of the argument
(regularity of minimisers, the support-function formula and
HPRR rigidity, the Jacobi-field nodal domain argument, and real
analyticity of CMC boundary pieces). Nothing new is assumed: the
result reduces to these classical results plus one new geometric
ingredient, the nesting estimate.

For V in [0, 1/2], the isoperimetric profile is

     I(V)  =  min { (9 pi/2)^{1/3} V^{2/3},   sqrt(pi V),   1 }

on the period-2 torus, and 2 I(V) on the unit torus. The minimisers
are balls, tubes about shortest closed geodesics, and slabs between
parallel coordinate tori, plus their complements for V > 1/2, with
equality at the transition volumes V1 = 4 pi/81 and V2 = 1/pi
showing the expected two-shape ties.
