# Critical slowing down in the Sherrington--Kirkpatrick model

*An approachable but complete proof.*

---

## 1. The model

Let n be a large positive integer. A spin configuration is a point


x=(x_1,...,x_n)in{-1,+1}^n .


The Sherrington--Kirkpatrick (SK) interaction is a random symmetric matrix W=(W_{ij})_{1le i,jle n} with


W_{ij}sim N(0,1/n)(i<j),

W_{ii}sim N(0,2/n),

W_{ij}=W_{ji}.


Thus W is a Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) matrix. The diagonal entries do not affect the spin model, because x_i^2=1 for every spin. They are included only because they make the matrix spectrum behave exactly like GOE; we will remove them from the Gibbs law whenever convenient.

The Hamiltonian is


H_W(x)=frac12 x^T W x
=sum_{i<j}W_{ij}x_ix_j+frac12sum_i W_{ii}.


The last term is the same for every configuration x, so it cancels from every probability ratio. The Gibbs measure is


mu_W(x)=frac{e^{H_W(x)}}{
sum_{yin{-1,+1}^n}e^{H_W(y)}
}
propto
exp!(sum_{i<j}W_{ij}x_ix_j).


We often write mu=mu_W when the disorder W is fixed.

The number beta is the inverse temperature. In this paper the inverse temperature is exactly beta=1, the critical value. For beta<1 the model is in the high-temperature phase and mixes quickly; at beta=1 the spectral edge is critical and slow modes appear on scale n^{-2/3}.

---

## 2. Heat-bath dynamics

A heat-bath update at site i replaces x_i by a fresh spin chosen from its conditional law given all other spins. Since a spin has only two possible values, this means:


x_i=
+1 & with probability dfrac{e^{g_i(x)}}{2cosh g_i(x)},[2mm]
-1 & with probability dfrac{e^{-g_i(x)}}{2cosh g_i(x)},


g_i(x)=sum_{jne i}W_{ij}x_j .


Equivalently, the new spin has conditional mean tanh g_i(x).

We use two clocks.

* **Continuous time.** Each site has an independent rate-one exponential clock. When its clock rings, that site is refreshed. The generator is


L={i=1}^n(P_i-Id),

P_if(x)=E_[f(x)mid x_{-i}],


where x_{-i} means all spins except x_i.

* **Discrete time.** At each step one site is chosen uniformly at random and refreshed. The transition operator is


P=frac1nsum_{i=1}^n P_i .


These clocks are related by


L=n(P-Id).L=n(P-Id).


So one unit of continuous time corresponds roughly to n attempted discrete updates, but the exact relation is the displayed identity.

For a starting configuration x, let


d_t^{ct}(W,x)
=|e^{tL},·)-mu_W|_{TV},

d_k^{dt}(W,x)
=|P^k(x,·)-mu_W|_{TV},


where total variation is


|nu-mu|_{TV}
=frac12sum_x|nu(x)-mu(x)|
=sup_{A⊆{-1,+1}^n}|nu(A)-mu(A)|.


The worst-start mixing times are


t_{mix}
=inf{tge0:max_x d_t^{ct}(W,x)le1/4},



k_{mix}
=min{kinN{0}:max_x d_k^{dt}(W,x)le1/4}.


All probabilistic statements below are over the random matrix W (and, when we temporarily add an auxiliary Gaussian diagonal, over that auxiliary randomness as well).

## 3. The three main results

### Theorem A (typical equilibrium starts are slow)

For every deterministic sequence t_nge0 with


t_n=o(n^{2/3}),


we have


mu_W{x:d_{t_n}^{ct}(W,x)>1/4}
→ 1
 in probability over W.


For every deterministic integer sequence k_nge0 with


k_n=o(n^{5/3}),


we have


mu_W{x:d_{k_n}^{dt}(W,x)>1/4}
→ 1
 in probability over W.


The theorem tests each realized equilibrium starting configuration separately. If instead one averaged the transition law over the initial configuration drawn from mu_W, stationarity would make the law equal to mu_W at every time. The theorem says that this averaging hides a split: most fixed starts are still far away.

### Theorem B (covariance and linear tests)

Let


Σ=Cov_{mu_W}(x)=mu_W[xx^T],


and let


D_{mu_W}(f)
=sum_{i=1}^nmu_W[Var(fmid x_{-i})]


be the unscaled Dirichlet form. For linear functions a^Tx, define


R_{lin}(mu_W)
=sup_{ainR^n∖R^n∖{0}}
frac{Var_{mu_W}(a^Tx)}
{D_{mu_W}(a^Tx)} .


For every positive deterministic sequence M_nto∞, with probability tending to one both |Σ| and R_{lin}(mu_W) lie in


[
frac{n^{2/3}}{M_n},
 M_n n^{2/3}
].


The factor M_n can diverge as slowly as we like. The theorem only controls linear functions; it does not by itself control every function.

### Theorem C (critical mixing bounds)

For every fixed ε>0,


P{
n^{2/3-ε}le t_{mix}le e^{ε n}
}→ 1,



P{
n^{5/3-ε}le k_{mix}le e^{ε n}
}→ 1.


The lower bounds come from Theorem A. The upper bounds are proved by comparison with one fixed high-temperature model and are exponentially weak. The sharper matching exponents are the subject of the companion paper, but the present paper proves the displayed bounds completely.

## 5. Elementary identities

Before proving anything hard, we record the bookkeeping that makes every later step work.

### Lemma 5.1 (Heat-bath forms)

For every function f:{-1,+1}^ntoR, each P_i is an orthogonal projection in L^2(mu). The unscaled Dirichlet form is


D_
=-< f,Lf>_calLf>_mu
=sum_{i=1}^nmu[(f-P_if)^2]
=sum_{i=1}^ninf_{h_i}mu[(f-h_i(x_{-i}))^2],


where the infimum is over all functions h_i of x_{-i}. The discrete form is


< f,(Id-P)f>_mu=frac1nD_,


and the spectrum of P lies in [0,1].

For a linear function a^Tx,


D_{i=1}^n a_i^2alpha_i,

alpha_i=mu[sech^2 g_i],

g_i(x)=sum_{jne i}W_{ij}x_j .


#### Proof

Conditional expectation onto functions of x_{-i} is the orthogonal projection in L^2(mu). Therefore


< f,(Id-P_i)f>_mu
=|f-P_if|_{L^2(mu)}^2,


and this squared distance is the infimum over all functions of x_{-i}.

Summing over i gives the formula for D_ Averaging instead gives


< f,(Id-P)f>_mu
=frac1nsum_i|f-P_if|_{L^2(mu)}^2
=frac1nD_


Since each P_i is an orthogonal projection, 0le< f,P_if>_mule< f,f>_mu. Averaging over i gives the same ineuality for P, so the spectrum of P lies in [0,1].

For a linear function, everything except a_ix_i is fixed once x_{-i} is fixed. Under mu(·mid x_{-i}), the spin x_i has mean tanh g_i and variance


1-tanh^2 g_i=sech^2 g_i .


Thus the contribution of site i to D_ is


a_i^2mu[Var(a_ix_imid x_{-i})]
=a_i^2mu[sech^2 g_i].


Summing proves the formula. QED

### Lemma 5.2 (Stationary increments)

Fix W and a function f:{-1,+1}^ntoR. Let (X_t)_{tge0} be the continuous-time chain and (Y_k)_{kge0} the discrete-time chain, each started from mu. Then


E_[(f(X_t)-f(X_0))^2]
le2t,D_,
 tge0,


and


E_[(f(Y_k)-f(Y_0))^2]
le(2k)/(n)D_,

In particular, for g(x)=v^Tx with |v|=1, the right-hand sides are at most 2t and 2k/n.

#### Proof

Stationarity gives


E_[(f(X_t)-f(X_0))^2]
=2< f,(Id-e^{tL}>_mu .


The operator -L is self-adjoint and nonnegative. Diagonalize it in an orthonormal eigenbasis with eigenvalues lambdage0. The multiplier of the f-component in eigenvalue lambda is


1-e^{-tlambda}le tlambda .


Therefore


2< f,(Id-e^{tL}>_mu
le2t< f,-Lf>_calLf>_mu
=2tD_


For the discrete chain, P is self-adjoint with spectrum in [0,1]. The stationary increment is


2< f,(Id-P^k)f>_mu .


For 0lethetale1,


1-theta^kle k(1-theta).


Also


< f,(Id-P)f>_mu=frac1nD_


This proves the discrete bound. Finally, Lemma 5.1 gives


D_
=sum_i v_i^2mu[sech^2 g_i]
lesum_i v_i^2=1.


QED

### Lemma 5.3 (Overlap controls covariance)

Zero field gives mu(x)=mu(-x), so every spin has mean zero. Hence


Σ=Cov_mu(x)=mu[xx^T],

Σ_{ii}=1.


Let


q(x,y)=(x^Ty)/(n)


be the overlap of two configurations. For independent x,ysimmu,


Tr(Σ^2)
=n^2E_{mu ⊗ mu}[q(x,y)^2].


#### Proof

Expand the square:


Tr(Σ^2)
=sum_{i,j}Σ_{ij}^2
=sum_{i,j}mu[x_ix_j]^2 .


For independent x,ysimmu,


E_{mu ⊗ mu}[q(x,y)^2]
=frac1{n^2}sum_{i,j}mu[x_ix_j]^2 .


Multiplying by n^2 proves the identity. QED

## 6. Spectral input near the critical edge

This section proves the matrix estimates that feed the spherical model. The reader should keep one picture in mind: GOE eigenvalues near the edge 2 are spaced like n^{-2/3}. We will look a tiny distance


s_0=L_n n^{-2/3}


above the edge, where L_nto∞ very slowly. The slowly growing factor L_n is essential: it gives enough room to prove estimates without destroying the sharp n^{2/3} scale.

### 6.1 The scales

Let L_nto∞ be deterministic with


L_nlelog n


for all sufficiently large n. Define


s_*=n^{-2/3},

s_0=L_n s_*=L_n n^{-2/3},



h_n=L_n^{1/8},

A_n=h_n+√(log(2+L_n)),

eta_n=A_n L_n^{-3/4}.


Then eta_nto0. Define the deterministic reference function


R(s)=frac{2+s-√(s(4+s))}2,
 s>0.


Note that R(s)=1-sqrt s+O(s) as s↓0.

### Proposition 6.1 (An eigenvalue-only good event)

Let W be the GOE matrix of Section 1, with eigenvalues


lambda_1gelambda_2ge...gelambda_n .


There are events G_n(L_n), measurable with respect to the eigenvalues alone, such that


P( G_n(L_n)^c)G_n(L_n)^c)le Cexp(-cL_n^{1/4})


for all sufficiently large n. On G_n(L_n), for every fixed M<∞,


lambda_j=2+o(s_0)(1le jle3),

max_i|lambda_i|le3,


and, for s_0le sle M,


frac1nsum_{i=1}^nfrac1{2+s-lambda_i}
=R(s)+o(sqrt s),



frac1nsum_{i=1}^nfrac1{(2+s-lambda_i)^2}
le(C_M)/(sqrt s).


All denominators are positive. The little-oh errors are deterministic on the event: the first three eigenvalue errors divided by s_0 are at most


C(L_n^{-1}+eta_n),


and the first resolvent error divided by sqrt s is at most


C_M(L_n^{-3/2}+eta_n).


The event does not depend on M.

The rest of Section 6 proves this proposition.

### 6.2 Tridiagonal reduction

The first step is classical: a GOE matrix has the same eigenvalues as a random tridiagonal matrix. Tridiagonal matrices are much easier because their entries are independent and because their resolvents satisfy a three-term recurrence.

#### Lemma 6.2 (Tridiagonal representation)

The eigenvalues of W have the same law as the eigenvalues of a symmetric tridiagonal matrix T with independent entries


T_{ii}sim N(0,2/n),

T_{i,i+1}=T_{i+1,i}simfrac{chi_{n-i}}{sqrt n}
(1le i<n),


where chi_m is the Euclidean norm of m independent standard normal variables.

#### Proof

Expose one column of W at a time.

The first diagonal entry, the off-diagonal entries in the first column, and the trailing (n-1)times(n-1) block are independent. The first column has n-1 independent N(0,1/n) off-diagonal entries. Choose an orthogonal transformation of the trailing n-1 coordinates that sends this column vector to its nonnegative length times the first coordinate vector. Conjugating by this transformation preserves eigenvalues and produces the desired first off-diagonal entry, whose law is chi_{n-1}/sqrt n.

The trailing block has density proportional to exp(-n,Tr(B^2)/4). This density and Euclidean volume are invariant under orthogonal conjugation. Conditional on the column vector, the chosen transformation is fixed, so the conjugated trailing block is still GOE and is independent of the entries already exposed.

Repeat the construction inside the trailing block, then inside each successive block. At step i, the fresh column has n-i independent N(0,1/n) entries. This gives the stated distributions and their joint independence. QED

### 6.3 Concentration of chi entries

We need two estimates for centered chi variables. For mge1, let chi_m be as above. Then for every real t,


E-E/(sqrt n))
leexp(Ct^2/n),


and


0lesqrt m-E/(sqrt m).


#### Proof

The Gaussian-square moment-generating function and Chernoff's ineuality give


P{chi_m^2ge ma}le(ae^{1-a})^{m/2}
(a>1),


and the same right-hand side for the lower-tail event when 0<a<1. Since


a-1-log age(sqrt a-1)^2,


we obtain


P{|chi_m-sqrt m|ge r}le2e^{-r^2/2}
(rge0).


This tail bound gives Var(chi_m)le C. By Jensen's ineuality,


sqrt m-E
=(Var(chi_m))/(sqrt m+E
le(C)/(sqrt m).


For the exponential bound, let X,X' be independent copies of chi_m and put Z=X-X'. Then Z is symmetric and


P{|Z|ge r}le4e^{-r^2/8},

E Z^{2k}le4·8^k k!
(kge1).


Jensen's ineuality conditional on X, followed by the power series of cosh, gives


E e^{t(X-E X)}
leE e^{tZ}
=Ecosh(tZ)
le1+4sum_{kge1}frac{8^k k!t^{2k}}{(2k)!}
le e^{32t^2}.


Replacing t by t/sqrt n proves the exponential bound. The centered diagonal entries satisfy the same estimate directly. QED

### 6.4 The noise is controlled by a deterministic energy

We now compare the random tridiagonal matrix T with a deterministic tridiagonal matrix D whose off-diagonal entries are the means of the random ones.

Let D be the deterministic symmetric tridiagonal matrix with zero diagonal and


D_{i,i+1}=D_{i+1,i}=c_i:=√(1-i/n)
(1le i<n).


Set


E=2I-D.


Then E is tridiagonal, has diagonal entries 2, and has off-diagonal entries -c_i. It is positive definite, and 0le Ele4I.

The random matrix is T from Lemma 6.2. Put


H=T-D.


The entries of H are centered: the diagonal entries are centered Gaussian, and the off-diagonal entries are


frac{chi_{n-i}}{sqrt n}-c_i .


#### Lemma 6.4 (Noise controlled by deterministic energy)

There is a universal constant C_0 such that, with


Phi_n(x)
=C_0n^{-1/2}A_n(x+s_0)^{1/4}
√(1+log(1+x/s_0)),
 xge0,


the following holds. On an event F_n with


P( F_n^c)F_n^c)le Cexp(-cL_n^{1/4}),


we have, simultaneously for every unit vector uinR^n,


|u^THu|lePhi_n(u^TEu).


Moreover,


sup_{xge0}|Phi_n'(x)|le Ceta_n,

Phi_n(0)le Ceta_ns_0.


#### Proof

The proof has three steps.

1. The deterministic energy u^TEu controls where a unit vector can concentrate and how fast it can oscillate.
2. One high-probability event controls block prefixes of the random entries at every relevant scale.
3. Summation by parts turns those prefix bounds into a quadratic-form bound.

##### Step 1: energy controls mass and gradients

For a unit vector u, write


e=u^TEu.


Expanding the quadratic form gives


e=sum_{i<n}c_i(u_i-u_{i+1})^2
+sum_{i=1}^n(2-c_i-c_{i-1})u_i^2,


where we set c_0=c_n=0.

The row sums of D are at most 2, so 0le Ele4I. The coefficients 2-c_i-c_{i-1} are at least i/(2n): for interior indices this follows from 1-√(1-t)ge t/2, and the boundary indices satisfy the same ineuality directly. Also c_ige1/sqrt2 when ile n/2.

Therefore


sum_i(i)/(n)u_i^2le2e,


and


sum_{i>n/2}u_i^2+sum_{i<n}(u_i-u_{i+1})^2le Ce.


For the second ineuality, control gradients in the first half by the weighted gradient term, and use


(u_i-u_{i+1})^2le2u_i^2+2u_{i+1}^2


for the remaining edges.

##### Step 2: one event for every energy scale

The vector u will be chosen after the matrix is realized, so the probability estimate must be simultaneous over all energy scales and all spatial shells.

Set


sigma_j=2^js_*,

0le jle J_n:=leftlceillog_2(4/s_*)rightrceil,


and


d_j=max{1,floor(sigma_j^{-1/2})}.


For each of the two independent entry sequences (diagonal entries and centered off-diagonal entries), partition its index set into consecutive blocks of lengths between d_j and 2d_j. Such a partition is obtained by merging a last short block with its predecessor.

Assign label r=0 when the first index of a block is at most 2nsigma_j. Otherwise assign label rge1 when the first index lies in


(2^rnsigma_j,2^{r+1}nsigma_j].


If xi_i is either centered entry sequence and a block has length mle2d_j, its partial sums


S_k=xi_1+...+xi_k


obey


P{max_{kle m}|S_k|ge a}
le2exp(-(na^2)/(Cd_j)).


Indeed, the exponential bound of Lemma 6.3 makes


exp(tS_k-(Ckt^2)/(n))


a nonnegative supermartingale. Stopping it at its first crossing of a bounds the one-sided probability by


exp(-ta+(Cmt^2)/(n)).


Optimizing t>0 and repeating for -S_k proves the displayed estimate.

At a fixed pair (j,r), the number of blocks is at most


C(1+(2^rnsigma_j)/(d_j))
le C2^rnsigma_j^{3/2}
=C2^{r+3j/2}.


For sigma_jle1, use d_jgesigma_j^{-1/2}/2. For sigma_j>1, use d_j=1. The additive constant is absorbed because nsigma_j^{3/2}ge1.

Consequently, for a sufficiently large fixed constant K, there is an event F_n on which every block of both sequences has all its centered partial sums bounded by


K√(d_j/n),(h_n+√(j+r)).


Moreover,


P( F_n^c)F_n^c)
le Csum_{j,rge0}2^{r+3j/2}
e^{-cK^2(h_n+√(j+r))^2}
le Ce^{-c'h_n^2}
=Ce^{-c'L_n^{1/4}}.


Here K is chosen so that the factors in j and r form summable geometric series. The bound does not acquire a factor from the number of scales.

##### Step 3: from block prefixes to quadratic forms

We now fix the noise event F_n and choose an arbitrary unit vector u.

Select the first j for which


elesigma_j.


Since ele4, this index exists, and


sigma_jle2(e+s_*).


For the weights w_i=u_i^2 or w_i=u_iu_{i+1}, the sum of their absolute values over all blocks with label r is at most C2^{-r}.

For rge1, every coordinate used is beyond 2^rnsigma_j. The energy-tail bound gives total squared mass at most


(2e)/(2^rsigma_j)le2^{1-r}.


The off-diagonal weights obey the same bound because


|u_iu_{i+1}|lefrac{u_i^2+u_{i+1}^2}{2}.


For r=0, use the unit norm.

The summed variation of these weights within those blocks is at most


C2^{-r/2}sqrt e.


To verify this, use


u_{i+1}^2-u_i^2=(u_{i+1}-u_i)(u_{i+1}+u_i)


for diagonal weights and


u_{i+1}u_{i+2}-u_iu_{i+1}
=u_{i+1}[(u_{i+2}-u_{i+1})+(u_{i+1}-u_i)]


for off-diagonal weights. Cauchy--Schwarz combines the tail mass bound with the total gradient bound. Each coordinate or edge is counted at most a fixed number of times.

For clarity, the summation-by-parts step can be made blockwise. Suppose all centered entry prefixes in a block are bounded by B, and let barxi be the entry average on that block. Then


|barxi|le B/d_j.


The prefixes of xi_i-barxi are at most 2B and vanish at the block end. Therefore


|sum_{i in block}xi_iw_i|
le(B)/(d_j)sum_{i in block}|w_i|
+2Bsum_{substack{i,i+1 in block}}|w_{i+1}-w_i|.


Using the prefix bound and summing by labels proves that either centered weighted sum is bounded by


Cn^{-1/2}sum_{rge0}(h_n+√(j+r))
(d_j^{-1/2}2^{-r}+d_j^{1/2}2^{-r/2}sqrt e)
le C(h_n+sqrt j)n^{-1/2}sigma_j^{1/4}.


For sigma_jle1, the last step uses


d_jasympsigma_j^{-1/2},

elesigma_j.


For 1<sigma_j<8, it follows from d_j=1 with a changed universal constant. The series in r converges geometrically, including its sqrt r factor.

The centered entries have now been controlled uniformly in u. The only remaining part of T-D is the deterministic error in the off-diagonal means. By Lemma 6.3, their difference from c_i is at most


C/sqrt n,√(n-i).


It is at most C/n for ile n/2 and at most C/sqrt n thereafter. Thus their quadratic-form contribution is at most


C/n+Ce/sqrt n,


using again the tail mass bound.

Finally,


jle Clog(2+e/s_*)
le C[log(2+L_n)+log(1+e/s_0)].


Together with sigma_jle2(e+s_*) and s_*le s_0, this converts the centered estimate into

The mean errors are absorbed into the same bound because 0le ele4 and L_nge1 for large n. Both entry sequences, and the factor two from off-diagonal entries, are absorbed by choosing C_0 once and for all.

##### Step 4: smallness of the comparison function

Finally, put


q(x)=1+log(1+x/s_0).


Direct differentiation gives

=C_0n^{-1/2}A_n(x+s_0)^{-3/4}
(frac{√(q(x))}4+frac1{2√(q(x))}).


Since y^{-3/4}√(1+log y) is bounded for yge1, its supremum is at most

### 6.5 The deterministic resolvent

Lemma 6.4 reduces the random trace problem to the deterministic matrix D. Its entries were chosen so that its characteristic polynomials satisfy a Hermite recurrence. That recurrence lets us compute its resolvent very accurately near the edge.

#### Lemma 6.5 (Hermite resolvent estimates)

Let


e_1le e_2le...le e_n


be the eigenvalues of


E=2I-D.


Set


R_D(s)=frac1nTr(s+E)^{-1},

T_D(s)=frac1nTr(s+E)^{-2}
=-R_D'(s).


For every fixed M<∞ and all sufficiently large n, uniformly for


s_0le sle M,


we have


T_D(s)le C_Ms^{-1/2},

0le R(s)-R_D(s)le(C_M)/(ns).


Moreover,


N(t):=#{i:e_ile t}le Cnt^{3/2}
(tge s_0).


#### Proof

Reverse the coordinates of D. Its off-diagonal entries become


√(k/n),

1le k<n.


Let P_k(z) be the characteristic polynomial of the ktimes k principal minor obtained from the first k reversed coordinates. Then


P_0(z)=1,

P_1(z)=z,

P_{k+1}(z)=zP_k(z)-(k)/(n)P_{k-1}(z).


Induction gives


P_k'=kP_{k-1}.


Differentiating the recurrence and using the recurrence one step earlier shows that


P_{k+1}'=P_k+kP_k.


Consequently,


P_n''-nzP_n'+n^2P_n=0.


Since P_n is the characteristic polynomial of D,


R_D(s)=(P_n'(2+s))/(nP_n(2+s)).


The differential equation therefore yields


R_D(s)^2-(2+s)R_D(s)+1=(T_D(s))/(n)>0.


For every s>0, R_D(s) is finite because Ege0.

The two roots of the quadratic on the left are


R(s)=frac{2+s-√(s(4+s))}2


and


R_+(s)=frac{2+s+√(s(4+s))}2.


At large s, R_D(s)to0 whereas R_+(s)to∞. Positivity in the Riccati equation thus places R_D below the smaller root there, and continuity prevents it from crossing either root anywhere on (0,∞).

If


delta(s)=R(s)-R_D(s),


then factoring the quadratic gives


0ledelta(s)le
T_D(s)/n√(s(4+s)).


Termwise comparison of resolvents shows that


T_D(s)lefrac2s[R_D(s/2)-R_D(s)]
lefrac2s[R(s/2)-R(s)+delta(s)].


Differentiating the explicit expression for R gives


0<-R'(s)
=2/√(s(4+s)),
(2+s+√(s(4+s)))
lefrac1{2sqrt s}.


Integrating from s/2 to s therefore gives


R(s/2)-R(s)lesqrt s-√(s/2)lesqrt s.


Hence


T_D(s)le(C)/(sqrt s)
+2T_D(s)/ns√(s(4+s)).


For sge s_0, the coefficient of the last term is at most


(ns_0^{3/2})^{-1}=L_n^{-3/2}to0.


Absorbing it proves the bound on T_D. The displayed bound on delta then follows from the root-difference ineuality. Notice that R_D(s/2)le R(s/2) was established for every positive s/2; it does not require s/2ge s_0.

The second-trace estimate also gives the counting estimate needed for the later dyadic transfer.

## 7. The spherical model

The Ising cube is hard because its coordinates are discrete. The spherical model replaces the cube by a sphere of radius sqrt n, where Gaussian conditioning gives exact formulas. We then transfer spherical estimates back to the cube in Section 8.

### 7.1 Tilting the sphere

Fix a deterministic ordered spectrum

### 7.3 Density at the mean

The next estimate is needed at the mean of a weighted sum of squares, including arrays with a dominant weight.

#### Lemma 7.2 (Density at the mean)

There is a universal constant c>0 such that, for every nge3 and every k_1,...,k_n>0 with


sum_{i=1}^n k_i=n,


the density p of


sum_{i=1}^n k_iG_i^2,


for independent standard Gaussians G_i, satisfies


p(n)ge c(sum_{i=1}^n k_i^2)^{-1/2}.


#### Proof

After normalization, it is enough to exclude a sequence of densities vanishing at zero. There are two ways such a sequence of weights can behave: all weights vanish individually, giving a Gaussian limit, or a positive largest weight remains and supplies a density bounded below near zero.

Set


tau=(sum_i k_i^2)^{1/2},

d_i=k_i/tau .


The centered random variable


T_d=sum_i d_i(G_i^2-1)
=(sum_i k_iG_i^2-n)/(tau)


has density f_d with


f_d(0)=tau p(n),

sum_i d_i^2=1.


Thus it suffices to bound f_d(0) below uniformly over all such finite arrays. Each array has at least three positive weights. Its characteristic function is integrable, since its absolute value decays as O(|t|^{-n/2}) at infinity; we always take the resulting continuous version of its density.

Suppose, to the contrary, that arrays d^{(r)} satisfy


f_{d^{(r)}}(0)to0.


Sort each array in nonincreasing order and extend it by zeros. A diagonal subsequence has


d_i^{(r)}to c_i


for every i, with


sum_i c_i^2le1.


We consider the two possible values of c_1.

##### Case 1: c_1=0

For dge0, define


psi_d(t)=E e^{itd(G^2-1)}
=e^{-itd}(1-2itd)^{-1/2}.


For logarithms of characteristic functions, use the continuous branch equal to zero at t=0. For fixed t and |td| small, expansion gives


logpsi_d(t)=-t^2d^2+O(|t|^3d^3).


It follows that the characteristic function

converges pointwise to e^{-t^2}, because


sum_i(d_i^{(r)})^3
le d_1^{(r)}sum_i(d_i^{(r)})^2
=d_1^{(r)}.


Weak convergence alone does not control a density at one point. To pass through Fourier inversion we need an integrable bound uniform in r. Concavity of log(1+x) and


sum_i(d_i^{(r)})^2=1


give


|phi_r(t)|
=prod_i(1+4(d_i^{(r)})^2t^2)^{-1/4}
le
(1+4(d_1^{(r)})^2t^2)^{-1/(4(d_1^{(r)})^2)}.


The function x|->log(1+Ax)/x is decreasing for Age0. Once (d_1^{(r)})^2le1/8, the last expression is at most


(1+t^2/2)^{-2}.


Fourier inversion and dominated convergence imply


f_{d^{(r)}}(0)
=frac1{2pi}int_{R},dt
→
frac1{2pi}int_{R}{-t^2},dt
=frac1{2sqrtpi},


a contradiction.

##### Case 2: c_1>0

Remove the largest coordinate and write


R_r=sum_{ige2}d_i^{(r)}(G_i^2-1).


We claim that R_r converges in distribution to


R=sum_{ige2}c_i(G_i^2-1)+Z,

Zsim N(0,,2(1-sum_{ige1}c_i^2)),


where Z and the displayed Gaussians are independent.

The series converges in L^2, since the tail beyond m has variance


2sum_{i>m}c_i^2to0.


To identify the limit, note that the sorted weights satisfy


d_{m+1}^{(r)}le(m+1)^{-1/2}.


For fixed t and sufficiently large m, the same logarithmic expansion as above gives, uniformly in r,


logE e^{itR_r}
=
sum_{i=2}^mlogpsi_{d_i^{(r)}}(t)
-t^2(1-sum_{i=1}^m(d_i^{(r)})^2)
+O(|t|^3/√(m+1)).


First let rto∞ and then mto∞. The resulting characteristic function is exactly that of R, which proves the claim.

The residual limit has positive mass arbitrarily close to zero. This is the fact that allows the surviving largest coordinate to control the convolution density.

For any delta>0, choose a finite truncation of its series whose remaining tail variance is less than delta^2/16. Each nonzero summand in the finite part has a density positive near zero, so the finite part together with Z has positive probability in (-delta/2,delta/2). Zero summands or a degenerate Z cause no difficulty. The independent tail has positive probability in the same interval by Chebyshev's ineuality. Intersecting these events proves the assertion. This argument uses square summability of (c_i), not summability.

In particular, by convergence in distribution and the open-set ineuality,


liminf_{rto∞}P{|R_r|<c_1/3}
geP{|R|<c_1/3}>0.

1_bf1_{{x>-d}}.

### 8.1 Haar averaging and the two overlap laws

Let O be Haar distributed on the orthogonal group, independent of the ordered spectrum, and put


W=OΛ O^T.


Orthogonal invariance of GOE justifies this representation: Haar averaging a test function of a symmetric matrix depends only on its eigenvalues, and gives the invariant probability measure on their conjugacy orbit.

We write E_O and P_O for expectation and probability over O with Λ fixed.

Define the normalized Ising partition sum


I=2^{-n}sum_{xin{-1,+1}^n}
exp((x^TOΛ O^Tx)/(2)).


For every fixed cube point x, the vector O^Tx is uniform on the sphere of radius sqrt n. Averaging each summand therefore gives the exact first-moment identity

To control the normalization of the Gibbs law, we will compare the second moment of I with S(1)^2. Both quantities can be expressed using the same pair integral; only their overlap laws differ.

Let


Q_n={-1+2j/n:0le jle n},


and


p_I(q)=2^{-n}binom{n}{n(1+q)/2}
(qinQ_n).


This is the overlap law of two independent uniform cube points.

Let (U,V) be a uniformly oriented orthogonal pair, each vector having norm sqrt n, and define, for -1le qle1,


M(q)=E
((1+q)U^TΛ U+(1-q)V^TΛ V)/(2)
).


If cube points x,y have overlap qin(-1,1), their normalized sum and difference


x+y/√(2(1+q)),

### 8.2 The cubic deficit

The next lemma supplies the margin needed in the pair bound for |q|ge q_0.

#### Lemma 8.1 (Cubic deficit)

There is a universal constant c>0 such that, for all sufficiently large n, uniformly for spectra in G_n and q_0le qle1,


B(1+q)+B(1-q)-2B(1)
le(q^2)/(2)-cq^3.


One may take c=1/24.

#### Proof

We estimate the derivative of the spherical variational value on both sides of the critical parameter a=1. Close to that parameter the resolvent estimates give a quantitative margin; away from it a bounded-convergence argument suffices.

The saddle equation in Lemma 7.1 and implicit differentiation show that z_a is continuously differentiable in a. Differentiating the objective at its minimizer gives

(lambda_i)/(z_a-alambda_i),

(a>0).


The first identity also holds at a=0, with a one-sided derivative at the endpoint. Since the positive weights (z_a-alambda_i)^{-1} sum to n,

(0le ale2).


For a>0, put r_a=z_a/a and

The saddle equation is equivalently

### 8.3 Concentration of the partition sum

#### Proposition 8.2 (Conditional partition concentration)

For spectra in G_n,

in probability.

#### Proof

The first-moment identity was established above. We now bound the exact second moment by treating the two overlap regions separately.

##### Large overlaps

p_I(q)(M(q))/(S(1)^2)
le Cn^2e^{-cn|q|^3}
(qinQ_n, |q|ge q_0).

In particular, tau_n is smaller than every fixed negative power of n.

##### Central overlaps

The remaining obstacle is that M(q) may vary too rapidly for a derivative-based Riemann-sum estimate. We compare the unweighted overlap laws locally, then move each lattice mass outward, where M is larger.

Let

with a uniform relative error, since nq_0^4to0.

Assign to each retained lattice point the outward bin

For even n, the lattice near zero is

The centered zero bin leaves gaps between Delta/2 and Delta, and between -Delta and -Delta/2.

## 9. Dynamics: from static estimates to slow mixing

We now use the static estimates to prove slow mixing. The core idea is the sign test from the roadmap.

### 9.3 The subexponential upper bound and Theorem C

We now prove the upper bounds in Theorem C. The strategy is to compare the critical Gibbs measure with one fixed subcritical high-temperature model.

#### Theorem 9.2 (High-temperature spectral gap)

Fix 0<beta<1. Let J be a symmetric matrix with zero diagonal and independent entries


J_{ij}sim N(0,beta^2/n)
(i<j),


and let


nu_J(x)=Z_J^{-1}exp(frac12x^TJx),
 xin{-1,+1}^n.


There is a finite constant C_beta, depending only on beta, such that


P_J{
Var_{nu_J}(f)le C_betaD_{nu_J}(f)
 for every f:{-1,+1}^ntoR
}→1.


Here D_{nu_J} is the unscaled sum of conditional variances. Consequently, the discrete-time heat-bath chain choosing one uniform site has spectral gap at least 1/(nC_beta) with probability tending to one.

This theorem is supplied by the separate high-temperature companion paper. The constant C_beta is required only for each fixed beta<1: we select beta after the desired exponential tolerance and before taking the limit in n.

#### Lemma 9.3 (Comparison of conditional-variance forms)

Let rho,nu be probability measures with full support on {-1,+1}^n. Suppose

#### Proof

The comparison works directly with least-squares characterizations; one need not compare the conditional kernels pointwise.

For any full-support probability measure eta,


Var_eta(f)=inf_{cinR}[(f-c)^2],


and


D_

where every h_i ranges independently over all functions of the other coordinates.

For the second identity, conditional expectation gives the orthogonal decomposition

The first identity follows by the same decomposition with constants.

For the exponential densities, rho(x)/nu(x) is a constant times e^{(1-beta)H(x)}; the common normalization cancels in the ratio of its maximum and minimum. QED

## 10. Appendix: why the equilibrium model alone has no mixing time

The main theorems specify both the update rule and the clock. Two elementary constructions show why these are mathematical assumptions, even if the equilibrium law and reversibility are already fixed.

A third calculation distinguishes high-probability asymptotic bounds from essential bounds over every disorder realization.

### Proposition 10.1 (Arbitrary refresh times)

Fix any realization of the SK measure on Ω_n={-1,+1}^n, nge2. For every positive integer r, there is an irreducible, aperiodic, reversible Markov kernel with stationary law mu whose worst-state threshold-1/4 mixing time equals r.

#### Proof

At each step, refresh the whole configuration from mu with probability p and otherwise leave it fixed. In operator notation,


Pi(x,y)=mu(y),

K_p=(1-p)Id+pPi,

0<ple1.


All entries of K_p are positive, and


mu(x)K_p(x,y)=mu(y)K_p(y,x).


The identity Pi^2=Pi gives, for 0<p<1,


K_p^k=Pi+(1-p)^k(Id-Pi),


and


max_x|K_p^k(x,·)-mu|_{TV}
=(1-p)^k(1-mu_{min}).


Here


mu_{min}le2^{-n}le1/4.


Choosing


1-p=[frac{1}{4(1-mu_{min})}]^{1/r}


gives distance exactly 1/4 at r and strictly larger at every earlier integer. The case p=1 also has mixing time one. QED

The same freedom remains if transitions are required to change at most one spin. Given the uniform-site heat-bath kernel P, the kernel


(1-p)Id+pP


has the same stationary law, detailed balance, irreducibility, and transition restriction. Starting from a minimum-mass state, its distance after k steps is at least


(1-p)^k-mu_{min},


by the event that no update was attempted. Its mixing time consequently diverges as p↓0. Allowing p to depend on n changes possible growth classes as well.

We next fix the dynamics completely. The remaining distinction is between typical disorder as n grows and all disorder at a fixed dimension. Unbounded Gaussian couplings already separate those notions for two spins.

### Proposition 10.2 (A two-spin example)

For n=2 and the discrete uniform-site heat-bath kernel, the mixing time has no finite deterministic essential upper bound over the Gaussian coupling, although it is finite for every realization.

#### Proof

A large positive coupling keeps the two aligned states persistent.

This can be quantified using the linear observable


g(x)=x_1+x_2.


Put w=W_{12}. Spin-flip symmetry gives mu[g]=0, and conditional expectation computes the eigenvalue exactly:


Pg=theta g,

theta=(1+tanh w)/(2).


Starting at (1,1), the expectation of g after k steps is 2theta^k. Since |g|_∞=2, the dual total-variation bound yields


|P^k((1,1),·)-mu|_{TV}
ge1/2theta^k.


For each fixed k, this exceeds 1/4 when w is sufficiently large and positive. Every such Gaussian tail has positive probability.

The chain for any finite w remains finite, irreducible, and aperiodic, so its own mixing time is finite. QED

Proposition 10.2 concerns essential bounds over all disorder realizations at fixed n. It does not contradict the typical-disorder asymptotic statements in Theorem C.
