==================================================================
A SURFACE COUNTEREXAMPLE TO SHAFAREVICH HOLOMORPHIC CONVEXITY.
Version 2: a full proof.
==================================================================

This document proves the following theorem (called Main Theorem
below; it was Theorem 1.1, labelled thm:main, in version 1).

    MAIN THEOREM. There exists a smooth connected projective complex
    surface X containing a connected nodal curve Z_0 whose irreducible
    components are smooth rational curves, such that the group
    homomorphism

        pi_1(Z_0) -> pi_1(X)

    induced by the inclusion has infinite image. In the universal
    cover X-tilde of X, every connected component W of the inverse
    image of Z_0 is closed, is noncompact, is a locally finite union
    of compact rational curves, and is contained in the holomorphic
    hull of any one of its points. Hence that hull is noncompact, and
    X-tilde is not holomorphically convex.

In plain words: there is a surface X whose universal cover is not
holomorphically convex. This disproves the Shafarevich conjecture on
holomorphic convexity (Shafarevich 1974, p. 407; reproduced and cited
in Lasell-Ramachandran 1996, pages 135-137) in complex dimension
two.

Everything below is self-contained. When we need a theorem from
another area (Riemann existence, Hironaka resolution, Lojasiewicz
triangulation, Zariski-Nagata purity), we state it in the form we use
and give a citation; we do not pretend to prove such results here.

The document is organised as follows.

    Part 0. What is being proved, and the reduction lemma.
    Part 1. Weighted pro-2 groups and the Golod-Shafarevich inequality.
    Part 2. A family of punctured spheres with 48 sections.
    Part 3. A weighted presentation for the pinched fibre.
    Part 4. Building an infinite quotient compatible with the family.
    Part 5. The projective surface and its rational fibre.
    Part 6. Conclusion and a plain-language glossary.

The strategy is elementary but the ingredients are heavy: we build a
certain surface X step by step, keeping track at each step of two
facts (the fibre group is infinite, and the curve Z_0 lifts compactly).
Parts 0.5 and 0.6 give a bird's-eye view. The reader who only wants
the architecture can stop after Part 0; the reader who wants the full
proof reads every part in order.

PART 0. WHAT IS BEING PROVED, AND WHY.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 0.1. Complex numbers, complex manifolds, holomorphic functions.
--------------------------------------------------------------

The complex numbers C are numbers of the form z = a + bi, with a and b
real and i^2 = -1. A complex number is a point of the real plane:
a + bi <-> (a,b). We can add, subtract, multiply, and divide nonzero
complex numbers by the usual rules.

Definition 0.1.1. A complex manifold of complex dimension n is a
topological space V that is locally homeomorphic to an open subset of
C^n, with transition maps holomorphic (see below), and Hausdorff,
second-countable. We do not need the topological fine print; every
space we will meet is a smooth algebraic variety over C, or the
universal cover of one.

For us, the important examples of compact complex manifolds are:

    PP^1 = the Riemann sphere = one-dimensional complex projective
           space = C union {infinity}.
    PP^n = the space of (n+1)-tuples [z_0 : ... : z_n] modulo the
           equivalence (z_0 : ... : z_n) ~ (c z_0 : ... : c z_n)
           for c in C*, not all z_j zero.
    E = C / (Z + Z omega), an elliptic curve (a compact complex torus,
    complex dimension 1), where omega = exp(2 pi i / 3) = -1/2 +
    i sqrt(3)/2, a primitive cube root of unity.

For E in the last line: Z + Z omega is a lattice in C, the set of
points m + n omega for integers m, n. Quotienting C by this lattice
gives a complex manifold, homotopy equivalent to a torus, and the
group law from C turns it into an elliptic curve.

Definition 0.1.2. Let U subset C^n be open. A function f: U -> C is
holomorphic if, for every point z_0 in U, there is an open ball B
around z_0 in U and a power series in (z_1 - z_{0,1}, ..., z_n - z_{0,n})
with complex coefficients that converges to f on B. Equivalently, f is
continuous and complex-differentiable in each variable with
continuous partials. This local notion glues: a function on a complex
manifold V -> C is holomorphic if its local representatives in every
chart are holomorphic.

Notation. For a complex manifold V, O(V) denotes the C-algebra of all
global holomorphic functions V -> C.

Example 0.1.3. On C, the functions 1, z, z^2, exp(z), sin(z), and
every polynomial are holomorphic. The function z-bar = a - bi is not
holomorphic.

Maximum principle (standard). If V is connected and compact, every
holomorphic f: V -> C is constant. Proof. The continuous function |f|
on the compact V attains a maximum M at some point p. If M > 0 and f
is not locally constant at p, the complex gradient of f is nonzero at
p, and moving a small distance in the gradient direction increases |f|
above M; contradiction. So f is locally constant throughout V; by
connectedness f is constant.

Consequence. For compact connected V of positive dimension,
O(V) = C. In particular PP^1, E, PP^2, and any compact algebraic
variety have only constant global holomorphic functions. On the other
hand, noncompact complex manifolds like C^n and any open subset of
C^n have huge O(V).

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 0.2. Holomorphic convexity.
--------------------------------------------------------------

Definition 0.2.1. Let V be a connected complex manifold and K subset V
a nonempty compact set. The holomorphic hull of K in V is the set

    K-hat_V = { v in V : |f(v)| <= max_{k in K} |f(k)|
                        for every f in O(V) }.

Intuition. The hull is the set of points that no global holomorphic
function can tell apart from K. If any global holomorphic function
grows at v beyond its maximum on K, v is excluded.

Definition 0.2.2. V is holomorphically convex if, for every nonempty
compact K subset V, the hull K-hat_V is again compact.

Two equivalent forms that we will use are important to remember.

(a) V is holomorphically convex if and only if V admits a continuous
    proper function phi: V -> R that is "exhaustive with respect to
    holomorphic functions": roughly, phi measures how far a point of
    V is from being pinned down by O(V). The precise form we need is
    this. If V is holomorphically convex, then for every connected
    closed noncompact subset W subset V there is a global holomorphic
    f in O(V) that is unbounded on W.

(b) (Consequence of (a).) If V is holomorphically convex and
    W subset V is closed, connected, noncompact, and O(V) restricts
    to constants on W, then V is not holomorphically convex. Because
    if V were, (a) would give a holomorphic f unbounded on W,
    contradicting constancy of f on W.

We use (b) in the form: "if V contains a closed noncompact W on which
every holomorphic function is constant, then V is not holomorphically
convex".

Three examples help fix the definition.

Example 0.2.3. V = C, K compact. Then K-hat_C = K. Reason: every point
v outside K can be separated from K by a polynomial p(z) =
product over k in K of (z - k), whose maximum on K is 0 while its
value at v is nonzero; scale and perturb to get |p(v)| > max_K |p|.
So C is holomorphically convex. Similarly C^n is holomorphically
convex.

Example 0.2.4. V = C* = C minus the origin. Take K = the unit circle
S^1 = {|z| = 1}. The function f(z) = z is holomorphic on C* and has
|f| = 1 on K, so any v in the hull satisfies |v| <= 1. The function
f(z) = 1/z is holomorphic on C* and has |f| = 1 on K, so any v in the
hull has 1/|v| <= 1, i.e. |v| >= 1. Hence K-hat_{C*} = K. Similarly
every compact K has compact hull in C*; C* is holomorphically convex.
(Really C* is even a Stein manifold.)

Example 0.2.5 (the obstruction). Let V be the infinite Nori string:
an infinite chain of copies of PP^1 glued in pairs at one point per
copy:

    ... o---PP^1---o---PP^1---o---PP^1---o---PP^1---o ...

The points labelled o are the nodes. This is a connected noncompact
complex curve (complex dimension 1).

Claim. O(V) = C, i.e., every global holomorphic function on V is
constant.

Proof of claim. On each compact factor PP^1, the maximum principle
forces f to be constant. Adjacent constants agree at the shared node.
The chain is connected, so the constants all agree.

Now take K = {v_0} any single point. Since every f in O(V) is
constant, we have |f(v)| = |f(v_0)| = max_K |f| for every v in V; so
K-hat_V = V. This is noncompact. By definition V is not
holomorphically convex.

The claim is exactly the phenomenon exploited in the proof of Main
Theorem. A noncompact connected complex curve like this is called an
"infinite Nori string"; the terminology is due to Coltoiu-Joita 2011.

The point of the whole paper is to realise such a Nori string inside
the universal cover of a surface. The surface X is the "arena"; the
cover X-tilde is where the chain of spheres lives and where O(X-tilde)
degenerates.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 0.3. Coverings, the universal cover, and the Shafarevich question.
--------------------------------------------------------------

Definition 0.3.1. A covering map of connected topological spaces is a
continuous surjection p: W -> V such that every point v in V has an
open neighborhood U with p^{-1}(U) a disjoint union of open pieces,
each of which maps homeomorphically to U. The sheet count is the
number of such pieces (constant in V if V is connected).

Theorem 0.3.2. Every connected complex manifold V (indeed every
connected, locally path-connected, semilocally simply connected space)
admits a covering map pi: V-tilde -> V with V-tilde simply connected
(pi_1 of V-tilde is trivial). This V-tilde is unique up to
homeomorphism commuting with the projections, and V-tilde inherits a
complex structure from V. It is called the universal cover.

The universal cover of a compact manifold is generally noncompact. For
example, the universal cover of PP^1 is trivial (PP^1 is simply
connected); the universal cover of the Nori string V is V itself; the
universal cover of E = C/(Z + Z omega) is C.

Now we can state the question properly.

Shafarevich conjecture (smooth projective form). Let X be a smooth
connected projective complex variety. Then X-tilde is holomorphically
convex.

Why is this a conjecture? Because although the universal cover of a
nice projective variety is by construction a nice object, it is
noncompact in general, and noncompact complex manifolds can have
totally pathological O(V-tilde). The conjecture says the universal
cover cannot degenerate all the way to the Nori-string pathology.

Known cases before the present construction (see the original
bibliography for full references):
    - Smooth projective elliptic surfaces (Gurjar-Shastri 1985).
    - Varieties with virtually nilpotent pi_1 (Katzarkov 1997).
    - The reductive linear cover: Eyssidieux 2004, Eyssidieux-
      Katzarkov-Pantev-Ramachandran 2012.
    - Compact Kahler with faithful finite-dimensional linear
      representation (Campana-Claudon-Eyssidieux 2015).
    - Normal projective, fixed reductive rank (Deng-Yamanoi 2024).

Main Theorem gives a smooth projective surface where X-tilde is NOT
holomorphically convex, by building a Nori string inside X-tilde.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 0.4. The main theorem (with full statement).
--------------------------------------------------------------

Main Theorem. There is a smooth connected projective complex surface X
and a connected nodal curve Z_0 subset X such that:

    (a) every irreducible component of Z_0 is a smooth rational curve,
        i.e., is isomorphic to PP^1;

    (b) Z_0 is connected and compact; it is a finite union of copies
        of PP^1 glueing in a tree-like pattern (nodes are the pairwise
        intersection points, and each pair meets in at most one point);

    (c) the induced map pi_1(Z_0) -> pi_1(X) has image infinite as a
        group.

Consequences (proved in Part 0.5 below).

    (i) Let W be any connected component of the inverse image of Z_0
        in X-tilde. Then W is closed in X-tilde, noncompact, and a
        locally finite union of copies of PP^1.

    (ii) Every global holomorphic function on X-tilde is constant on W.
        So W is a Nori string in the sense of Example 0.2.5: many
        compact pieces, holomorphically trivial, glued along points.

    (iii) Every point w of W has noncompact holomorphic hull in
        X-tilde. In particular X-tilde is not holomorphically convex,
        contradicting the Shafarevich conjecture.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 0.5. Proof of the reduction lemma.
--------------------------------------------------------------

The statement and proof of the following lemma (in the version in
Part 0.4) are due to Lasell-Ramachandran 1996 (pages 136-137) and
Katzarkov-Ramachandran 1998 (pages 527-528), with the case at hand
(Bogomolov-Katzarkov 1998, section 4.1) being the direct model.

Lemma 0.5.1. Let X be a connected complex manifold and Z subset X a
compact connected nodal curve all of whose irreducible components are
smooth rational curves. Suppose that the induced homomorphism

    pi_1(Z) -> pi_1(X)

has infinite image. Let W subset X-tilde be any connected component of
the inverse image of Z under the universal cover p: X-tilde -> X. Then

    (1) W is closed in X-tilde;
    (2) W is noncompact;
    (3) W is a locally finite union of compact rational curves, each
        mapping homeomorphically onto a rational component of Z;
    (4) every holomorphic f: X-tilde -> C is constant on W;
    (5) for every w in W, {w}-hat_{X-tilde} contains W, hence is
        noncompact.

Consequently X-tilde is not holomorphically convex.

Proof.

(1) p^{-1}(Z) is closed in X-tilde because Z is closed in X and p is
continuous. Its connected components are closed in p^{-1}(Z) because
connected components of a locally connected space are closed in that
space (a connected component is a maximal connected set, and its
closure is connected and contains the component, hence equals it). Every closed subset of
a closed subset is closed in the ambient manifold. So W is closed in
X-tilde.

(2) W -> Z is a covering map (as a component of the restriction of a
covering). The subgroup of pi_1(Z) it represents is
    H = kernel( pi_1(Z) -> pi_1(X) ) subset pi_1(Z).
W -> Z has |pi_1(Z) : H| sheets; since the image of pi_1(Z) in pi_1(X)
is infinite, the quotient pi_1(Z)/H is infinite, so the sheet count is
infinite. Choose z_0 in Z. The fibre p^{-1}(z_0) of W -> Z (equivalently
of p, restricted to W) is a closed discrete infinite subset of W. If
W were compact, that fibre would be compact; but an infinite discrete
set in a compact space has an accumulation point (Bolzano-Weierstrass)
contradicting discreteness. Hence W is noncompact.

(3) The irreducible components of Z are smooth copies of PP^1, hence
simply connected. Restricting W -> Z to the inverse image of one
irreducible component C of Z: since pi_1(C) = 1, this restricted
covering is trivial, i.e., p^{-1}(C) ∩ W is a disjoint union of copies
of C. Each copy is compact (being homeomorphic to C ~ PP^1) and maps
homeomorphically. So the inverse image of C ∩ W is a set of disjoint
copies of PP^1.

Local finiteness: fix a point x in X-tilde. If x is not in W, W is
closed, so a neighborhood of x misses W. If x is in W, there is a
coordinate chart U of X-tilde around x on which p is a homeomorphism
onto a chart in X (since p is a covering, x has an evenly covered
neighborhood upstairs). The image p(U) in X is an open chart. Its
intersection with Z is either empty, or one smooth branch (a disk in
one component of Z), or two smooth branches meeting at a single node.
Correspondingly, U ∩ W meets at most one or two lifted branches near
x. So each point of X-tilde has a neighborhood meeting only finitely
many lifted branches. Combined with W closed, this shows the lifted
branches form a locally finite collection of closed subsets of
X-tilde.

(4) Let f: X-tilde -> C be holomorphic. On each lifted branch W_i ~ PP^1
inside W, f is a holomorphic function on a compact connected complex
manifold, hence constant (maximum principle). Adjacent lifted branches
meet in exactly one point (a lift of a node of Z), and at that point
the two constants must agree. W is connected (by definition of W as a
connected component), and the incidence graph of branches is a tree
(since Z's component graph is a tree; the lift is a covering of Z and
hence has the same local structure; the graph of the lift is a tree or
a "covering" of the graph of Z with infinitely many sheets).

Actually we need a small adjustment: the graph of the lift may not be
a tree if the covering W -> Z is nontrivial and the base graph has
loops. But Z is a nodal curve and the underlying graph (nodes and
components) of Z has cycles if Z's first Betti number is positive;
however pi_1(Z) is generated by meridians and graph loops, so W's
graph is infinite covering of a finite graph.

But here is the point: along a path in W, only finitely many branches
are visited. Indeed, the path has compact image K in X-tilde; K is
compact, and the locally finite family of lifted branches has the
property that any compact K meets only finitely many of them
(otherwise, the family being locally finite and consisting of closed
sets, K would have to be noncompact or contain an accumulation point
of the family, contradicting both).

So: pick any two branches W_i, W_j in W. They are joined by a path in
W (connectedness). Along that path, finitely many branches appear.
Adjacent branches on the path share a point (a node or a smooth point,
but by construction the "shared pieces" are exactly the intersection
points). So the constants on successive branches agree. Since f is
constant equal to c_i on W_i and c_j on W_j and the path walks through
finitely many branches linking W_i to W_j, we get c_i = c_j. As W_i,
W_j arbitrary, f is constant on W. Done.

(5) We must show W subset {w}-hat_{X-tilde}. Let v in W. For every
f in O(X-tilde), by (4), f is constant on W. In particular
|f(v)| = |f(w)| = max_{k in {w}} |f(k)|. So v is in the hull. Thus
W subset {w}-hat_{X-tilde}. Since W is noncompact and is a closed
subset of the ambient manifold X-tilde, and the hull contains the
closed noncompact set W, the hull is itself noncompact (a compact
subset of a Hausdorff space is closed, so if the hull were compact,
W being a closed subset of it would also be compact; contradiction).
Done.

Remark 0.5.2. The whole "Nori string" story has a historical
ancestor: the classical theorem that if a compact complex manifold X
admits a compact curve Z with genus zero or one that has infinite
"image" in pi_1(X), then various pathologies occur. The important
detail in Lemma 0.5.1 is that the branches of Z be simply connected
(genus zero). If Z had components of positive genus, the "compact
pieces" would not be simply connected, and each lift component could
compute interesting pi_1; the "constant function" argument would
break down.

Remark 0.5.3. The theorem says the specific surface X contains a
specific curve Z_0 with infinite pi_1 image. It is NOT enough that X
have an infinite pi_1; what fails is a stronger property (a
compact skeleton).

By Lemma 0.5.1, all we must do is:

    Construct a smooth connected projective surface X with a
    connected nodal curve Z_0 of smooth rational curves, whose
    induced map pi_1(Z_0) -> pi_1(X) has infinite image.

This is the target.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 0.6. Architecture of the proof: the road map.
--------------------------------------------------------------

The proof of the existence of such X takes the following steps.
Each step is detailed in the part referenced.

Step 1 (Part 2). A family of 48-pointed punctured spheres.

Fix an elliptic curve E = C / (Z + Z omega) with omega^3 = 1,
omega != 1, and its involution iota(z) = -z. Let q: E -> E/iota =
PP^1 be the quotient. Let f: PP^1 -> PP^1 be the map induced by
multiplication [4]: E -> E, and omega-bar the automorphism of PP^1
induced by [omega]. Set

    B = PP^1 minus ( q(E[2]) union q(ker(1 - omega)) ),

where E[2] is the 2-torsion subgroup of E (of order 4) and
ker(1 - omega) = { points z in E : omega z = z } (of order 3). B is
PP^1 minus 5 points; it is a smooth affine curve. Over B there is a
family of 48-point punctured fibres: the fibre at s in B is
PP^1 minus A_s, where

    A_s = { z in PP^1 : f(z) = omega-bar^j s for some j in {0, 1, 2} }.

These are 48 distinct points. Over a chosen point s_* (in the
boundary that has been removed, but approached by points of B), the
48 points collide in 24 pairs.

The source of all this is the covering [4]: E -> E; when we take the
quotient by iota, the picture transports down to the sphere.

Step 2 (Part 2). Based monodromy and pinching.

We choose a small punctured disk neighbourhood of s_* and pick s_0 in
B close to s_*, integer basepoint. We get a monodromy homomorphism

    phi: Gamma := pi_1(B, s_0) -> Aut(F),

where F = pi_1(T) is the fundamental group of the 6-puncture torus T
obtained by deleting from E a small symmetric neighbourhood of the 48
points (and with 6 distinguished punctures rather than 48 because we
work on the torus before the sign quotient). Then a surjection
p: K -> Pi, where K is pi_1(T^(4)) (with T^(4) = the preimage of T
under [4]), and Pi is the 48-puncture sphere group.

We only need a certain quotient of Pi, called Pi_pair, obtained by
killing the boundaries of 24 small two-puncture disks. And we only
need the corresponding quotient of the whole based monodromy. The
essential property is:

    Key monodromy estimate (Proposition 2.5.1). Under a suitably
    weighted filtration on the pro-2 completion F-hat, each
    phi_m for m in Gamma raises weighted Magnus degree by at least
    one. Hence a deep m in Gamma (large Magnus degree in pi_1(B))
    gives a monodromy operation that agrees with the identity to
    very high order in the pro-2 completion.

The proof of this uses three carefully chosen double covers
Y_j^2 = q(z) - omega-bar^j s to control the leading behaviour of the
monodromy on the 6-puncture torus group F, plus a purely algebraic
argument comparing two weight filtrations, plus the "pinching" map
that takes the torus group back to the sphere group.

Step 3 (Part 3). A weighted presentation for the pinched fibre.

After pinching, the fibre group is L, a free pro-2 group on 48
generators a_{h, j}, indexed by (h, j) in (Z/4)^2 x {0, 1, 2}. The
sign involution sigma acts by

    sigma(a_{h, j}) = (a_{b_j - h, j})^{-1}, b_j mod 2 != 0.

We must impose sigma = identity. Naively, one relation per pair of
swapped generators suffices; but such relations have low Magnus
degree, and the low-degree relations are too costly for the
Golod-Shafarevich inequality. Instead, we pass to a new, cleverer
basis

    u_{i, l, j} = (T_1 - 1)^i (T_2 - 1)^l (a_{0, j}), 0 <= i, l <= 3,

where T_1, T_2 are the two cyclic shifts from the (Z/4)^2 deck group.
In this basis, sigma - 1 raises degree by one automatically, and we
can pick 24 relations whose weight polynomial is

    G(t) = 3t (1 + t) (1 + t^2)^2.

By contrast, the full generator weight polynomial in this basis is

    P(t) = 3t (1 + t + t^2 + t^3)^2 = (1 + t) G(t).

So we spend "generator weight" P(t) and recover all but a G(t) worth
of "surplus". Technically: we spend P(t) on generators, pay back
t G(t) on relations (the cheap ones), leaving a surplus of

    P(t) - t G(t) = G(t).

Since P = (1 + t) G, this is a large surplus; at t close to 1/4,
G(t) is around 1.028, so the net generator-relation surplus is about
0.028, enough to absorb all the extra relations we will add later.

Step 4 (Part 4). An infinite quotient compatible with the family.

Now we combine Step 2 (monodromy) and Step 3 (surplus). The idea:

    (i) Choose the finite parameter cover B' -> B so that the
        parameter monodromies act increasingly deep. Specifically,
        choose J large that pi_1(B') lies in the J-th term of the
        lower 2-central series of pi_1(B); then pi_1(B') acts
        trivially at degrees below J on F.

    (ii) Compactify B' to a smooth projective curve C', and separate
        all 48 marked sections on PP^1 x C' by blowing up their
        collision points. Get a smooth projective surface Y -> C'
        with a distinguished fibre Y_{c_*} consisting of a main
        sphere and 24 rational tails.

    (iii) Now impose the relations: for each pair (h, j), the sign
        involution of Step 3 identifies the positive and negative
        punctures with a sign; we impose the 24 relations imposing
        these signs (Step 3). Then, for each deep monodromy m, we
        impose the relations that m acts trivially on the whole K.

        Key: the relation cost of all deep monodromies is at most
        4^N t^{N+1} summed over N >= J, which is O((4t)^J) times a
        bounded factor. At t = 49/200, this is at most (4*49/200)^J
        = (196/200)^J = (49/50)^J, which we can make less than
        0.01 by choosing J large.

    (iv) The remaining boundary meridians: there are finitely many;
        impose big powers mu_i^{2^e}, with e large. The relation cost
        is b t^{2^e}, where b is the number of them, again easily
        made small.

    (v) Check the weighted Golod-Shafarevich inequality (Part 1.5):
        1 - P(t) + Q(t) < 0 for some t in (0, 1), where Q(t) is the
        sum of t^{v_lambda} over all relators. Cost accounting:

        1 - (generator cost P(t))
          + (involution cost t G(t) = P(t) - G(t))
          + (deep monodromy cost <= 0.01)
          + (boundary meridians cost <= 0.01)
            <= 1 - P(t) + P(t) - G(t) + 0.02
            = 1 - G(t) + 0.02
            <= 1 - 1.028 + 0.02
            = - 0.008 < 0.

        So the constructed quotient Q is infinite.

    (vi) Conclusion of Step 4: we have an infinite pro-2 quotient Q of
        pi_1(U), where U is the punctured-sphere bundle over B',
        with the property:
            - The map rho: pi_1(U) -> Q has dense image on every
              punctured fibre of U.
            - Every component meridian of the boundary divisor D
              (of Y) has finite image under rho.

Step 5 (Part 5, first half). Finite local detection and descent.

We now want to pass from "rho: pi_1(U) -> Q infinite" to "some nice
projective compactification X of a finite cover of U has
pi_1(X) -> Q infinite". This is done with a finite group-theory
argument: given the dense image rho: pi_1(U) -> Q with finite-order
images on all component meridians, find a finite quotient P_f of Q
that is injective on the entire local group A_V = image of
fundamental group of (small polydisk around boundary point) minus D
inside Q. Then form the finite etale cover U' -> U corresponding to
ker(pi_1(U) -> P_f). This extends to a projective morphism X -> Y
after normalization and resolution, and (crucially) the map rho
extends to a homomorphism rho_X: pi_1(X) -> Q.

The whole subtlety is that rho_X exists, i.e., rho kills all the
"new" boundary loops that arise when we compactify U' to X. The
finite-local-detection lemma is designed exactly so that the "new"
meridians land in rho(X) as identity.

Step 6 (Part 5, second half). The distinguished fibre lifts to
rational curves.

Here we need the special structure of D_* (the distinguished fibre
of Y). It has trivial image in Q (by Lemma 2.6.3), so all its
meridians are already killed -- and the preimage D_*' in X, a priori
a singular curve, resolves to a smooth nodal union of copies of
PP^1. We check this in Lemma 5.4.1 by local coordinate analysis.

Step 7 (Part 5, end). Infinite image on the compact fibre.

Apply Lemma 5.5.1: the smooth fibre F'_s of U' sits inside a small
neighborhood of the boundary fibre h^{-1}(c_*) and deforms onto one
connected component Z_0 of the preimage of D_*. The fundamental group
of F'_s has infinite image in Q (because pi_1(F_s) is dense in Q and
the open subgroup N = ker(P_f) is infinite). Hence pi_1(Z_0) also has
infinite image in pi_1(X). Then apply Lemma 0.5.1.

That completes the proof.

The remaining parts prove the specific lemmas invoked above, with
full details. If you only want the shape of the argument, you can
stop here and trust the references; if you want the actual
mathematics, read on.

PART 1. WEIGHTED PRO-2 GROUPS AND THE GOLOD-SHAFAREVICH INEQUALITY.

This part is purely algebraic. It records, with proofs, four tools we
will use in Parts 2-5:

    Section 1.1. Pro-2 groups.
    Section 1.2. The weighted Magnus algebra and Magnus degree.
    Section 1.3. The substitution lemma for degree-raising automorphisms.
    Section 1.4. The Frattini quotient and a basis criterion.
    Section 1.5. The weighted Golod-Shafarevich inequality.

Historical credit: the Golod-Shafarevich inequality is from Golod and
Shafarevich 1964 (Izvestiya Akad. Nauk SSSR, 28, 261-272); the filtered
form is due to Vinberg 1965 (Izvestiya, 29, 209-214); the exact
"completed-algebra" form we use, including the pro-2 consequence, is
Ershov 2011 (Proceedings of the LMS, 102, 599-636, Theorem 2.1 and
Corollary 2.2); the general "positive weighted deficiency" context is
Ershov-Jaikin-Zapirain 2013 (Crelle 677, 71-134, Corollary 4.4). The
proofs below are included for a self-contained exposition.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 1.1. Pro-2 groups.
--------------------------------------------------------------

Intuition. Finite groups whose order is a power of 2 are the simplest
interesting finite groups for our purposes. A pro-2 group is an
"infinite 2-group built from finite 2-groups", in the same way that
the 2-adic integers are built from Z/2^k.

Definition 1.1.1. A pro-2 group is an inverse limit

    G = inverse limit over i in I of G_i,

where {G_i} is a projective system of finite groups whose orders are
powers of 2, indexed by a directed partially ordered set I. We give G
the inverse-limit topology (a profinite topology). Equivalently, G is
a compact Hausdorff topological group in which the open subgroups have
2-power index and intersect to the identity.

Closed subgroups, quotients by closed normal subgroups, and continuous
homomorphisms are the natural operations on pro-2 groups. In the rest
of this document we drop the word "continuous" and simply say
"homomorphism"; all homomorphisms between pro-2 groups are assumed
continuous.

Definition 1.1.2. Let S be a finite set, |S| = d. Let F(S) be the
abstract free group on S and let

    F_d = F_d^{pro-2} = inverse limit over N of F(S) / (the N-th
        term of the 2-lower-central series)

be the pro-2 completion of F(S). We call F_d the free pro-2 group on d
generators. Every finitely generated pro-2 group is a quotient of
F_d for some d.

Concrete model. If S = {g_1, ..., g_d}, a "continuous homomorphism"
from F_d to a finite 2-group P is the same as a function from S to P
that extends to a group homomorphism F(S) -> P. Continuity in the
profinite topology is automatic when P is finite.

The key fact: a finitely generated pro-2 group is a specific quotient
of F_d by a closed normal subgroup, and every such quotient is again a
pro-2 group.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 1.2. The weighted Magnus algebra and Magnus degree.
--------------------------------------------------------------

We now construct a way to "linearise" a pro-2 group: replace group
elements with formal power series, using power series over F_2 in
noncommuting variables.

Definition 1.2.1. Let F_d be free pro-2 on g_1, ..., g_d. Choose
positive integer weights w_1, ..., w_d, one for each generator. Define
Lambda_d = F_2 << U_1, ..., U_d >> to be the F_2-algebra of formal
noncommutative power series in d symbols U_1, ..., U_d. Declare

    weighted degree of U_i = w_i,

and extend to a filtration

    Lambda_d(>= n) = F_2-linear span of monomials of weighted
                     degree at least n.

The product in Lambda_d respects the filtration: Lambda_d(>= m) times
Lambda_d(>= n) lies in Lambda_d(>= m+n).

Let Lambda_d(+) = sum_{n >= 1} Lambda_d(>= n) (the "positive part",
i.e., series with zero constant term). Then the set

    1 + Lambda_d(+) = { 1 + f : f in Lambda_d(+) }

is a group under multiplication (the inverse of 1 + f is the finite
geometric sum 1 - f + f^2 - f^3 + ... which converges in the
inverse-limit topology).

Definition 1.2.2 (Magnus expansion). The map from the generating set
of F_d to 1 + Lambda_d(+) is defined by

    g_i |-> 1 + U_i.

It extends uniquely to a continuous group homomorphism

    Magnus: F_d -> 1 + Lambda_d(+).

We call this the Magnus expansion of F_d with weights w_1, ..., w_d.

Fact 1.2.3 (Magnus is faithful). The Magnus expansion is injective.

Sketch proof. Truncate: for every integer N >= 1, define
Lambda_d^{(N)} = Lambda_d / Lambda_d(>= N); this is a finite-dimensional
F_2-algebra (because only finitely many monomials of degree <= N are
nonzero, only values 0, 1). Denote by (1 + Lambda_d^{(N)})^x the
subgroup 1 + (positive part). The truncated Magnus map F_d ->
1 + Lambda_d^{(N)} factors through some finite 2-quotient of F_d,
because the target is a finite 2-group.

Conversely, given a finite 2-quotient P of F_d (a finite group whose
order is a power of 2), the augmentation ideal I_P = ker(F_2[P] -> F_2)
of the group algebra F_2[P] is nilpotent: induct on |P|, and for P != 1
choose a central involution z in P (exists since P is a finite 2-group),
replace P by P/<z>, whose group algebra is F_2[P]/(z - 1); the element
(z - 1) has square zero in F_2[P] because (z - 1)^2 = z^2 - 2z + 1 =
1 - 1 = 0 in characteristic 2, and the quotient by z is nilpotent by
induction; so I_P is nilpotent.

Fact: the truncation map F_d -> 1 + Lambda_d^{(N)} has image is
exactly P for some open normal P of F_d, and these images are cofinal
in all open subgroups of F_d. That is, we can recover the pro-2
topology of F_d from the Magnus filtration. Since the truncations
{F_d -> 1 + Lambda_d^{(N)}}_{N >= 1} jointly separate points of F_d, the
full map is injective. Concretely, if Magnus(g) = 1 for some g in F_d
with g != 1, then some truncation Lambda_d^{(N)} has a nonzero image
of g - 1, contradicting Magnus(g) = 1 mod Lambda_d(>= N).

Positive weights give the same topology as ordinary weight (degree).

Definition 1.2.4 (Magnus degree). For nontrivial g in F_d, define
the weighted Magnus degree

    nu(g) = least n >= 1 such that the image of g - 1 in
            Lambda_d / Lambda_d(>= n) is nonzero.
    nu(1) = infinity.

Since the Magnus expansion is faithful, every nontrivial g has finite
nu(g).

Basic estimates. Direct from multiplication of power series, for
g, h in F_d:

    nu(g h)      >= min( nu(g), nu(h) ),
    nu([g, h])   >= nu(g) + nu(h),
    nu(g^{2^e})  >= 2^e nu(g),     for every e >= 0, g in F_d.        (1.2.5)

The third inequality uses the identity (g - 1)^{2^e} = g^{2^e} - 1,
which holds in characteristic 2 (all internal binomial coefficients
2^e choose k for 1 <= k <= 2^e - 1 are divisible by 2). Both
commutator conventions [g, h] = ghg^{-1}h^{-1} and [g, h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh
have the same Magnus degree lower bound; only the degree matters.

Interpretation. Magnus degree measures "how close g is to the
identity, in the pro-2 topology". nu(g) = n means g is trivial modulo
Lambda_d(>= n) but nonzero modulo Lambda_d(>= n - 1); higher nu means
"deeper" in the filtration.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 1.3. The substitution lemma.
--------------------------------------------------------------

This is how we will compare two filtrations on the same group.

Lemma 1.3.1 (Substitution). Let alpha: F_d -> F_d be a continuous
automorphism. Suppose that, for some integer a >= 1, we have

    nu( alpha(g_i) * g_i^{-1} ) >= w_i + a        for every i = 1..d.   (1.3.2)

Then the action of alpha on Lambda_d raises degree by a:

    (alpha - identity)( Lambda_d(>= n) ) subset Lambda_d(>= n + a)

for every n >= 1.

Proof. Assumption 1.3.2 says: alpha(U_i) - U_i has weighted degree at
least w_i + a.

Let U_{i_1} U_{i_2} ... U_{i_m} be a mono mial of weighted degree N =
w_{i_1} + ... + w_{i_m} >= n. Its image under (the induced operator)
alpha is

    alpha(U_{i_1}) alpha(U_{i_2}) ... alpha(U_{i_m}).

Write each factor alpha(U_{i_k}) = U_{i_k} + E_k, where E_k has weighted
degree at least w_{i_k} + a. Expand the product:

    product over k of ( U_{i_k} + E_k ).

The term with all E_k = 0 is the original monomial
U_{i_1} ... U_{i_m}, of degree N >= n. All other terms contain at
least one E_k, and hence have weighted degree at least N + a >= n + a.
So the difference alpha(monomial) - monomial lies in Lambda_d(>= n+a)
for every monomial of degree >= n. Extending linearly to polynomials
gives the same for every element of the n-th truncation of Lambda_d,
i.e., for every element of the F_2-span of monomials of degree >= n
that furthermore has bounded degree. Passing to formal series by
completeness gives the stated result.

The completeness/convergence step: in each truncation
Lambda_d / Lambda_d(>= N) for fixed N, the operator alpha only involves
finitely many terms (those with E contributing less than N - n of
extra degree); the limit N -> infinity is well defined in the
inverse-limit topology. So the operator extension to all of Lambda_d
is a legitimate continuous operator. End.

Corollary 1.3.3 (Powers of a substitution). Under 1.3.2, for every
integer k >= 1, the operator alpha^k - 1 also raises degree by a.

Proof. alpha is an automorphism, so alpha - 1 raises degree by a. By
the same argument, alpha^k - 1 = sum_{j=0}^{k-1} alpha^j (alpha - 1),
and each term raises degree by a. Sum of terms raising degree by a
also raises degree by a. End.

Corollary 1.3.4 (Operator substitution). Suppose for each i,
we are given a continuous endomorphism phi_i of a filtered F_2-vector
space W such that phi_i raises degree by at least w_i, i.e.,
phi_i(W(>= n)) subset W(>= n + w_i). Then substituting U_i |-> phi_i
gives a continuous endomorphism Phi of W extending the map on the
monomial basis by

    U_{i_1} ... U_{i_m} |-> phi_{i_1} ... phi_{i_m}.

Consequently, Phi raises degree by at least (weighted degree of the
input monomial). In particular, any sum that converges in the
inverse-limit topology gives a continuous operator on W.

Proof. Same estimate as in Lemma 1.3.1: each factor raises the
degree by at least its weight; multiplying m factors raises by at
least the sum. End.

We will use Corollary 1.3.4 with W = Lambda_L (the Magnus algebra of L,
introduced in Part 3) and phi_i being the operators representing the
generators of F in the representation obtained by extending the
homomorphism Phi: F -> L x| H (see Lemma 3.5.2). The hypothesis
phi_i raises degree by w_i will hold by construction.

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 1.4. The Frattini quotient and the basis criterion.
--------------------------------------------------------------

We will need to tell when a proposed set of elements of a pro-2 group
is a free basis, or at least a generating set. The tool is a finite-
dimensional F_2-vector space called the Frattini quotient.

Definition 1.4.1 (Frattini quotient). Let G be a finitely generated
pro-2 group. Let

    G^2 = the smallest closed normal subgroup of G
          whose quotient G/G^2 is an elementary abelian 2-group,
    [G, G] = the closure of the commutator subgroup of G.

The Frattini quotient is

    G^{Frat} = G / (G^2 [G, G])-closure,

an F_2-vector space of finite dimension d_Frat >= d_min(G) (where
d_min(G) is the minimal number of generators of G).

Lemma 1.4.2 (Frattini basis criterion). Let G be a finitely generated
pro-2 group, and let x_1, ..., x_m in G. Suppose that the images of
the x_i in G^{Frat} are F_2-linearly independent and span G^{Frat}.
Then x_1, ..., x_m is a minimal topological generating set for G.

If moreover m = d and G is the free pro-2 group F_d, then x_1, ...,
x_d is a free pro-2 basis of F_d.

Proof. First sentence. Suppose the closed subgroup H generated by
x_1, ..., x_m were proper in G. Since G is a pro-2 group, H is
contained in an open subgroup M of index 2 (every proper closed
subgroup of a pro-2 group lies in some maximal open subgroup of
index 2: consider G/H as a nontrivial pro-2 group, take a maximal
proper open subgroup of it, pull back to G). Then the projection
G -> G/M = Z/2 kills all x_i, so their images in G^{Frat} lie in the
kernel of the map G^{Frat} -> Z/2, which is a subspace of codimension
1. But the (x_i)^{Frat} span, contradiction. So H = G.

Second sentence. Now suppose m = d and G = F_d. The Frattini basis
criterion gives a surjective continuous homomorphism F_d -> F_d (via
g_i |-> x_i). We need: every surjective endomorphism of a finitely
generated profinite group is injective.

Proof of the Hopfian property. Let phi: G -> G be a surjective
continuous endomorphism of a finitely generated profinite group G.
Let K = ker(phi). For every open normal subgroup N of G, the
preimage phi^{-1}(N) is also open normal (because phi is continuous
and surjective, and N open normal, the preimage is open normal). So
phi acts on the set of open normal subgroups by N |-> phi^{-1}(N),
permuting the finite set of open normal subgroups of each fixed index.
For fixed index k, phi permutes the finite set {N: N open normal,
[G:N]=k}; hence some positive power phi^j preserves every N in that
finite set (take j = order of the permutation, or any common multiple
over finitely many k). Fix N. Then phi^j induces an endomorphism of
G/N that is injective (it is an automorphism of the finite set, as a
permutation of subgroups of index k it must be a bijection).
So ker(phi^j) subset N. But ker(phi) subset ker(phi^j) (since
phi^j is a power of phi). Hence ker(phi) subset N. As N was arbitrary
open normal, and open normal subgroups intersect to the identity in a
profinite group, ker(phi) = identity. So phi is injective, hence an
automorphism. End.

Remark 1.4.3. The Frattini basis criterion is used in Parts 3 and 4:
every "free pro-2 basis" we produce will be justified by checking its
Frattini images form a basis of the F_2-vector space R = F_2[H].

--------------------------------------------------------------
Part 1.5. The weighted Golod-Shafarevich inequality.
--------------------------------------------------------------

This is the engine that will turn "the weighted sum of generators is
strictly bigger than the weighted sum of relations, at some 0 < t < 1"
into "the quotient is infinite".

Statement (Lemma 1.5.1, weighted Golod-Shafarevich criterion). Let
F_d be free pro-2 on g_1, ..., g_d with weights w_1, ..., w_d in N (the
positive integers). Let (r_lambda)_{lambda in Index} be a finite or
countably infinite family of relators in F_d, each nontrivial, with
positive integer LOWER bounds v_lambda such that for every lambda:

    nu(r_lambda) >= v_lambda >= 1.                                   (1.5.2)

Assume only finitely many v_lambda lie below any fixed integer
(bounded-degree local finiteness). Define weight polynomials

    P(t) = sum_{i=1}^d t^{w_i},     Q(t) = sum_lambda t^{v_lambda}.  (1.5.3)

Suppose there exists t in the open interval (0, 1) such that

    (i) Q(t) converges as a real power series, and
    (ii) 1 - P(t) + Q(t) < 0.                                        (1.5.4)

Then the pro-2 quotient Q-out := F_d / (closed normal subgroup generated
by the r_lambda) is infinite.

Before the proof, let us note the two standard consequences of the
hypothesis (i).

Remark 1.5.5. Hypothesis (i) means that sum_lambda t^{v_lambda} is
finite for that t. Bounded-degree local finiteness ensures this holds
for every t in (0, 1): if v_lambda -> infinity, then only finitely many
v_lambda are <= N, and the sum splits as sum_{N} (number of lambda
with v_lambda = N) t^N, which converges for t < 1 provided the numbers
of lambdas at each level grow subexponentially. In our applications
below, v_lambda -> infinity, so we can take any t < 1.

Remark 1.5.6 (why lower bounds). If we knew nu(r_lambda) >= v_lambda,
the contribution of r_lambda to the weighted relation sum is at most
t^{v_lambda} for t in (0, 1) (since t^{nu(r_lambda)} <= t^{v_lambda}).
Using a lower bound smaller than the true degree is CONServative
(it makes Q(t) larger, so the inequality 1 - P + Q < 0 harder). So
we use true lower bounds, computable from explicit degree estimates.

Proof of Lemma 1.5.1.

Let Lambda_d be the weighted Magnus algebra of Part 1.2 with the given
weights. For lambda, let f_lambda = r_lambda - 1 in Lambda_d. Let

    I = closed two-sided ideal generated by the f_lambda in Lambda_d,
    R = Lambda_d / I.

The algebra R inherits a filtration R(>= n) = image of Lambda_d(>= n).
We define, for any integer n (positive or nonpositive),

    C(n) = dim_{F_2} ( R_{>=1} / R(>= n) ) if n >= 2,
    C(n) = 0                               if n <= 1.               (1.5.7)

Actually we will use C(n) = dim_{F_2} R/R(>= n), with C(n) = 0 for
n <= 0, and C(1) = 1 (the constant). This C(n) is nondecreasing in n.

We proceed in four steps.

Step 1. First-letter decomposition. Every element of Lambda_d(>= 1)
has a unique decomposition of the form

    sum_i U_i b_i,     b_i in Lambda_d,

by looking at the first letter of each monomial (with U_i ranging over
generators). This decomposition is compatible with the filtration and
respects the ideal I. Hence there is a surjective map of series (at
each level)

    bigoplus_{i=1}^d R / R(>= n - w_i) ->> R_{>= 1} / R(>= n),      (1.5.8a)
        ( bar b_i )_i  |->  sum_i U_i bar b_i mod R(>= n).           (1.5.8b)

The target R_{>=1}/R(>= n) has dimension C(n) - C(1) = C(n) - 1 (we
count the full quotient and subtract off the constant 1).

We must bound the kernel of (1.5.8). A vector ( bar b_i )_i is in the
kernel exactly when the sum sum_i U_i b_i lies in (I + Lambda_d(>= n))
mod Lambda_d(>= n). Now I is the closed two-sided ideal generated by
the f_lambda. So the elements of I are limits of sums of products
x_m f_{lambda_m} y_m.

Take a single product x f_lambda y. We can write

    f_lambda = sum_i U_i f^{(lambda)}_i,

where f^{(lambda)}_i in Lambda_d and nu(r_lambda) = nu(f_lambda) >=
v_lambda. This first-letter decomposition of f_lambda implies that

    x f_lambda y = sum_i (x U_i) f^{(lambda)}_i y
                 = sum_i U_i (x f^{(lambda)}_i y)
                   + sum_i [x, U_i] f^{(lambda)}_i y.

The second sum: [x, U_i] has degree >= deg(x) + w_i in the sense of
the filtration, at least when x is a nontrivial element of
Lambda_d(>= 1). When x = 1 (i.e., the "constant part" of the
multiplier), the first-letter contribution of x f_lambda y is exactly

    ( f^{(lambda)}_i bar y )_{i=1..d}  modulo  R(>= n - v_lambda),

where bar y is the image of y in R. This vector is "shifted": each
component f^{(lambda)}_i has degree at least v_lambda, and the
quality "mod R(>= n - v_lambda)" reflects that only the y-parts of
size < n - v_lambda matter (any larger y contributes 0 mod R(>= n) to
the product). So the first-letter data arising from a single relator
f_lambda span a space of dimension at most C(n - v_lambda).

Taking limits and summing over lambda:

    dim( kernel of (1.5.8) )  <=  sum_lambda C(n - v_lambda).        (1.5.9)

Consequently, from (1.5.8) surjection + (1.5.9) kernel bound:

    dim (target)  >=  dim (source)  -  dim (kernel),
    C(n) - 1      >=  sum_i C(n - w_i)  -  sum_lambda C(n - v_lambda).  (1.5.10)

Equivalently, adding back:

    C(n) - sum_i C(n - w_i) + sum_lambda C(n - v_lambda) >= 1.       (1.5.11)

Step 2. Cumulative Ginibre. Multiply (1.5.11) by t^{n-1} (for 0 < t <
1) and sum over n >= 1. The left-hand side becomes

    sum_{n >= 1} C(n) t^{n-1}
    - sum_i [sum_{n>=1} C(n - w_i) t^{n-1}]
    + sum_lambda [sum_{n>=1} C(n - v_lambda) t^{n-1}].

For the i-sumband: sum_{n>=1} C(n - w_i) t^{n-1} =
t^{w_i - 1} sum_{n>=1} C(n - w_i) t^{n - w_i} = t^{w_i - 1} S, where
S = sum_{n >= 1} C(n) t^{n-1} (with C(k) = 0 for k <= 0). Similarly
for lambda: t^{v_lambda - 1} S. So

    (1 - P(t) + Q(t)) S  >=  1 / (1 - t).                            (1.5.12)

Since Q(t) converges by hypothesis (i), and C(n) >= 0 by definition,
all sums are convergent or nonnegatively divergent to +inf; the
inequality holds in the extended real sense.

Step 3. Contradiction. Suppose the quotient Q-out := F_d / (normal
closure of r_lambda) is finite, of order m.

Then R / R(>= n) is a quotient of F_2[Q-out] (the group algebra of
Q-out over F_2) for every n: the map F_d -> Q-out factors through the
truncated Magnus at each level because the relators r_lambda are
killed. Specifically, the group ring F_2[Q-out] has dimension m, and
the natural map R -> F_2[Q-out] (or R / R(>= n) -> F_2[Q-out]) is
surjective. So C(n) <= m for every n. (Actually, C(n) = dim R/R(>= n)
<= dim R / (union R(>= n)) = dim R <= m, using the standard fact that
R is spanned by the group-algebra of any lift of Q-out.)

Boundedness of C(n) by m gives

    S = sum_{n>=1} C(n) t^{n-1}  <=  m sum_{n>=1} t^{n-1}
       =  m / (1 - t).                                               (1.5.13)

Step 4. Apply hypothesis (ii). From (ii), 1 - P(t) + Q(t) < 0 as a
real number. Combining with (1.5.12):

    S  >=  (1 / (1 - t)) / (1 - P(t) + Q(t)) < 0.

But S >= 0 by definition. Contradiction. Therefore Q-out is infinite. End.

Remark 1.5.14. The condition Q(t) < +infinity for some t in (0, 1)
is automatic when only finitely many relators are below each bound
(Lemma's hypothesis). In our later applications we will make sure
the numbers of relators at each degree are bounded by a fixed
geometric sequence, guaranteeing convergence for all t < 1/4.

Remark 1.5.15. The inequality (1.5.4) has a beautiful interpretation.
The quantity

    (weighted generator count) - (weighted relation count)

at "t-scale" is P(t) - Q(t). If P(t) - Q(t) > 1 for some t in (0, 1),
the Golod-Shafarevich inequality holds. The quantity 1 - P(t) + Q(t) is
the "deficit at scale t"; if it is negative, the group must be infinite.
Think of P(t) - Q(t) as a continuous analogue of (generators -
relations) in Euler characteristic; positivity of the "excess" forces
infinite.

We will use Lemma 1.5.1 again and again with concrete polynomials P
and G (the involution polynomial); the natural t to plug in is near
1/4, giving clear numerical margins (see Part 4.4).

End of Part 1.
