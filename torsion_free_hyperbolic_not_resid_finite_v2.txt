A finitely generated group is word-hyperbolic if some Cayley graph has uniformly thin geodesic triangles: a constant delta such that every point of one side of any geodesic triangle lies within delta of the other two sides. The residual finiteness question asks whether every word-hyperbolic group is residually finite, that is, whether coarse negative curvature by itself forces separation by finite quotients.

parnoindenthttps://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4613-9586-7_3

Hyperbolicity in this sense is Gromov's; the metric theory of polygonal and non-positively curved complexes, including the angular-link control of curvature used below, is developed by Bridson and Haefliger.

parnoindenthttps://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-12494-9

Residual finiteness is common but far from automatic. Finitely generated free groups have it, by Stallings' finite-graph argument, and finitely generated linear groups over a field have it, by Malcev. Dehn's algorithm solves the word problem for many finitely presented groups, but solvability of the word problem does not by itself give residual finiteness, and the combinatorial background is in Lyndon and Schupp. At the opposite extreme, infinite simple groups such as Ol'shanskii's Tarski monsters admit no nontrivial finite quotient at all, so their finite residual is the whole group and they are emphatically not residually finite; none of these monsters is hyperbolic. Whether hyperbolicity rules this phenomenon out is the question.

There is a torsion-free word-hyperbolic group that is not residually finite.


The group is the fundamental group G=pi_1(K,O) of a finite Euclidean triangle complex K. A finite family mathcal RcalR of explicitly described nonidentity elements is produced, and no finite quotient separates all of them; consequently some fixed winmathcal RcalR lies in every finite quotient, i.e. win R_f(G), and since wneq1 the group fails to be residually finite. The argument proves that one member of mathcal RcalR works for all finite quotients, without identifying which member.

The idea is short. The complex K is built from Euclidean triangles so that every vertex link has no short cycle; this much positive angle excess forces pi_1(K) to be torsion-free and word-hyperbolic. Inside K sit many four-edge loops, the rectangular words, each a nonidentity element. A single finite quotient can separate only finitely many elements, so residual finiteness would supply one finite quotient keeping all the rectangular words nontrivial at once. A rank count over a fixed finite field forbids this: each loop array contributes a matrix of large rank, while the local triangle relations force the same matrix to have small rank. Hence one rectangular word dies in every finite quotient.

The same obstruction has a consequence for linear representations. A group is linear over a commutative field k if it embeds in GL_n(k) for some n. Malcev proved that every finitely generated linear group over a commutative field is residually finite.

For the group G above, every commutative field k, every nge1, and every homomorphism rho:GtoGL_n(k), one has R_f(G)⊆kerrho. Thus one fixed nonidentity element is killed by every such representation, and G is linear over no commutative field.

Proof. 
The image rho(G) is a finitely generated linear group, hence residually finite by Malcev. If gin R_f(G) had rho(g)neq1, residual finiteness of rho(G) would give a finite quotient separating rho(g), contradicting gin R_f(G). So R_f(G)⊆kerrho, and the nonidentity element of R_f(G) is killed by every rho.


Several class-wide statements accompany the result. Kapovich and Wise proved that all hyperbolic groups are residually finite if and only if every nontrivial hyperbolic group has a nontrivial finite quotient, if and only if every hyperbolic group is virtually torsion-free. Agol, Groves, and Manning proved that a positive answer would make every quasiconvex subgroup of every hyperbolic group separable. Additional geometric structure does force residual finiteness: Haglund and Wise showed that compact virtually special cube complexes are linear, hence residually finite, and Agol showed that a word-hyperbolic group acting properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex is virtually special, so a counterexample admits no such cubulation. Nonpositive curvature alone does not suffice: Wise's complete square complexes and Burger--Mozes' finitely presented simple groups act on products of trees and are not residually finite, though those spaces contain flat planes and are not hyperbolic. Nonlinearity also does not suffice: Kapovich produced a hyperbolic group all of whose finite-dimensional representations over any field have finite image, and Tholozan and Tsouvalas produced residually finite nonlinear hyperbolic groups.

The load-bearing input is the following criterion, whose hypotheses concern only the angular lengths of closed walks in vertex links.

A finite Euclidean triangle complex is a finite graph with finitely many Euclidean triangles attached side-to-side along full edges, with positive compatible lengths and isometric gluings. Each closed cell embeds; distinct cells may meet in a union of faces. The angular link at a vertex has one vertex per incident edge-germ and one edge per incident triangle corner, of length equal to the corner angle. A link walk is reduced if it has no immediate reversal, and cyclically reduced if this also holds across its closing seam; link distance between distinct components is +∞. The intrinsic metric is the infimum of lengths of polygonal paths made of straight segments inside cells. A turn of a closed edge path compares the direction back along the incoming edge with the direction forward along the outgoing edge, as points of the vertex link.

[Angular criterion]
Let K be a connected finite Euclidean triangle complex. Suppose that for some eta>0 every nonempty cyclically reduced closed edge walk in every vertex link has angular length at least 2pi+eta. Then pi_1(K) is torsion-free and word-hyperbolic. Moreover, any nonempty closed edge path whose every cyclic turn has link distance at least pi is not nullhomotopic.


The three conclusions are the closed-path statement, torsion-freeness, and word-hyperbolicity, proved after the construction. The criterion is the classical large-link curvature condition; the special case of metric polygonal complexes with angle le2pi everywhere is Bridson--Haefliger's.

The combinatorial input is the high-girth alteration argument of Erdős.

The metric picture. Throughout, K is the complex above; "original" refers to its cells before subdivision, and links are metric graphs with edge lengths equal to corner angles. Barycentric subdivision gives a genuine simplicial complex S whose vertices are the original cells and whose simplices are strict inclusion chains (distinct parallel edges get distinct barycenters). Realized by straight segments, it preserves the link metric at original vertices; at a new vertex inside a triangle the link is a circle of length 2pi; at a new vertex inside an edge the two tangent directions are joined by one path of length pi per incident triangle (or are isolated). So every nonempty cyclically reduced closed link walk at a new vertex still has length at least 2pi, and at an original vertex length at least 2pi+eta. These facts persist under further compatible Euclidean subdivision, and the subdivision may be chosen to contain any fixed finite collection of edgeloops.

[Metric preliminaries]

The intrinsic distance is a metric inducing the complex topology; K is compact; any two points are joined by a shortest path; every constant-speed local geodesic, closed or not, has finitely many straight pieces. If such a path lies in the edges of a Euclidean subdivision, its link distance at each turn is at least pi.

Proof. 
Around x, the truncated incident sectors (and possibly isolated radial intervals) are glued along radial sides; the radial coordinate agrees across gluings, a path leaving the star of radius rho has length gerho, and the radial segment has length exactly rho and is shortest. This gives positivity, identifies metric with star neighborhoods (so the metric induces the topology), and gives contracting neighborhoods. Compactness yields shortest paths as limits of proportional-arclength minimizing sequences: speeds are bounded, equicontinuity gives a uniformly convergent subsequence, and length is lower semicontinuous. A sufficiently short unit-speed minimizing arc inside a star has radial coordinate r(t)=t; its direction in the finite graph of directions is locally constant, so the arc is straight in one sector, giving finitely many straight pieces on any compact parameter set (and, for a closed geodesic, around the parameter circle). A turn whose angular distance is less than pi unfolds two nearby equal-radius points into a sector of total angle <pi and a straight chord shorter than the two radii, contradicting local minimality; this includes the closing seam of a closed geodesic.


Two-dimensional filling theory is classical; the account followed here is Lyndon and Schupp's.

parnoindenthttps://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-61896-3

A planar diagram is a finite connected complex embedded in the plane, with edges and triangular faces, in which every bounded complementary face of its graph is filled by a triangle. Its exterior walk goes once around the unbounded face, counting incidences; it may repeat vertices and traverse a spur (an edge with no incident triangle) twice. The embedding fixes cyclic orders; the Euclidean metrics on the triangles need not come from this embedding.

[Reduced fillings]

Let S be a Euclidean simplicial subdivision of K. Every nonempty nullhomotopic closed edge path of S is the exterior walk of a planar diagram Delta carrying a map to S that takes each edge and triangle isometrically onto an edge and triangle. Every triangle has three distinct vertices and edges. At each interior vertex of Delta the cyclic sequence of corners maps to a nonempty cyclically reduced closed walk in the target link. Every edge loop in Delta contracts in Delta.

Proof. 
Start from a continuous filling of the path by a disk, its boundary mapped linearly along each prescribed edge traversal, all boundary breakpoints marked. Barycentric coordinates of the vertices of S, extended by zero off their stars, are continuous; triangulate the disk finely enough that each coordinate varies by less than 1/4 on each small triangle. Label a domain vertex by a target vertex of largest coordinate (at least 1/3, as S has dimension le2), so at every point the chosen labels all have positive coordinate and lie in one simplex; extending linearly gives a simplicial map, allowing collapses. Along each prescribed boundary edge the only admissible labels are its two endpoints and the largest-coordinate label changes from one to the other exactly once, so the noncollapsed boundary steps are exactly the prescribed edge word in order; the same on a circle or interval makes every vertex loop a based edge-loop.

For the simplicial filling, draw the inverse images of the ties for the largest coordinate. In a triangle mapped onto a triangle these are three arcs meeting at a trivalent node; onto an edge, one separating arc; onto a vertex, absent. Suppressing valence-two bends gives a polygonal picture: regions are labelled by target vertices, an arc separates labels spanning a target edge, three labels at each node span a target triangle, and boundary leaves correspond to the letters of the edge word. Among all such pictures with the given boundary data choose one with the fewest nodes.

No arc joins two nodes labelled by one target triangle. If its two sides are a,b and d is the third vertex of that triangle, the arc with its endpoints is an embedded interval whose thin regular neighborhood meets the four other half-arcs in four distinct boundary slots; the two a-side slots both separate a from d, likewise the two b-side slots. Erasing the two nodes and the arc and rejoining each pair along its side, with label d between, gives a picture with two fewer nodes and unchanged boundary labels. This is unaffected by the half-arcs reconnecting outside the neighborhood. An arc cannot join a node to itself, since the three arcs there carry three distinct target-edge labels.

Next erase every component off the boundary. The retained picture with the boundary circle is a connected polygonal graph H whose inside regions are disk interiors with (possibly repeated) boundary walks. A thin regular neighborhood of H, missing the finitely many erased compacta, carries in each region a connected curve following the whole boundary walk on the region side (both sides of a bridge, every incidence at a repeated vertex); this curve crosses no old arc, so all boundary labels of the region agree, and the label extends over it. Erasing cannot add nodes, so minimality survives.

Now dualize: a vertex per region, radii to arc midpoints joined across arcs as dual edges; around each node the three radii bound a triangle whose dual vertices are distinct. With E arcs, N nodes, L>0 boundary leaves, adjoining the leaf-subdivided circle gives a connected planar graph with N+L vertices, E+L edges, and E-N+1 inside regions; the dual has E-N+1 vertices and E edges, hence exactly N bounded faces, exhausted by the N constructed triangles. This is the required holed-free planar diagram mapping to S, its exterior walk the prescribed word and a boundary-to-boundary arc a spur-edge traversed twice. At an interior dual vertex an immediate link reversal would put two adjacent triangles on one target triangle, joined by the arc dual to their shared edge, contradicting minimality, cyclic seam included. Every simple edge circuit bounds its filled disk, and any edge loop splits into circuits and cancellable backtracks, so edge loops contract.

[Closed paths]

In K, or any compatible Euclidean simplicial subdivision, a nonempty closed edge path whose every cyclic turn has link distance at least pi is not nullhomotopic.

Proof. 
Work in a simplicial subdivision and suppose such a path nullhomotopic, so Lemma~lem:reduced-diagrams supplies a reduced diagram. At an interior vertex the summand of~(eq:gauss-bonnet) is at most 0. At a vertex with n_y=1 the two interior corners give a link path of length theta_y between the two boundary directions, so the prescribed turn forces theta_ygepi and the summand is at most 0; the only cornerless case is a spur tip with coincident directions, excluded. For n_yge2 the summand is at most 0 outright. All summands nonpositive contradict~(eq:gauss-bonnet). A path in the original complex passes to the barycentric subdivision with unchanged turn distances (straight-through directions along an original edge have link distance pi, or are an excluded spur), preserving the hypothesis.


Shortest free loops. A closed local geodesic bounds a free homotopy class in which it is shortest; the closed-path obstruction then kills torsion.

[Torsion-free]

pi_1(K) is torsion-free.

Proof. 
Contracting stars and compactness give ε>0 so every ball of radius 5ε is contractible in K, and any two loops uniformly ε-close are freely homotopic: cone each quadrilateral through shortest connectors in a ball of radius 3ε and glue along the connectors into an annulus. Let gne1 and take the infimum ellge5ε of lengths in its free class. A proportional-arclength minimizing sequence has bounded speed, so Lemma~lem:finite-geodesics gives a length-ell minimizer in the same class; arclength parametrization shows it is a local geodesic, since otherwise a subarc of length <ε is replaced by a shorter shortest path with the same endpoints, the difference contracting in a star and shortening the loop within its class. This closed local geodesic has finitely many straight pieces; insert them into a finite compatible Euclidean subdivision so it lies in the edges, preserving links and turn distances. If g had finite order, a positive repetition would be nullhomotopic, contradicting Proposition~prop:closed-paths, since its turns, including repeated seams, still have link distance at least pi.

[Stability of controlled paths]

Let X have delta-thin geodesic triangles, lambdage1, Cge0. There is D=D(delta,lambda,C) such that any rectifiable sigma with length(sigma|_{[u,v]})lelambda d(sigma(u),sigma(v))+C for all ule v lies in the D-neighborhood of a geodesic J with the same endpoints, and J lies in the 2D-neighborhood of sigma. Triangles whose three sides obey the subarc bound are (3D+delta)-thin.

Proof. 
Projection. If R_0>4delta, d(x,J),d(y,J)ge R_0, d(x,y)le R_0/2, then nearest points x',y' satisfy d(x',y')le8delta: every zin[x',y'] is within 2delta of one of the other sides of the quadrilateral x,x',y',y, cannot be near [x,y] (else d(x,J)le R_0/2+2delta<R_0), and near [x,x'] or [y,y'] forces d(z,x')le4delta; apply this at the midpoint.

Excursions. Take R_0=1+32lambdadelta, so R_0>4delta and 16lambdadelta/R_0<1/2. A component of sigma beyond distance R_0 from J has both ends at distance R_0; partition it by arclength into at most 1+2H/R_0 pieces of length le R_0/2, with H its length. All partition points are at distance ge R_0, so summing the projection bound gives 8delta(1+2H/R_0) for the end projections' distance, and the subarc bound gives Hlelambda(2R_0+8delta)+(16lambdadelta)/(R_0)H+C, hence Hle2[lambda(2R_0+8delta)+C]. Every excursion point is within H/2 of an end, so sigma lies within D=R_0+lambda(2R_0+8delta)+C of J. Conversely, for qin J choose xinsigma with d(s,x)=d(s,q) (s the start); a nearest x'in J gives d(q,x')=|d(s,x)-d(s,x')|le D, so d(q,x)le2D. Replacing path sides by geodesics gives (3D+delta)-thinness.


The Cayley graph. Word-hyperbolicity is the last step: relate distances in reduced diagrams to word distances in a Cayley graph, then convert diagram thinness into thinness of Cayley triangles.

[Word-hyperbolic]

G=pi_1(K) is word-hyperbolic.

Proof. 
There is B_1 with d_{Delta^{(1)}}(u,v)le B_1d_Delta(u,v) for diagram vertices: replace a straight piece through a triangle interior by a route over its boundary across a corner of angle omega, of length at most 1/sin(omega/2) times the Euclidean distance (law of cosines), with one factor c over the finitely many model angles. Partial edge traversals combine into a full-edge walk of length le c,d_Delta(u,v) and at most c,d_Delta(u,v)/ell_* edges, ell_* the minimal model edge length.

Fix o and a spanning tree of the 1-skeleton; for an oriented edge e:uto v take the element of tau_u etau_v^{-1} and let S be the finite symmetric set of nonidentity values, which generates G. Choose one edge loop per sin S and let B_2 bound all their lengths. Expand a Cayley geodesic triangle Cay(G,S) letter by letter into these loops. Their concatenation is a nullhomotopic edge path with a reduced diagram; the letter transitions partition each side into loops of length le B_2 whose marks are the successive group vertices.

For marks u,v with labels g_u,g_v, d_S(g_u,g_v)le d_{Delta^{(1)}}(u,v)le B_1d_Delta(u,v): any diagram edge path has the same displacement as the outside walk (they differ by a contractible edge loop), and inserting tree paths gives at most one letter of S per edge. Along one geodesic side the letter count between marks equals their group distance, so the expanded subarc between them has length le B_1B_2d_Delta(u,v); extending an arbitrary subarc to enclosing marks adds at most B_2 per end. With lambda=max{1,B_1B_2} and C=2lambda B_2, every subarc obeys the stability bound. Lemmas~lem:diagram-thinness and~lem:path-stability give thinness T=3D+delta of the expanded path triangle; a mark on a side is within T+B_2 of a mark on another side, so group vertices are thin with H_0=B_1(T+B_2), H=H_0+1. Replacing vertexless sides and tails by segments at distance le1, and reducing arbitrary geodesic triangles to vertex polygons, finally gives a uniform thinness constant 4H+2 for all geodesic triangles in Cay(G,S), which is word-hyperbolicity.


The closed-path statement, torsion-freeness, and word-hyperbolicity together prove the angular criterion, hence the main theorem.
