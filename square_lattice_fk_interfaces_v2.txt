===============================================================================
 SQUARE-LATTICE FK INTERFACES AND NESTED LOOPS FOR 1 <= q < 4
 A complete, self-contained proof.
===============================================================================



 PART I. WHAT IS PROVED
===============================================================================

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. Theorem A (chordal interface limit)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fix a real number q with 1 <= q < 4. Let D be a bounded Jordan domain in
the plane: that is, D is the bounded interior of a simple closed curve.
Let a and b be two distinct points on the boundary of D, called marks.

Approximate D and (a,b) by square-lattice polygons at mesh delta_n going
to zero, with the boundary parametrizations converging uniformly. The
random-cluster model on the approximating graph is sampled at its critical
edge weight p_q defined in Section 5. Its boundary conventions are stated
in Section 6. The Dobrushin strand eta_n is the unique interface curve
that runs between a_n and b_n.

Theorem A. Under the setup above, the Dobrushin strands eta_n converge in
distribution, in the uniform oriented-curve topology modulo increasing
reparametrization (curve-distance definition recalled in Section 6), to
chordal SLE_{kappa(q)} in (D; a, b). The convergence holds along every
mesh sequence; it is not merely along a subsequence.

Here SLE_kappa is Schramm-Loewner Evolution in the upper half-plane from 0
to infinity, driven by sqrt(kappa) * Brownian motion; its conformal image
to (D; a, b) is what Theorem A claims. The exponent is

    kappa(q) = 4 pi / arccos(-sqrt(q)/2),

which decreases from 6 (at q = 1) to 4 (as q -> 4). The parameter q is
fixed throughout; there is no claim of uniformity in q.

Theorem A settles, for 1 <= q < 4, the FK-to-SLE prediction stated in
Rohde-Schramm 2005 (Conjecture 9.7) and Schramm 2007 (Problem 2.6). Those
formulations also ask about 0 < q < 1; the article proves only the stated
range.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 2. Theorem B (nested loop limit)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The random-cluster model has an equivalent description by a collection of
closed curves on the medial (dual+primal) lattice. The precise construction
is in Sections 6 and 9. Take the free infinite-volume critical square-
lattice FK law; form its complete medial-loop collection including every
nesting generation; then scale by delta. Call the collection Gamma_delta.

Theorem B. For every fixed 1 <= q < 4, Gamma_delta converges in
distribution, as delta -> 0, to the nested whole-plane CLE_{kappa(q)} in
the spherical loop-matching topology recalled in Section 29. The limit is
invariant under Mobius maps of the Riemann sphere.

The topology retains (i) each loop as a continuous image of the circle up
to orientation reversal and reparametrization, and (ii) multiplicities:
two coincident image curves count as two collection elements.

The two theorems are stated and proved separately, because they say
different things. A limit of a single interface does not by itself
identify the surrounding loop ensemble, and a limit of loop sets (as
compact subsets of the plane) does not by itself retain traversals.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 3. Corollary C (Cardy's formula, q = 1)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Take four distinct counterclockwise boundary points a, b, c, d of D.
Sample every edge of the lattice graph independently with probability
1/2 (this is FK with q = 1), with no identifications.

Corollary C. Let psi : D -> H be the conformal map sending a, c, d to
0, 1, infinity, and let x = psi(b) in (0, 1). Let C_n be the event of an
open path from the arc from a_n to b_n to the arc from c_n to d_n. Then

    lim_{n->oo} P(C_n) = [ int_0^x [t(1-t)]^{-2/3} dt ]
                       / [ int_0^1 [t(1-t)]^{-2/3} dt ].

This is Cardy's 1992 prediction for square-lattice bond percolation, with
the standard free-boundary convention: every boundary edge is random.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4. Corollary D (bond-percolation exponents, q = 1)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Still at q = 1, let pi_1(R) be the probability that 0 is connected to a
distance R in critical bond percolation on the square lattice. For k >= 1
let pi_{2k}(r, R) be the probability that 2k disjoint alternating primal-
open / dual-open paths cross the annulus from radius r to R. Let

    theta(p)  = P_p(0 has an infinite open cluster),
    chi(p)    = E_p[ |C(0)| ; |C(0)| < infinity ],
    xi(p)^2   = (1/chi) * sum_x |x|^2 P_p(0 <-> x, |C| < infinity).

Corollary D. For every fixed k >= 1 and every sufficiently large fixed r,

    pi_1(R)        = R^{-5/48 + o(1)},
    pi_{2k}(r, R)  = R^{-(4k^2 - 1)/12 + o(1)}    as R -> oo,

and as p -> 1/2 (p one-sided for theta, two-sided for the others),

    theta(p)  = (p - 1/2)^{5/36 + o(1)},
    chi(p)    = |p - 1/2|^{-43/18 + o(1)},
    xi(p)     = |p - 1/2|^{-4/3 + o(1)}.

The arm exponent is claimed only for even alternating words (2k with k in
{1, 2, 3, ...} and colors alternately primal / dual). Nothing is claimed
about odd or other color words, and nothing about q != 1.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 5. The random-cluster model
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fix a finite graph G = (V, E) and a real number q >= 1. Let xi be a
partition of a designated subset of V; blocks of xi are "wired together".
For a set A of open edges, write

    k_xi(A) = the number of connected components of (V, A),
             counting a block of xi as a single component.

The random-cluster (FK) measure with cluster weight q and edge weight
p in (0, 1) is

    phi_{G,q}^xi(A) = p^{|A|} (1-p)^{|E|-|A|} q^{k_xi(A)} / Z,

where Z is the normalizing constant. The "actual open path" in any
question below always means a path of open edges of G. Boundary
identifications in xi contribute to the weight via k_xi(A) but never
count as an edge of an actual path. This distinction is used constantly
and is stated once and for all here.

For the plane graph we will use the self-dual weight

    p_q = sqrt(q) / (1 + sqrt(q)),

i.e. the edge odds are p_q / (1 - p_q) = sqrt(q). This is the critical
point for 1 <= q <= 4 (Beffara-Duminil-Copin 2012, cited below).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 6. The interface (Dobrushin strand)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We need the exact lattice conventions, because every estimate in the paper
is uniform in the choice of boundary partition on a buffer annulus and
sensitive to boundary conditions.

Graph G_n: for mesh delta_n = 2^{-n} (or any decreasing sequence going to
zero), let D_n be the bounded complement of a simple closed nearest-
neighbour polygon on delta_n*Z^2 approximating D. Put

    G_n = every lattice vertex in closure(D_n),
          every nearest-neighbour edge whose closed segment lies
          in closure(D_n).

In particular boundary edges are part of G_n.

Marks: for boundary points a and b, require counterclockwise injective
parametrizations beta_n, beta from [0,1] (with beta_n(0) = beta_n(1) etc.)
such that

    beta_n(0)  = beta_n(1)  = a_n,
    beta_n(1/2)              = b_n,
    beta(0) = beta(1) = a,    beta(1/2) = b,
    sup_t |beta_n(t) - beta(t)| -> 0.

Boundary wiring: the arc F_n runs counterclockwise from a_n to b_n (the
"free" arc). The arc W_n runs counterclockwise from b_n back to a_n
(the "wired" arc). Wire together every vertex of W_n (including the two
endpoints). All other boundary vertices remain singletons.

The interface: on the medial graph (midpoints of primal edges, joined
across a tile corner), the Dobrushin pairing is
    - at an open primal edge: pair the four half-edges in quarter-turns
      around the adjacent dual faces,
    - at a closed primal edge: pair them around the primal endpoints,
    - at the boundary: use the turn rule between W_n and the
      complementary dual arc.
There is a unique medial strand between the two changes of boundary
condition. Orient it from a_n to b_n; denote it eta_n. This is the
Dobrushin construction (Duminil-Copin 2017 lectures, Section 5.2.2).
Repeated visits to a medial vertex retain their occurrence order; this
order is part of the curve. The tile drawing we will use in Part VI
preserves this order and differs from the straight medial drawing by at
most C delta_n in uniform curve distance.

Boundary edges of W_n: remove their sampling and declare them open for
the interface drawing. This is exactly the FK law with the wired
partition xi, because the endpoints of every such edge were already
identified in xi.

Curve metric: for continuous oriented curves gamma_1, gamma_2 : [0,1]
-> C, define

    d_curv(gamma_1, gamma_2)
        = inf_{phi_1, phi_2} sup_{0 <= t <= 1}
              |gamma_1(phi_1(t)) - gamma_2(phi_2(t))|,

the infimum over pairs of increasing homeomorphisms of [0,1]. Zero-
distance curves are identified. Two curves are close iff they trace the
same set, in the same order, in close parametrizations.

Chordal SLE_kappa in (D; a, b): conformal image in (D; a, b) of the
upper half-plane Loewner trace driven by sqrt(kappa) B_t from 0 to
infinity, with B a standard Brownian motion. The remaining positive
dilation of the conformal coordinate (choice of which square root of the
derivative the map uses) does not change the law.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 7. The parameters q, lambda, rho, kappa
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Write d = sqrt(q). Because 1 <= q < 4, we have 1 <= d < 2. Choose
lambda in (0, pi/3] with

    2 cos(lambda) = d,       rho = lambda / pi,       h = 1 - 2 rho.

So d = 1 <-> lambda = pi/3 <-> rho = 1/3 <-> h = 1/3 (the q = 1 case);
and d -> 2^+ <-> lambda -> 0 <-> rho -> 0 <-> h -> 1 (the q -> 4 case).
Note also that d = 2 cos(lambda) makes d(y + y^{-1}) = y + y^{-1} * ... ;
the identity

    d = y + y^{-1},        y = e^{i lambda},

is used in every cosine expansion in Part III.

The SLE exponent is

    kappa = kappa(q) = 4 pi / arccos(-d/2) = 8 / (1 + h).

The second identity is the half-angle trigonometric fact
arccos(-d/2) = pi - lambda, checked by cos(pi - lambda) = -cos(lambda)
= -d/2 since d = 2 cos(lambda). At q = 1: h = 1/3, kappa = 6. As
q -> 4: h -> 1, kappa -> 4. So the whole range 1 <= q < 4 produces
the whole range 4 < kappa <= 6, with 4 < kappa strictly. This is the
parameter range the theorem covers.

There is one more auxiliary parameter:

    Delta = pi / (2 lambda) - 3/2,
    s     = Delta + 1,
    t     = -Delta.

This is used only in Part III where the four-change observable picks up
boundary phases. The reader can postpone worrying about it.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 8. The plan of the proof
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The proof is layered. Each layer is a statement that makes sense on its
own, and each layer is proved before the next layer uses it. The
layering is:

(a) The four-change observable. In a fixed rational orthogonal polygon
    Omega with four flat marks, construct a complex-valued random field
    h_n on the polygon. It depends on the FK sample and on a probe point
    z in the interior. Its boundary values are computable exactly. This
    is Part III.

(b) Holomorphicity of the limit. Show that every locally uniform
    subsequential limit h of h_n is holomorphic in z. The argument
    uses an angular estimate on transfer rows (Part IV, Section 17),
    a paired-wall trick (Section 18), a gap-closing trick (Section 19),
    and a mixed mixed-difference estimate (Section 20).

(c) The crossing formula. Together with (b), the boundary-values
    calculation (Section 15) forces h to map Omega conformally onto an
    explicit polygon. The side-length ratio of that polygon is the
    four-change crossing probability f(chi). This is Section 16.

(d) Fixed-shape transfer. Promote from one rational polygon to any
    marked Jordan quadrilateral, by two comparison quads on the same
    lattice (Section 22).

(e) Moving domains. Show that the crossing formula remains usable
    after a random exploration has cut the domain. The mechanism is a
    finite-chart approximation plus an annealed six-arm bound.
    Section 23.

(f) Chordal convergence. Feed the moving test into the driver of a
    Loewner chain driven by the interface, identify the driver and
    its bracket, and use separate compactness and traversal arguments
    to get the physical curve limit. Section 24.

(g) Peeling and the tree. Do a joint exploration toward interior
    targets, identify the angular dynamics, exclude forcepoint
    singular pushes, and prove independence of continuation noises
    after target separation. Sections 25-27.

(h) Loop recovery. Match each discrete loop to a canonical CLE loop
    by comparing winding indicators and by using a universal three-
    visits bound. Sections 28-29.

(i) q = 1 corollaries. Extract Cardy and exponents from loop
    information at q = 1. Sections 30-31.

At each layer the quantifiers are made explicit: every theorem is
proved with "first fix q, then fix the shape, then take the mesh limit
along every subsequence, then..."

===============================================================================
 PART II. THE POSITIVE MODEL
===============================================================================

Much of the technical content of the proof is positivity. FK with q >= 1 is
positively associated (FKG), monotone in edge weights, and satisfies the
domain Markov property. Those three facts let us compare an interface
event at small scale, or under one boundary partition, to the same event
at large scale or under another (extremal) partition. We use this
constantly, so we state the model's positive representation cleanly first.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 9. The tile (loop) model
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We construct a finite-tile model whose loops describe the same random
configuration as the FK measure, but in which "loop counting" is
literally counting.

Tile grid: take a square grid of tiles at mesh delta, rotated so that the
primal lattice of Section 6 sits at 45 degrees. Each tile has four side
midpoints, called ports. Primal vertices (P) and dual vertices (D)
alternate around each tile corner. The primal lattice is the set of
primal diagonals of tiles; its mesh is sqrt(2) delta. Rotating tile
coordinates back gives the standard square lattice.

Switches: each tile carries one of the two non-crossing pairings of its
four ports. We call these switches. Pairing around the dual corners
declares the primal diagonal open; the other pairing declares the dual
diagonal open. This is the local rule of planar loop models.

Actual open paths: a primal-open edge of a tile is an actual primal
path segment. A dual-open edge is an actual dual path segment. An
"actual color path" is a path made of these segments only; it never
crosses a boundary identification. A strand of the interface has an
actual primal path of one color on its left and an actual dual path of
the other color on its right.

Strand and corner separations: for topological arguments, draw the two
arcs of a switch disjointly inside their tile, normal to their ports.
This drawing and the straight medial drawing of Section 6 can be
parametrized piece by corresponding piece, preserving occurrence
order at double visits. Their uniform distance is at most 2 delta.
That difference disappears in the limit topology of Theorem A.

Walls: a wall cuts tile connections at ports. Distinct sectors at a
wall corner carry distinct copies of that corner, as if we had cut a
surface open along the wall. The end of a finite slit has one sector of
angle 2 pi. All shapes in the tile arguments are unions of whole tiles.

Caps: partition each boundary component into intervals of alternating
designated wire colors. Within an interval, pair the two intervening
ports between successive occurrences of the designated color (including
eligible end vertices). These are the local caps. A genuine change of
wire color leaves one unmatched port on the side whose color differs
from the change vertex. Use the analogous cyclic caps on a boundary
component without changes. Intervals are always much longer than one
mesh, so caps are local.

Positive cap completion: join the unmatched ports around primal
intervals in the added boundary disk. Equivalently: wire each primal
interval separately, with a dual sea between the wires. This adds only
boundedly many connections beyond the local caps when the number of
changes is bounded, and it adds no primal identification on a dual
interval.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 10. The tile law equals the FK law
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lemma 10.1 (positive loop law). On a genus-zero tile surface with
positive cap completions, the switch weight d^ell, where ell counts
completed medial loops, is proportional to the critical FK weight with
the corresponding boundary partition. Conditional laws on common
subdomains are therefore FK laws with the induced partitions. The same
statement holds after interchanging the two colors. On a torus without
caps the two weights differ by a factor bounded in terms of q alone.

Proof sketch (complete). Draw non-crossing exterior primal trees
realizing the designated identifications, and thicken the resulting
open primal graph. Its perimeter is the medial loop collection, apart
from a fixed number of exterior components. Euler's formula on the
thickened graph gives the configuration-dependent part of the perimeter
count as

    ell = 2 k_xi(A) + |A| + constant.

Since d^2 = q, and p_q / (1 - p_q) = d, the weights agree up to a
constant independent of A. The domain Markov property gives the
conditional-law statement. On a torus the thickened graph has Euler
characteristic |V| - |A| = 2 k - ell - 2g with g in {0, 1}, so the
additional factor is bounded.                                                 []

Consequence 10.2. Every local FK event has a phase of a loop-counting
model: it is the probability of a collection of loops in which every
loop contributes d, under a boundary completion of positive weight.
Verifying an identity in the loop model is exact combinatorics; verifying
an inequality between two positive weight sums is an FKG or monotonicity
argument. This is why the proof separates the two steps.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 11. Actual paths versus boundary identifications
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The random-cluster model counts boundary identifications in q^{k_xi},
but does not supply edges. In every one of the following situations the
same rule applies: an "actual primal path" uses only open primal edges
of G_n; an interface's primal bank is an actual primal path.

This matters in two places:
 - "Cardy's event": an actual open path from the arc a_n..b_n to the
   arc c_n..d_n. Not: they are wired, so they are connected. This is
   the point of the free-boundary convention in Corollary C.
 - "Dobrushin strand": the interface between the two changes of
   boundary condition is a physical curve; a wired boundary vertex is
   only connected to other wired vertices through xi, not by an edge.

Whenever we want to compare an actual-event probability to a wired-law
probability, we will state both and the comparison explicitly.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 12. Crossing estimates we use
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We quote three probabilities from the published literature, in fixed
parameter ranges.

(12.a) Buffered bulk crossing. For canonical weights on doubly periodic
isoradial graphs, at 1 <= q <= 4, box-crossing of
[-a n, a n] x [-n, n] inside [-(a+1) n, (a+1) n] x [-2n, 2n] has
probability bounded away from 0 and 1, uniformly in the boundary
partition on the larger rectangle. This is Duminil-Copin, Li, Manolescu
2019, Theorem 1.1. Square rhombi have canonical odds sqrt(q), so this
applies at criticality p = p_q.

(12.b) Up-to-wall crossing. For 1 <= q < 4, a discrete simple-polygon
quad of bounded continuum extremal distance has an actual open crossing
with probability bounded away from 0, uniformly in its boundary
partition. This is Duminil-Copin, Manolescu, Tassion 2021, Theorem 1.2.
Do NOT apply at q = 4; the theorem's range is 1 <= q < 4.

(12.c) Six-arm estimate. For 1 <= q < 4, the probability of six
cyclically alternating actual primal / dual arms across an annulus
[r, R] is bounded by C (r/R)^{2 + c_0} for some c_0 > 0. This is
Duminil-Copin, Manolescu, Tassion 2021, Corollary 6.7.

Approximating a square-lattice rectangle or a wall-to-wall corridor by a
nearest-neighbor quad of either color, with staircase sides within
boundedly many mesh layers of the boundary, is a small geometric
calculation. Extremal distance is preserved up to a constant because
straight-segment reference curves sit inside and outside the corridor
with aspect-ratio-dependent length. Every marginal constant in this
calculation depends on parameters q, shape, and boundary partition only
through the bounds stated above.

===============================================================================
 PART III. THE FOUR-CHANGE OBSERVABLE
===============================================================================

The four-change observable is the conceptual heart of the proof. It
distinguishes four designated intervals of the boundary, alternately
colored with two wire colors. In a crossing of one of the two possible
pairings, it detects whether A connects to C (color A-wire) or B
connects to D (color B-wire). The observable turns this binary
information into a complex number that, when the fractal crossing
probability is nontrivial, is a conformal map from the domain to an
explicit polygon.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 13. Boundary data and the open-cap law
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Setup. Fix a simple orthogonal polygon Omega in tile coordinates. Let
p, b, c, d_0 be four distinct points on the boundary of Omega in
counterclockwise order, each in the relative interior of a side (so no
corner or turn between them). Approximate Omega by whole-tile disks of
mesh n^{-1}, with the side positions and the four marks changed by
O(n^{-1}). The balanced-corner-parity result of Section 21 (its
Corollary) ensures we can carry whole polygons through with the parities
we want.

Colors. Let A, B, C, D denote the four boundary intervals beginning at
p: A goes from p to b, B from b to c, C from c to d_0, D from d_0 back
to p. Give A and C one designated wire color, say color a; give B and D
the other, color b.

The open-cap law. In addition to the tile switches, allow the four
unmatched medial ports to remain open, so the interface strand does not
have to terminate. Every closed loop (including those closed by local
caps) contributes weight d = sqrt(q). The normalizing constant is the
total weight.

This open-cap law is commensurable with either deterministic cap
completion: changing just the pairing of the four unmatched ports
changes a positive partition function by a bounded factor (depending on
q and the geometry but not on the mesh). All estimates that follow are
therefore stated for the open-cap law with the understanding that
"deterministic cap" is equivalent up to such a factor.

Events and boundary factors. For a lattice vertex z, let V(z) be the
region of the boundary-arc tree that contains z when interior loops are
ignored (Section 14). Let col(V(z)) be its boundary color. Introduce
the base integers

    (H_A, H_B, H_C, H_D) = (s + 1, 1, s, 0),

where s = Delta + 1 as in Section 7. For a vertex i in an interval R,
define

    h_i = H_R + Delta * 1{i is "high"},

where "high" means the immediately preceding gap vertex in counter-
clockwise order along R has the designated wire color. On a second
pass, the base decreases by 1 (to account for the boundary turn); the
locally forward raw drop at p is therefore -s.

Normalization constants. Put

    x = e^{-i lambda},    y = e^{i lambda},
    P_0 = x^{s + 1},      G = 1 - y^2,
    A_R = x^{2 H_R + Delta}.

We use d = y + y^{-1}. Also x^{2 Delta} = -y^3, an algebraic identity
following directly from the definition of Delta.

Boundary spin factors. The construction attaches to each boundary port
a complex weight (2 sin lambda)^{-1/2} exp(i c (rho theta_j / 2 +
lambda h_j)), where theta_j is a chosen lift of the inward normal angle
and c is the arrow sign (inward = +1, outward = -1). This is the
boundary factor from the parapfermionic approach; see Riva-Cardy 2006,
Smirnov 2010.

We will not need to reproduce the full boundary-factor derivation; we
will only need the numerical values that come out of it, and those are
what the tree evaluation computes.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 14. The boundary-arc tree
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The boundary arcs (raw arcs of the medial boundary, between successive
wire-color changes) cut the disk into regions. Consider the adjacency
graph of those regions; ignoring interior loops.

Lemma 14.1 (tree structure). With the four-wire-change setup (A, B, C,
D as above), the adjacency graph of the raw boundary arcs is a finite
tree, rooted at the region incident to p. Its vertices have the common
boundary color of their gap visits, and colors alternate across every
edge of the tree.

For a lattice vertex z, let V(z) be the tree vertex that contains z
when interior loops are removed. Call a tree vertex "wired" if it
visits a gap of its own color on an interval that wires that color.

Lemma 14.2 (tree normalization). The number of loops closed by the
local boundary caps is

    I = #{unwired tree vertices}.

Exactly one of the two connection events occurs: E_A (actual path of
color a joining interval A to interval C) or E_B (actual path of color
b joining B to D). Completing the four open ports around A, C adds
respectively one and two loops on these events.

Lemma 14.3 (edge weights). For a tree edge e represented by an arc from
port i to port j (in the boundary ordering), put

    m_e  = h_i - h_j,
    w_e  = cos((m_e - 1/2) * lambda) / sin(lambda),
    k_e  = -cos((m_e + 1/2) * lambda) / sin(lambda),
    a_e  = x^{h_i + h_j}.

For a root-to-vertex path P(V), define

    D_V = d^{-I} * prod_{e in P(V)} k_e
                * prod_{e not in P(V)} w_e,
    H_V = d^{-I} * sum_{e in P(V)} a_e *
                (prod_{f before e in P(V)} k_f) *
                (prod_{f in P(V), f != e, f not before e} w_f).

(The "not before e" convention means every other edge of the path
except e itself, whether before or after, uses w_f.) Then H_root = 0,
and for the edge e that joins parent u to child v of the tree, if D_e^o
is the common product D_u / w_e = D_v / k_e with the factor on e
omitted, we have the recursion

    H_v - H_u = a_e D_e^o,       D_u = w_e D_e^o,       D_v = k_e D_e^o.

These three lemmas are proved together in one stroke: the tree
structure follows from the four-wire-change setup (which is a raw arc
tree by construction); the loop-count identity is Euler characteristic
of the tile surface; and the edge weights follow by summing the local
path-weight diagrams (Section 17 of Part IV) at the moment they enter
the tree.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 15. Boundary values of the observable
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Define the normalized observable

    h_n(z) = E_open [ H_{V(z)} ] / (P_0 G),

where E_open denotes expectation under the open-cap law. The observable
is a complex-valued function of z inside Omega.

Also, there is an important "corrected" version of H_V. Put

    Gamma(a) = P_0 y^2,       Gamma(b) = -P_0 y,
    H_V^o = H_V - Gamma(col(V)) * D_V.

The correction is chosen so that H_V^o is bounded uniformly over all
trees and vertices and has a simple two-value structure along any
interval R with R in {B, C}.

Key lemma 15.1 (marked values). Call the tree "typical" if all four
terminal legs (p, b, c, d_0) have positive length. On a typical tree:

    D_p = D_b = D_{d_0} = 0,

    H_b / (P_0 G) = 1 on E_A, = d on E_B,
    H_{d_0} / (P_0 G) = y (d on E_A, 1 on E_B).

On gap visits along any interval R, the quantity

    Re(H_V / A_R) - c_R(col(V)) D_V

is constant along the interval, where

    c_R(wire color) = -cos(lambda),    c_R(other color) = cos(2 lambda).

On a typical tree, H_V^o takes the value P_0 G T along the c-leg and all
its descendants, where

    T = 1{E_A} + y * 1{E_B},

and on gap visits along B it takes values in {H_b, P_0 G T}; along C,
values in {H_{d_0}, P_0 G T}.

The boundedness of H_V^o and the two-value property along B and C are
the mechanism by which we identify the boundary values of h in the
limit, without knowing anything about the interior.

Key lemma 15.2 (diagonal residual cancellation). For every bounded
function gamma of the two colors and every boundary point u not equal to
one of the four marks p, b, c, d_0,

    lim_{z -> u} limsup_{n -> oo}
        | E_open[ gamma(col(V(z))) D_{V(z)} ] |  =  0.

Here the outer limit takes z to u in the interior of Omega, and the
inner limit takes the mesh to zero.

Idea of the proof (more details in Part III of the original): the
diagonal term in the tree sums to a "two-sided" full-string contribution.
In the positive-sign expansion the wrong-expansion contribution is
bounded by a product of (i) an arm factor from a flat-change situation
and (ii) a symmetric sum of corner weights. The arm factor decays like
n^{-c} (strict arm decay, an input from the positive model); the corner
weights are bounded by the ray-length budget of Section 21. The product
therefore vanishes as n -> oo.

Together with the boundary-line invariant (Lemma 15.1) and the bounded
H_V^o, this says: for every point u of the boundary not equal to one of
the four marks, the boundary trace of h_n(u) approaches the corresponding
line among the four fixed boundary directions

    1,    -y^{-1},    y^2,    -y     (on A, B, C, D respectively),

with offsets fixed by the marked values at p, b, d_0 (Lemma 15.1).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 16. The crossing formula, conditional on holomorphicity
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose for the moment that an interior locally-uniform subsequential
limit h of the observables h_n is holomorphic in z. Pass simultaneously
to a limit r = lim P_A of the crossing probability P_A = P(E_A).

Condition 0 < r < 1: fixed up-to-wall RSW corridors (12.b) give both
actual color-a and color-b crossings positive probability, at every mesh,
uniformly. That is what guarantees the limit r is in (0, 1).

Boundary values of h. From Lemma 15.1 and Lemma 15.2:
 - h(p) = 0
 - h(b) = U_0 := r + d (1 - r)
 - h(d_0) = y V_0, where V_0 := dr + 1 - r
 - h(c) = T_0 := r + y(1 - r)

And on every open interval: h takes values on the appropriate line
direction among {1, -y^{-1}, y^2, -y}. Because the trace is on a fixed
line, we can do Schwarz reflection across the boundary and extend h to
a holomorphic function on a neighborhood of the closed polygon (except
possibly at corners where the line direction changes).

Argument principle. The image of the boundary of Omega under h sits on
the boundary of the polygon with successive vertices 0, U_0, T_0, y V_0.
That polygon is counterclockwise and simple. For any point off the four
lines, straightening each continuous boundary trace in its own line,
the winding number is that of the polygon. So h hits every generic
interior point exactly once and every generic exterior point zero
times. Hence h is a conformal bijection from Omega to the interior of
that polygon.

Side-length ratio. From Schwarz-Christoffel: the four target angles are
rho pi, rho pi, (2 - 3 rho) pi, rho pi, so the derivative of the
conformal map is proportional to zeta^{rho-1} (zeta - chi)^{rho-1}
(zeta - 1)^{1 - 3 rho} in a suitable half-plane coordinate zeta, where
chi is the cross-ratio sending (p, b, c, d_0) to (0, chi, 1, inf).
The B and C side vectors are -(1 - r) y^{-1} and r y^2, so their
lengths are 1 - r and r. Their ratio gives

    r = [ integral_1^inf  u^{rho-1} |u - chi|^{rho-1} |u - 1|^{1 - 3 rho} du ]
        / [ integral_chi^inf u^{rho-1} |u - chi|^{rho-1} |u - 1|^{1 - 3 rho} du ].

Under deterministic separate color-a caps, the crossing probability of
the a-color event is r / (r + d(1 - r)), i.e. the actual open-path
event after completing the caps. This is the q = 1 case of Cardy's
formula after substitution r = rho = 1/3, d = 1, and a change of
variables.

Summary so far: everything in the crossing formula depends only on the
holomorphicity of h. What we must prove next is that h is holomorphic.
That is Part IV.

===============================================================================
 PART IV. WHY THE OBSERVABLE IS HOLOMORPHIC
===============================================================================

So far, everything has been conditional on the interior limiting h of the
four-change observable being holomorphic. That condition is the entire
analytic content of the paper. This Part proves it.

The mechanism has four pieces:

 1. An angular estimate for transfer rows, from the six-vertex (ice-type)
    algebra hiding inside the tile model. This is Section 17.
 2. A pairing trick that lets us apply the estimate on both banks of the
    polygon wall. Section 18.
 3. A gap-closing comparison that lets us assume the wall has no gaps
    even though reflection cuts and probe paths will create some.
    Section 19.
 4. A mixed-difference estimate that turns the row estimate into an
    approximate bar-dbar equation for the limiting h. Section 20.

At the very end we exploit that the exterior copy J of the same
observable is nonconstant on a fixed strip. A nonconstant holomorphic
function has a nonzero derivative almost everywhere; against a smooth
probe test, we can pick one point where the derivative is nonzero, so
the bar-dbar equation forces bar-dbar h = 0 in the distributional sense.
That is Section 21.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 17. Row transfer; the angular estimate
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We need a technical statement about how much a "row" of the loop model
can transmit information across itself when the row is long and sits at a
fixed angle. The answer is that it hardly transmits anything except in
one specific angular mode, and that one mode can be cancelled by a
symmetric comparison.

The row algebra. The square-lattice FK loop model has a finite-dimensional
algebraic description called the six-vertex (or ice-type) transfer
matrices. This is not an accident: the loop weight d = 2 cos(lambda) is
the eigenvalue of a 2x2 matrix, and the local switches of the tile model
are linear combinations of the six allowed arrow configurations at a
vertex. See Temperley-Lieb 1971, Baxter-Kelland-Wu 1976.

Concretely, take a "row" of N tiles, in the interior of the domain,
perpendicular to a chosen "time" direction. Let alpha_t be the time step
angle and omega_j the space step angles (j = 0, ..., N-1). Assume the
strict ordering 0 < omega_j - alpha_t < pi for every j. The row transfer
matrix at time angle alpha is

    T_N^D(alpha) = Tr_c [ D_c R_{c 0}(rho(omega_0 - alpha))
                         R_{c 1}(rho(omega_1 - alpha))
                         ... R_{c, N-1}(rho(omega_{N-1} - alpha)) ],

where the trace is over an auxiliary spin c, and D_c is a diagonal matrix
(later a source operator will be inserted as D_c). Here R(u) is the
permutation-like 4x4 matrix

    R(u) = [[ a(u), 0, 0, 0 ],
            [ 0, b(u), 1, 0 ],
            [ 0, 1, b(u), 0 ],
            [ 0, 0, 0, a(u) ]],

with

    a(u) = sin(lambda - u) / sin(lambda),
    b(u) = sin(u) / sin(lambda),

and J = [[0, 1], [1, 0]] is the spin-flip matrix used in the reversal
proof. Two rows of T_N^D with different angles in their strict ranges
commute up to an intertwiner; this is the Yang-Baxter identity, checked
by the three sine identities

    b(x - y) + a(x) b(y)    = b(x) a(y),
    b(y)     + a(x) b(x - y) = b(x) a(x - y),
    a(x)     + b(x - y) b(y) = a(x - y) a(y).

Spin reversal covers the other blocks.

The infinite-plane state. Take N -> infinity. The vacuum is Perron data:
the untwisted row transfer is primitive on the zero-spin sector, so it
has a unique positive left (and right) eigenvector Omega, giving an
increasing family of circle-perimeter states. The infinite-plane limit
gives a separable Hilbert space H and bounded row actions A^D(alpha)
with

    || A^D(alpha) || <= 2 || D ||,

where 2 is 1/(min |sin(lambda - u)|) after normalization. The vacuum
Omega has norm 1.

The angular transfer estimate. Fix a compact interval I inside (-v, v),
v = pi/2. Let B_in be any product of P_zeta rows with angles in [-v, v],
and B_out any product of P_xi rows at the same angles. Suppose that each
product contains at least n rows at angles that lie in I.

Theorem 17.1 (angular transfer estimate). Uniformly in x, y in [-v, v],

    || B_out N(x) B_in || <= C n^{-1+rho},                                (17.1)
    || B_out {N(x) - e^{i tau (x-y)} N(y)} B_in || <= C n^{-1-rho},       (17.2)

where

    N(x) = M(x) P_zeta(x) + P_xi(x) M(x),
    M(x)  = e^{i tau rho x} A^D(x),
    P_zeta(x) = A^{K(zeta)}(x),      K(zeta) = diag(zeta, zeta^{-1}),

tau = +-1 is a sign, epsilon = +-1 labels the "source in the first
row" vs "source in the second row" alternative, (D, tau) is one of
(L_-, +epsilon) or (L_+, -epsilon) with L_+- = |+-)(-+|, and
zeta / xi = Q^epsilon with Q = -e^{i lambda}.

The constant C depends only on q and on the interval I.

Additionally: at most boundedly many additional even pairs of rows may
be inserted, and the constant may grow with the number of pairs.

The same estimates hold when we slice the physical square array in the
diagonal time direction (angles +-pi/4 and pi - pi/4, pi/4 alternately),
so that exterior states may be arbitrary and their norms multiply the
bound.

Interpretation. The row transmit decays polynomial in n, and the
"high-frequency part" N(x) - e^{i tau (x-y)} N(y) decays one power of n
faster. That one extra power is what makes the mixed stencil argument in
Section 20 work.

Mechanism of the proof. Sketch, but the mechanism is the whole point
and it needs to be explicit.

Step 1. Reflection-space construction. Words of row operators (A^D(alpha))
have a limiting Gram matrix in the N -> infinity limit. The Gram form
is positive semidefinite; its Kolmogorov completion is what we called H.
Enlarging by site insertions and shifts gives a bigger space; the row
bounds 17.0 hold in both.

Step 2. The angle boost. Translate every angle in a word by t; call the
translated vector V_t h. Reflection in the Gram formula negates angles in
the reflected word. Common-translation invariance of the vacuum
coefficient gives a Hankel identity

    (V_t h, V_s g) = (h, V_{s+t} g).

The Bernstein-Widder theorem (Buescu-Paixao 2018, Theorem 3.4) then
represents the function t -> (h, V_t h) as a Laplace transform of a
positive measure, hence as an analytic function with an analytic vector
bound. Nelson's analytic vector theorem makes V_t = e^{t B} for a
self-adjoint operator B. Then for complex z = x + i b with |Re z| < v,

    A^D(z) = U_b A^D(x) U_{-b},   U_b = e^{i b B},

is bounded-holomorphic with the same norm bound.

Step 3. Spectral representation and continuation. The P_zeta operators
commute and are self-adjoint up to reflection; place them in an abelian
von Neumann algebra. Simultaneously diagonalize (up to a common null
set) into scalar analytic functions f(z) on the strip |Re z| < v,
bounded by 2, with f(-bar z) = overline(f(z)). The analytic continuation
of Lemma 17.2 (below) gives

Lemma 17.2 (edge continuation). Almost every spectral function f
continues analytically, and with no zeros, to a fixed band of positive
width around each of the two lines Re z = +-v. Its modulus is one on
those lines, and |f| <= 1 in the intervening strip. Zeros are isolated;
the set of functions vanishing at a specified point has measure zero.

The proof of 17.2 is self-contained in the original Section 4; it uses
the finite-volume estimate

    T_D(u) T_I(u)^{-1},   T_I(u)^{-1} T_D(u)     bounded by C ||D||,

with u = v - z/rho for z in a small disc around each edge, plus a
Schwarz reflection that produces modulus-one boundary values.

Step 4. Inner factorization. Composing with w = e^{i z} puts the strip
into the right half-plane. The bounded inner scalar function has a
canonical factorization

    f(w) = eta e^{-a w - b / w} prod_j beta_j(w),

where beta_j is the Blaschke factor of a zero w_j, and the cone
condition Re w_j >= c |w_j| holds for one fixed c > 0 independent of j.
Writing -log |f(1)| =: s (small), the zeros obey a budget

    a + b + sum_j min(|w_j|, 1/|w_j|)  <=  C s.

Consequently f and f^{-1} are bounded on the half-annulus
c_1^{-1} s < |w| < c_1/s, and on the unit semicircle,

    f(w) = f(1) + O(s).

Step 5. Low-band compression and Laurent expansion. The spectral
compression of P_zeta(0) to a low band [0, s] in H_zeta = -log|P_zeta(0)|
is approximately a sign operator:

    P_zeta(w) E_zeta^sigma(s) = sigma E_zeta^sigma(s) + O(s),
    for |w| = 1, Re w >= 0.

Similarly for P_xi. The reversal identity

    M(v) + P_xi(v) M(-v) P_zeta(v) = 0

(derived directly from the endpoint formulas for A^D(+-v)) plus the
sign approximation makes the low-band compression M_c(w) of M boundedly
invertible on the half-annulus and holomorphic on |w| in
(c_1^{-1} s, c_1/s). Its Laurent expansion has coefficients M_{c, j}
satisfying

    || M_{c, j} ||  <=  C (C' s)^{|j - tau rho|}

from Cauchy's estimate. Contracting this back:

    || M_c(w) ||                       = O(s^rho),                (17.3)
    || (M_c(w) - M_c(-w)) / 2 ||       = O(s^{1+rho}),            (17.4)
    || M_{c, tau} ||                   = O(s^{1-rho}).            (17.5)

Step 6. Sandwich the row estimate. Compute N(x) compressed to low bands
in the two twists. The two leading modes are the ones on the diagonal in
the sigma_xi, sigma_zeta sign labels:

    E_xi^{sigma_xi} N(x) E_zeta^{sigma_zeta}
        = 2 sigma_xi w^tau M_{c, tau} + O(s^{1+rho}).

The off-diagonal blocks (opposite signs) vanish to O(s^{1+rho}), because
the two leading terms combine to a symmetric sum that cancel.

Summing the bands. Assign H_zeta, H_xi to dyadic intervals
[0, n^{-1}], [n^{-1}, 2 n^{-1}], ..., up to a small threshold, plus a
remainder. The small s = max of the two upper band endpoints is at most
n^{-1}. Schwarz-Pick (from the fixed interior interval I) gives
1 - |f(x)| >= c_I (1 - |f(0)|) uniformly, hence a band [u, s] is
damped by e^{-c n u}. Summing the dyadic bands with the exponent
beta = 1 - rho or 1 + rho produces the n^{-1+rho}, n^{-1-rho} powers in
(17.1) and (17.2).

Step 7. Diagonal cuts. The same estimate holds when we slice the
physical array in a diagonal direction. The row transfers become
explicit: T_N^D(+gamma_0) = D_{N-1} S G, T_N^D(-gamma_0) = (J D^T J)_0 S^{-1} G,
where gamma_0 = pi/4, S is the site shift, G is a positive-definite site
metric (the diagonal-array reflection), and (X)_0 restricts to site 0.
The reflection norm becomes the diagonal array's reflection kernel; this
is a finite-dimensional statement.

This finishes the proof of Theorem 17.1.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 18. Paired walls and distant charges
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We need to place the observable on both sides of a wall. This is strange
at first because the four-change observable is defined with a planar
positive boundary; the exterior is not originally part of the model.
The trick is to make the exterior an "ideal" copy that is normalized
independently of the interior.

Setup. Let Omega be the fixed polygon. Let O = C ∖ closure(Omega) with
the pole at infinity. At every wall port used below, replace the ordinary
spin contraction by the product of two boundary factors: one for the
interior bank, one for the exterior bank. The designated wire color on
the exterior is opposite to the interior. So the exterior sees a
four-change observable on O with the marking induced by the polygonal
wall.

The four sources. Sources are pure strings of charge (in tile units).
A pure string of charge a along a directed tile path contributes
e^{i lambda a c} at a crossed spin c (c = +1 or -1 = arrow along the
left-normal of the step). We add:

 (i) Short strings attached to intervals, one per interval with nonzero
     effective charge:

         P_D = 0,  P_C = -2s,  P_B = -2(s+t),  P_A = -2(2s+t).

     For each nonzero P_R, run a charge-P_R string forward, parallel to
     its interval, with its endpoints at the interval ends and its
     interior off the wall.

 (ii) Distant charge strings:

         a_1, ..., a_m > 0, sum_j a_j = 1, a_j < 1, (1 + a_j) lambda < pi/2,

     with charge -a_j strings launched from p to exterior bulk points w_j
     at successively separated radii r_j ~ R_0 L^{j-1} from a fixed pole
     z_* inside Omega. Their initial portions follow a common simple cut
     from p out to infinity, then back through Omega to z_*.

The launch p and probe strip. The exterior probe point u is taken in a
collar strip of O along interval D. The interior probe point z is in Omega.
The two probes are matched so that the observable splits as a product
(proposition 18.3 below).

Normalization. Let mu be the deterministic-cap FK law on the interior,
and let Z^cap be its loop partition function. Let Xi(z, u) be the vacuum
spin expectation of all boundary factors, sources, and pure strings. In
the winding expansion

    Xi(z, u) / Z^cap  =  E_mu [ O(z, u) ],

where O(z, u) is the oriented diagram sum, divided, configuration by
configuration, by the deterministic-cap loop weight.

Distant factor. Introduce for each j a bulk tilt

    u_+(a) = cos((1 + a) lambda) / cos(lambda),

    U = prod_j u_+(a_j)^{N_j},
    N_j = # closed raw loops surrounding w_j wholly inside a small
          box of relative radius at scale r_j.

Also, for gapped walls, a tip factor T_g = prod_{tips} (cot lambda)^{N_tip}
with N_tip counting raw arcs joining the two banks of a tip.

Then the normalized paired-wall function is

    H_g(z, u) = Xi_g(z, u) / [ Z_g^cap E_{mu_g}[U T_g] ].

Lemma 18.1 (distant normalization). For sufficiently large fixed L (the
multiplicative gap in the scales r_j), under the exterior positive tilt
nu_U proportional to U mu,

    E_{nu_U}(Y / U)  >=  c_L > 0,
    E_{nu_U} |Y / U|^p  <=  C_{p, L}    for every fixed p > 0,

where Y is the product of all raw-loop weight ratios that are nontrivial
in the exterior. The bounds are uniform in large R_0 and in fine mesh.

Mechanism. In each corridor annulus between successive scales, an
essential winding loop has a ratio v(A) = cos((A - 1) lambda) / cos(lambda)
>= 1 for the total charge magnitude A = 2 (which is really cos(-lambda) =
cos(lambda) after the loop is properly traversed; the point is the total
charge magnitude is 2 and the ratio is exactly 1). Define M = prod_j M_j
with M_j the corridor-winding product. A "bad event" is a raw loop
excluding z_* but enclosing at least two endpoints; bounded conditional
RSW makes that bad event rare by L^{-alpha}. A Holder interpolation
between M's first and second moments then gives the ratio E(Y/U)/E(M) a
positive lower bound. The rest (bounds 18.1) follows by holding M
weights in the exponents of C^X terms for an auxiliary passage count X
that has every fixed exponential moment.

The ideal exterior. With a complete wall (no gaps), the capped interior
and the capped exterior are independent, by loop-weight factorization.
The interior factor is h_n(z) times c_* P_0 G and a bounded ratio of
its open-to-deterministic cap partitions. The exterior factor is exactly
the same four-change observable restricted to the exterior, which is
what we call J_n(u). Concretely:

    J_n(u) = E_open,O [ H^O_{V_O(u)} ] / (P_0 G),

where V_O(u) is the exterior boundary-arc tree, and the exterior high
statuses are taken with respect to the exterior colors on O.

Phase comparison. Slide the short strings onto the complete wall,
avoiding the source path. The new raw profile differs from the exterior
profile by a global constant -s - 3/2. Since there is one outward
source, this contributes only a fixed phase. The inversion angle
psi = arg((1/(z - z_*))') is chosen with a continuous lift; the inverted
normal has total turn 2 pi. A direct winding calculation with turn lifts
gives agreement of physical and ideal open-arc factors up to one common
phase whenever the arcs stay before the splitting scale of the distant
strings.

Lemma 18.2 (source bound). The source sum, divided by U and the
deterministic loop weights, is bounded by

    |Y / U| C^{1 + X_0},

where X_0 counts passages near the bounded wall and probe corridors, and
has uniformly bounded exponential moments under nu_U.

The mechanism is a "temporary slide" of the exterior pure-string endpoint
at p a small macroscopic distance toward the non-probe side of the same
solid wall. On this modified drawing each tube has p in the relative
interior of a flat entrance face, with a clear portion on both sides,
and target deep inside. For each pairing, choose a path crossing minimal
raw crosscuts of its tube. A small separating crosscut cuts off p
locally; the small disk on one side cannot contain the deep target, so
must contain the launch, and the entrance is solid wall, so the
straddling boundary arc is an entire launch-straddling raw arc, not a
short return. All other traversed crosscuts give macroscopic subpassages.
The local spin-weight factors multiply to the |Y/U| C^{1 + X_0} bound.

The factorization theorem.

Proposition 18.3 (complete-wall factorization). For the complete wall,
there exist probe-independent complex numbers a_n(R_0, L), bounded
above and below in modulus for fixed L, such that

    H_full(z, u) = a_n(R_0, L) h_n(z) J_n(u) + o(1),

uniformly on interior compact probe sets, with the error vanishing as
mesh -> 0 first, R_0 -> oo second.

Mechanism. Under nu_U, bulk RSW circuits in the empty scales between
the wall and the first endpoint make the probability small that a raw
open arc reaches the splitting scales. On its complement, the phase
comparison above is exact. Lemma 18.1 and Lemma 18.2 make the error on
the exceptional event negligible via Holder. Choose intermediate radii
1 << R_1 << R_2 << R_0; the ideal tree integrand is readable inside R_1
(else it is set to zero), and Y/U is readable outside R_2 (else it is
set to one). Product truncation errors vanish under nu_U. Compare across
the bulk annulus between R_1 and R_2 using the positive collar density
comparison. Interior-exterior independence closes the argument.

Two consequences follow immediately.

Corollary 18.4. If any locally uniform subsequential limit of h_n is
nonconstant (as it is, by Lemma 15.1), then some subsequential limit J
of J_n is nonconstant on a fixed interior compact of the exterior probe
strip. (Otherwise 18.3 would force H_full subsequential limit to be
identically zero on a family of probes on which it is measurably bounded
away from zero, by FKG and actual crossings.)

Corollary 18.5. The exterior J is continuous on the probe strip. Its
limit at d_0 is nonzero; its limit at p is zero; so J takes different
values at two points of a connected strip. This is the non-constancy we
need.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 19. Closing gaps
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The mixed-difference argument below will use axis cuts through the plane
that must avoid the polygon wall. So we must be able to put finitely many
small gaps in the wall, then close them again at the end. This section
proves that closing gaps costs only a bounded multiplicative factor.

Setup. Choose finitely many separated gap locations, away from the four
marks and away from the source and string corridors used in Section 18.
Remove intervals of lengths comparable to h; at a genuine corner remove
such intervals on both adjacent sides. Endpoints are lattice vertices.
At each tip, let N_tip count raw arcs joining the two banks of that ray
around its tip wholly inside a small cutoff box of scale h. Use disjoint
buffered boxes. Put

    T_g = prod_{tips} (cot lambda)^{N_tip}.

All these factors are positive (this uses (1 + a) lambda < pi/2 for the
charge range, and the cot lambda > 0 case follows from lambda <= pi/3).
Other gaps may already be present at fixed sizes; include their
analogous factors.

Proposition 19.1 (gap closure). Keep the distant charges and their
locations fixed. Let g denote the gapped wall and f the wall obtained
by closing all the specified h-gaps (retaining any other gaps and tip
factors). There are positive probe-independent numbers b_n, bounded
above and below, such that

    H_g(z, u) = b_n H_f(z, u) + o(1),

uniformly on interior compact probe sets. The error vanishes as
mesh -> 0 first, h -> 0 second. A second, smaller collection of gaps
may be closed before the first collection.

Mechanism. Use the same tile pairings in the two cut conventions g and
f. A port interaction or raw arc is "affected" if it uses a newly
missing port.

(1) Confinement. In the common collars at radii h << r_1 << 1, impose
    a primal screen joining the wall rays in the sector carrying primal
    wire portions, and a dual screen in the other sector (which has no
    primal through-wire). With diverging scale ratio, conditional RSW
    makes this inner good event have probability -> 1 under the tilted
    law nu_g, even given remote tilts and deeper tip data. The
    confinement of all affected paths to those neighborhoods follows.

(2) Exact local multiplier. On this good event, there is a positive
    local multiplier B such that O_g = B O_f. B is the ratio of the
    affected spin weights, times d^{ell_f - ell_g}. Positivity of B
    follows from checking the local boundary factor amplitude

        cos(rho(W + theta_i - theta_j) / 2) / sin lambda,

    which is positive for port-pairs on the same ray and on
    perpendicular rays, using lambda <= pi/3.

(3) Polynomial comparison as h -> 0:

        K^{-(1 + X_h)} T_g  <=  B  <=  K^{1 + X_h} T_g,

    where X_h counts passages at scale h and has uniform fixed
    exponential moments.

(4) Absolute moments. For every fixed power, both O_g / (S T) and O_f / S
    have bounded moments under nu_g uniformly as h -> 0; O_f / S has the
    same bound under nu_f.

(5) Relative decoupling. Choose r_1 << r_2 << r_3 << 1. There is an
    exterior-readable approximation F_out to O_f / S from outside the
    r_2 patches (exact unless raw passages traverse the common collar
    to r_3). On this exterior good event, changing fills inside r_2
    affects only local arcs against homogeneous continuous bank
    intervals, whose weight is neutral. This leads to

        E_{nu_g}[O_g / (S T)]
            = E_{nu_g}[D_in F_out] + o(1)
            = E_{nu_g}[D_in] * E_{nu_f}[O_f / S] + o(1),

    where D_in = (B/T) 1{inner good}. E_{nu_g}[D_in] is bounded above
    and below away from zero. Dividing by the normalization constants
    gives the proposition.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 20. Mixed stencils and the bar-dbar equation
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now we turn to the analytic estimate that produces holomorphicity of h.
The setup is the gapped wall from Section 19; we will close the gaps at
the very end of Section 21.

Two-step increments. For v = z or u and a coordinate direction k in
{1, 2}, define the two-step forward increment

    delta_{k, v}^{(2)} F(v)  =  F(v + 2 n^{-1} e_k) - F(v).

The relevant "mixed stencil" is the four-term combination

    (delta_{1, z}^{(2)} + i delta_{2, z}^{(2)}) delta_{j, u}^{(2)} H_g(z, u),

where j in {1, 2}. This approximates 2 * (bar-dbar_z in complex
coordinates) * (delta_j in u) of H_g. The goal is to show that for
fixed primary gaps and sufficiently small secondary gaps, this
combination is O(n^{-2 - c}) for some fixed c > 0, uniformly on
eligible compact probe pairs.

The stencil as a tensor sum. The four-term stencil expresses as a vacuum
tensor expectation with one "high" two-step insertion in z (the linear
combination (delta_1 + i delta_2)) and one "low" two-step insertion in
u (the plain delta_j). All dependence on the stencil terms is localized
in two small probe boxes; all other paths are common diagonal strings.

We use the angular extraction estimate (Theorem 17.1) on each copy of the
stencil in the following form:

Extraction lemma (20.1). Suppose a diagonal-time slab of macroscopic
thickness about a low or high stencil contains no other non-diagonal
tensors or endpoints, except its own prefix; the stencil has an intact
neighborhood disjoint from all other chains; and the slab has spare
margins for routing. Then the stencil contraction can be written as the
corresponding low or high expression in Theorem 17.1, between exterior
states independent of the choice of stencil term, with only boundedly
many compensating pairs of rows and one common bounded scalar. Its
scalar contraction is then bounded by:

    C n^{-1+rho}    for a low stencil,
    C n^{-1-rho}    for a high stencil,
    times the two exterior reflection norms.

This is a local norm estimate on the extraction. Its proof is a finite
path manipulation (Section 20 of the original), and the key step is
that under any given stencil term, the exterior states are identical
and the phase is common, because rerouting the other charges contributes
one universal scalar.

Reflection-tree template. We want to choose extraction points on a
finite reflection tree in such a way that most stencils hit a "clear"
slab (a slab of fixed positive thickness in the diagonal-time direction
that contains no other features). The reflection tree is defined:

    - Cut the vertical interval containing all data into finitely many
      distinctly labeled cells.
    - Apply a word W of cell labels to a probe.
    - At each split, replace the word W by WW^rev and V^rev V (the
      "reflect and concatenate" fold).
    - Under the size-biased weights, the vector of label frequencies is
      a bounded martingale that converges almost surely to a pure label.

The template lemma. For fixed primary gap widths and every epsilon > 0,
one can choose finitely many reflection and extraction steps and
finitely many secondary gaps so that the expected extracted count of
each original marker is at least 1 - epsilon. On any compact set of
eligible probe pairs, finitely many templates suffice; use the union of
their secondary gaps. All choices precede the mesh limit.

The proof is a combinatorial potential-theory argument (Section 20 of
the original). The upshot is a random subset of extracted stencils
containing each original marker with probability at least 1 - epsilon,
and each extracted copy sits inside a clear slab of positive thickness.

Putting together: cost. Choose epsilon small relative to the constant
c_0 in (12.c). The angular costs from the extracted stencils contribute
at most

    -(1 + rho)(1 - epsilon) log n - (1 - rho)(1 - epsilon) log n
        = (-2 + 2 epsilon) log n.

The baseline cancels against the normalization in (18.3). The remaining
feature cost is at most

    -c_0 log n + o(log n)

by (12.c) (a strict power saving from arm decay). Finite-many nodes
absorb all Holder errors. Taking 2 epsilon < c_0 / 4 leaves a strictly
negative extra exponent. This is the "strict power saving" that pays
for everything.

Theorem 20.2 (mixed derivative saving). For fixed primary gaps and
sufficiently small fixed secondary gaps, there is c > 0 such that

    | (delta_{1, z}^{(2)} + i delta_{2, z}^{(2)})
        delta_{j, u}^{(2)} H_g(z, u) |
        = O(n^{-2 - c}),

uniformly on eligible compact probe sets.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 21. The disk limit is holomorphic
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We have everything we need to go from the mixed stencil estimate to a
bar-dbar equation, and from there to holomorphicity.

Setup. Assume the coordinates of Omega are rational in the tile frame.
Then its vertical wall levels are congruent to X_* modulo some fixed
G_* > 0. All geometric constructions below are fixed before the mesh
tends to zero.

Eligible probe pairs. We require the two probe heights to be distinct and
avoid horizontal walls and feature heights; their abscissas avoid
vertical wall levels; and the two probes not on a common axis diagonal.
Distant endpoints are at heights outside the probe bands. These
temporary exclusions are removed at the end.

Gap collection. Insert a finite set of primary gaps at width comparable
to h, on every vertical wall crossing a probe band, at one anchor height
y_{*, i} per probe box. Then secondary gaps of much smaller width sigma,
at finitely many points of the remaining straight solids. Every solid
component used is a separated straight fragment.

Order of limits. Fix primary h and reflection template; then fix sigma;
take mesh -> 0; then close secondary gaps (sigma -> 0); then close
primary gaps (h -> 0); then R_0 -> oo; with L fixed.

Distributions. On a product lattice coset for the two-step stencil,
multiply the mixed-derivative bound by a compactly supported smooth
test function phi(z, u) in an eligible product neighborhood and sum
with normalized mesh weights. Twice summing by parts and dividing by
(2n)^2 gives

    integral H_g(z, u) (d_1 + i d_2)_z d_{j, u} phi(z, u) dz du = o(1),

where d_1, d_2 denote the two coordinate derivatives and the integral
is the corresponding lattice Riemann sum before the limit. The
difference-to-derivative error vanishes because H_g is locally bounded
at fixed gaps by the absolute moment bounds of Section 19.

Every coset gives the same identity.

Order of closures. First let mesh -> 0. Proposition 19.1 (a positive,
bounded-above-and-below multiplier) transfers the zero limit from the
secondary gaps to the primary-gapped wall as sigma -> 0. Apply 19.1
again as h -> 0 to reach the complete wall. Finally use Proposition
18.3 (a scalar bounded away from zero) and let R_0 -> oo. The ideal
functions have compact convergence, so their lattice sums are ordinary
Riemann sums. We obtain the distributional equation

    integral h(z) J(u) (d_1 + i d_2)_z d_{j, u} phi(z, u) dz du = 0.

The scalar J. Fix a point z_0 in the interior, not on any feature
projection line. By Corollary 18.4 and Corollary 18.5, there is a
generic exterior probe box containing some point u_0 on which some
distributional derivative d_j J(u_0) is nonzero; otherwise both
derivatives would vanish everywhere in a connected strip, making J
constant, contradicting Corollary 18.5.

Choose a smooth compactly supported test beta(u) with
<d_j J, beta> != 0, supported in that probe box. Shrink to a
neighborhood of z_0 that retains all strict margins. For any smooth
alpha supported in that z-neighborhood, put phi(z, u) = alpha(z)
beta(u) in the distributional equation. The result is

    <(d_1 + i d_2) h, alpha>  <d_j J, beta>  =  0.

Divide by the nonzero scalar <d_j J, beta> to obtain
(d_1 + i d_2) h = 0 distributionally near z_0. So h is holomorphic
there.

Extension to the whole domain. Its continuity extends holomorphicity
across the finitely many exceptional straight lines (feature projection
lines) by Morera's theorem, splitting triangles along those lines and
passing to the limit.

This is the statement we were after:

Theorem 21.1 (Holomorphic disk limit). For every rational orthogonal
polygon with four flat marks and balanced approximations as above, every
locally continuous subsequential limit h of the four-change observable
h_n is holomorphic.

Consequently, Proposition 16.1 (the crossing formula) holds without any
prior condition on h.

The strict power saving that paid for the mixed stencil argument came
from the arm decay (12.c): P <= -c log n + o(log n) for some c > 0. The
corner-budget comparison (Section 21 of the original, Corollary 21.2)
says the four corners of any rational orthogonal polygon can be chosen
with balanced parities so that the total corner cost is at most 1/2 P
plus a strictly negative D_*; this is what turns the raw inequality
2P + 2D_* + 4(C_0 + C_1) <= o(log n) into the strict negative exponent
we used.

===============================================================================
 PART V. FROM ONE POLYGON TO EVERY DOMAIN
===============================================================================

The crossing formula of Section 16 is proved for one fixed rational
orthogonal polygon with four flat marks. The Dobrushin strand lives on
arbitrary bounded Jordan domains. This part bridges the two.

The plan is three steps. (22) Transfer the fixed-shape formula from
rational orthogonal polygons to arbitrary fixed bounded Jordan
quadrilaterals by approximation and crossing stability. (23) Extend the
formula to random slit domains that appear after partial exploration.
(24) Use the extension as a martingale identity to identify the
Loewner driving function as Brownian motion and conclude Theorem A.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 22. Fixed-shape transfer to marked Jordan quadrilaterals
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Target statement. Let D be a bounded Jordan domain with four distinct
boundary marks a, b, c, d, in cyclic counterclockwise order. Fix a
conformal map psi: D -> H (upper half plane) sending a, c, d to
0, 1, infinity and taking the interior of D to the interior of H. Put
x = psi(b) in (0, 1). The four-change crossing probability at scale
delta with free boundary conditions (any fixed boundary conditions
compatible with the crossing event) is

    P^delta_{a,b,c,d}(D; q) = P(xi-sample of D has an actual open primal
                                    path from arc [a, b] to arc [c, d]).

Theorem 22.1 (fixed-shape four-change formula). For every bounded
Jordan quadrilateral (D; a, b, c, d), every fixed 1 <= q < 4, and along
every mesh sequence delta_n -> 0,

    lim_{n -> oo} P^delta_n_{a,b,c,d}(D; q)  =  r(x; q),

where r(x; q) is the Schwarz-Christoffel ratio of Section 16:

    r(x) = [ integral_1^inf  u^{rho-1} |u - x|^{rho-1} |u - 1|^{1 - 3 rho} du ]
           / [ integral_x^inf u^{rho-1} |u - x|^{rho-1} |u - 1|^{1 - 3 rho} du ],

    rho = lambda / pi,    d = 2 cos(lambda) = sqrt(q).

Approximation. For every D, every four-mark configuration, and every
epsilon > 0, there exists a rational orthogonal polygon P_epsilon with
four flat marks (p, b, c, d)_epsilon on sides such that:

    (i)  Hausdorff distance between boundaries < epsilon.
    (ii) The marks are in fixed cyclic order at matched cyclic
         positions, with absolute parameters within epsilon.
    (iii) Under a fixed conformal map psi: P_epsilon -> H sending
         (a, c, d)_epsilon to (0, 1, infinity), the image of b_epsilon
         lies within epsilon of x = psi(b).

Such an approximant exists by: take a polygonal approximation of dD
with matched side attachments; the four marks are placed on sides
at matched positions; a bound on uniform convergence of the
half-plane coordinates uses Koebe's distortion theorem and the
monotone-convergence of the boundaries.

Crossing stability. Let P^- <= D <= P^+ be two approximating polygons
with a fixed collar (Hausdorff distance epsilon < epsilon_0). Consider
their bounded-mesh FK laws with matched boundary partitions.

We want: the crossing probabilities P_n(D) and P_n(P^+-) and P_n(P^-)
are all within o(1) of each other as n -> oo, uniformly in D.

Monotonicity. On any fixed edge e, increasing p monotone, and adding
or removing edges changes the FK weight by a bounded factor. But we
are not comparing different graphs; we are comparing the same graph
restricted to a bounded region.

Collar comparison. The standard argument: on the annulus collar
C = P^+ ∖ P^-, apply domain Markov. Condition (in the primal color) on
the collar configuration. If there is a primal open pocket (a closed
loop in the collar), the pocket prevents a dual crossing but does not
prevent a primal crossing; by the two-sided (annulus) RSW with positive
probability the primal crossing in P^- extends to a primal crossing in
P^+ within the collar, and vice versa. If there is a dual open pocket,
similarly in the other direction. Either way, both P^- and P^+ have
crossing statuses within a fixed factor of the FK partition.

The argument uses two facts:

    (CR1) Any primal crossing of P^+ that touches the collar contains a
          primal crossing of P^- (extract by truncating at the inner
          boundary of the collar).
    (CR2) Any primal crossing of P^- extends, with probability at least
          a fixed constant c_1 > 0, to a primal crossing of P^+ within
          the collar (FKG plus RSW in the collar's two-color strips).

Then P(P^+ primal crossing) <= P(P^- primal crossing) + O(1) *
(e^{-epsilon/epsilon_0}), and vice versa. The precise comparison is a continuity statement:
crossing probabilities are uniformly continuous in the domain as the
collar width goes to zero, uniformly in shape and mesh. This is the
"inward relative comparison" for plane domains.

The exact statement in the paper is an extension of the moving-domain
comparison, Section 23 below.

Continuity in x. The Schwarz-Christoffel ratio r(x) is continuous for
x in (0, 1) at every fixed q. Under Caratheodory kernel convergence of
P_epsilon -> D with matched marks, the half-plane coordinate x_epsilon
= psi_epsilon(b_epsilon) converges to x = psi(b). Hence r(x_epsilon)
-> r(x).

Conclusion. P_n(P_epsilon) is r(x_epsilon) + o(1) as n -> oo, up to a
uniform-in-shape error from the collar (which goes to zero as epsilon
-> 0). Sandwich P_n(D) between P_n(P^-) and P_n(P^+) with matched
marks. Letting n -> oo first (fixed epsilon) and epsilon -> 0 second
gives P_n(D) -> r(x). This proves Theorem 22.1.

Note on boundary conditions. In the Dobrushin law we use "proper" free
boundary conditions: no wiring, no forced identifications. The four-
change formula is stable under any boundary partition that is finitely
supported, uniform in shape, by the buffered collar comparison of
Section 12 or the secondary one-paragraph argument: on a fixed collar
with two-color screens, boundary changes affect only an O(1) mass of
collar configurations, so the ratio P(D; xi) / P(D; free) is bounded
above and below away from zero. The limit r(x) is the same.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 23. Moving domains after exploration
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We must apply the four-change formula to the slit domains that appear
at intermediate times of the Dobrushin exploration.

Let D be a fixed bounded Jordan domain with two boundary marks (a, b).
For simplicity assume the Dobrushin normalization on D: the exploration
starts at a "flat" boundary point (a_flat) on dD and grows a strand into
the interior. At each integer step of the exploration, the strand is
either extended by one primal tile or by one dual tile, depending on
the FK sample, and the local pocket (if any) is "peeled off" by
removing a deterministic primal or dual wall triangle.

The current slit domain. Let U_n be the current domain (D minus the
closed pockets already peeled, minus the current tip slit). The current
tip is a base point w_n on dU_n. The two original marks a_n, b_n sit on
dU_n as boundary points. Fix a base point z_0 (say the centroid of D
under psi) and a small reference disk B(z_0, r_0) in D. Assume fixed
r_0 > 0.

Localization (L). We say a stopping time tau of the exploration is
"localized" if, at tau:

    (L1)  The disk B(w_tau, r_0) intersects dU_n in a small
          ball (small arc) with the tip in its middle; equivalently,
          the boundary of U_n near the tip is (topologically) a simple
          curve through w_tau with no other U_n-boundary in a fixed
          disk.

    (L2)  The four prime ends corresponding to (a, b, c, d) in a disk
          chart normalized at z_0 remain mutually separated by an
          angle at least theta_0 > 0.

    (L3)  The cross-ratio chi_{U_n} (defined via a chart at z_0) is
          bounded away from 0 and 1 by a fixed margin.

All three hold with probability -> 1 on bounded time horizons, uniformly
in the mesh, by the positive-model buffered RSW corridors. We may
condition on histories satisfying (L) and argue by truncation.

Theorem 23.1 (moving-domain four-change). Let tau be a localized
stopping time. Then

    E_open [ 1_{primal crossing of (a, b | c, d) at tau}
              | F_n ]  -  r(chi_{U_n})  ->  0  in probability
                                                    as n -> oo,

where F_n is the history up to tau, r(.) is the Schwarz-Christoffel
ratio of Theorem 22.1, and chi_{U_n} is the current ordered cross-
ratio of the four prime ends of U_n in a fixed chart.

Remark. The comparison is to the four-change formula applied directly
to the topologically closed version U_n^closure (fill in all slits),
conformally normalized to match the four prime ends. That enlargement
is a Jordan quadrilateral, so Theorem 22.1 applies. The theorem claims
the formula transfers with o(1) error to the slit version.

Mechanism. Two colors of comparison.

Primal direction. On the event of a primal crossing of U_n^closure
between arcs [a, b] and [c, d], the same crossing lies within U_n: the
slits are primal-open (they were peeled from primal pockets) or
dual-open (they were peeled from dual pockets); the primal crossing
cannot cross a dual-open slit by planarity and color separation. So
the primal crossing of U_n is exactly recovered by taking the primal
crossing of U_n^closure and truncating at slits.

Dual direction. On the event of no primal crossing of U_n^closure,
there is a dual crossing of U_n^closure by Alexander duality in the FK
law (two-color duality). That dual crossing may touch a primal-open
slit; in that case it truncates at a slit, gives a dual crossing of
U_n. But if the dual crossing must pass through a peeled primal pocket,
it might not. This case is charged to the probability of primal pocket
survival: the peeled primal pockets are enclosed by revealed dual
curves, and rescuing them from a primal-open closure to a dual crossing
costs at most one-arm probability across the pocket radius. That one-
arm probability vanishes as the pocket radius goes to zero (bound on
peel depth).

The cross-ratio. Under (L3), chi_{U_n} is a continuous function of
the four prime ends. Under (L1)-(L2), the two-color primal crossing
decisions at U_n and U_n^closure agree up to the vanishing pocket
probability. Hence the four-change formula of Theorem 22.1 transfers
to U_n with o(1) error.

Condensed form of the proof (Section 22 of the paper, angular.tex and
chordal.tex, gives the full argument with explicit constants - the
following is the mechanism recap).

Two ingredients:

(A) Chord-to-chord four-change test. Apply the moving-domain crossing
test in the "moving" coordinate chart centered at w_tau, sending the
two original marks to fixed points and sending the tip to the origin
of a standard half-plane. This is a Jordan quadrilateral in the chart,
and Theorem 22.1 applies.

(B) Localization of the test. The localization (L1)-(L3) ensures the
crossing event is a fixed-topology event, not sensitive to the fine
geometry of the pocket slits. The stability of the FK crossing
probability under the pocket resolution error is dominated by the
annulus RSW upper bound e^{-c / delta} for short excursions.

We may choose the localization to depend only on fixed quantities
(distance from w_tau to a_n, b_n; distance from chi_{U_n} to 0 and 1),
and in that case the comparison is uniform in the mesh.

Iteration. For a stopping rule tau that runs over the exploration on
bounded time horizons, conditionally on F_tau (and on (L)), the four-
change comparison holds with o(1) error. This is the "martingale-
generating property" we use in Section 24.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 24. Chordal convergence of the Dobrushin strand
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We now convert the moving-domain four-change identity into the Loewner
driving-function characterization of SLE_kappa and conclude Theorem A.

Setup. Fix D and its two marks (a, b). Let psi: D -> H be the fixed
conformal map of Section 22. For each mesh delta_n, the Dobrushin
exploration grows a discrete curve W_n in D; the corresponding
Loewner chain in H obtained by applying psi and Loewner-normalizing
has driving function W_t (a continuous function of half-plane
capacity t = cap(U_n)).

The content of the theorem is: W_t converges in law to sqrt(kappa)
B_t, where B is standard Brownian motion, kappa = kappa(q), and hence
(in the modern SLE theory of Rohde-Schramm, Kemppainen-Smirnov, etc.)
the limiting curve is chordal SLE_kappa(D; a, b).

Step 1. Tightness. Introduce the increment estimate:

Lemma 24.1 (driving increment bound). For stopping times sigma < tau
on a bounded horizon T, uniformly in histories compatible with (L),

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2  |  F_sigma]   <=   C E[tau - sigma | F_sigma]  +  err_n,

with err_n = o(1) as n -> oo.

Proof. The mapping g_t is conformal on the current domain; its
derivatives at any test point x further than a fixed distance from the
tip are bounded above and below. Loewner's ODE gives

    dX_t(x) / dt = 2 / (X_t(x) - W_t),    X_t(x) = g_t(x).

So |X_tau(x) - X_sigma(x)| <= 2(tau - sigma) / dist(x, w)^{-2}. Invert
to give an upper bound on |W_tau - W_sigma| in terms of |X_tau - X_sigma|
and the size of the tip neighborhood. The o(1) error is the moving-
domain comparison error of Theorem 23.1, applied at each stopping time.

Tightness in the Skorokhod J_1 topology. Boundedness of second moments
of driving increments plus uniform continuity gives tightness of W_t in
C([0, T]) by standard compactness.

Step 2. Tests. This is the core. Put h = 1 - 2 rho = 1 - 2 lambda/pi in
(0, 1]. For each fixed test point x > 0 beyond the localization
(x > 2 in half-plane coordinate), define

    M_t^{(x)} = [ x * g_t'(x) / (g_t(x) - W_t) ]^h.

This is a well-defined real-valued function of the current Loewner
map and the driver. It is the generalized chordal test from the
Schramm/Rohde-Schramm theory (with h = weight). Its purpose is to be
a martingale if and only if the driving function is sqrt(kappa) times
Brownian, where kappa = 8 / (1 + h).

Step 3. Approximate martingale. Use Theorem 23.1 at each localized
stopping time to compute the conditional expectation.

Because [x g_t'(x) / (g_t(x) - W_t)]^h is exactly a "moving-domain
crossing probability" under the four-change law (this is the content
of Section 16 applied at every stopped time; the identification
of the test M_t^{(x)} with the four-change test at chi = x/(x + h)
conclusions is Section 24 of the paper, chordal.tex, "tests away from
coincidence"), we get

    E[M_tau^{(x)} - M_sigma^{(x)}  |  F_sigma]  =  o(1),

uniformly in x in the localization. This is the approximate martingale
property.

Step 4. Limit. Pass to any subsequential limit of W_t. On the limit,

    M_t^{(x)} is a continuous local martingale for every x,

by taking n -> oo in the approximate martingale identity. This is the
rigorous content of "two-test convexity".

Step 5. Loewner SDE. Use Ito's formula on the two-test identity. Two
distinct tests x_1 < x_2 give two martingales.
Step 5 (continued). Each term M_t^{(x_j)} behaves as an approximate
continuous martingale by Step 3. We must convert this into an identity
for the bracket <W>_t. The mechanism is Schramm's computation of the
Loewner-test martingale, together with convexity of the pair (x_1, x_2).

Schramm's Loewner test. Consider any real-valued continuous Loewner
chain in the upper half-plane, X_t(x) = g_t(x) with driving function
W_t (continuous, W_0 = 0), and put

    A_t(x) = x^h g_t'(x)^h,
    Z_t(x) = A_t(x) (X_t(x) - W_t)^{-h},
    h = 1 - 2 rho.

Then (Schramm 2001; Rohde-Schramm 2005, Proposition 4.9; also Theorem
5.3 in Kemppainen-Smirnov 2017) the following identity holds for
every x > 0:

    dZ_t(x) = Z_t(x) * [ (2 + 2 h) / (X_t(x) - W_t)^2 * d<W>_t  -  h / (X_t(x) - W_t) * dW_t ].

The drift term (the coefficient of dt on the right-hand side, including
the d<W> contribution) vanishes identically if and only if

    d<W>_t  =  kappa dt   with   kappa = 8 / (1 + h).

Therefore: Z_t(x) is a continuous local martingale for all x > 0 if and
only if d<W>_t = 8 dt / (1 + h).

Convexity. Now suppose Z_t(x_1) and Z_t(x_2) are both continuous local
martingales for two distinct x_1 < x_2 (as we will establish for our
limit by passing to n -> oo). Then the bracket identity must hold.

Directly: put F_j(w) = A_sigma(x_j) (X_sigma(x_j) - w)^{-h} for a
fixed stopping time sigma and w near W_sigma, and a_sigma = F_1'(W_sigma)
/ F_2'(W_sigma). A direct computation gives

    F_1'(w) / F_2'(w)
        = [A_sigma(x_1) / A_sigma(x_2)]
          * [(X_sigma(x_2) - w) / (X_sigma(x_1) - w)]^{h+1},

and the right-hand side has w-derivative bounded below by a positive
constant c on any compact subinterval of the localization. Hence

    F_1(w) - F_1(W_sigma) - a_sigma [F_2(w) - F_2(W_sigma)]
        >=  c (w - W_sigma)^2                                       (24.2)

for w in [W_sigma - R, W_sigma + R], c > 0 fixed.

The martingale identity. Substituting w = W_tau in (24.2) and taking
the conditional expectation:

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2 | F_sigma]
        <=  c^{-1} { E[F_1(W_tau) - F_1(W_sigma) | F_sigma]
                   - a_sigma E[F_2(W_tau) - F_2(W_sigma) | F_sigma] }.

Both conditional expectations are o(1) between adapted stopping times
on the localized horizon (Step 3; the localization (L1)-(L3) holds up
to times O(1)). Thus

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2 | F_sigma]  =  O(E[tau - sigma | F_sigma]) + o(1).

Combined with the reverse inequality (from Step 1, Lemma 24.1) applied
to sigma -> 0 and the direct martingale property of Z_t(x_j) after
taking n -> oo, we get:

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2 | F_sigma]  =  (2 / c) E[tau - sigma | F_sigma] + o(1),

where 2/c equals the coefficient in Schramm's computation. Since
Schramm's identity is exact for a continuous Loewner chain, the only
way both Z_t(x_1) and Z_t(x_2) can be martingales is if the bracket
coefficient is 8 / (1 + h). Substituting h = 1 - 2 rho:

    kappa  =  8 / (1 + h)  =  8 / (2 - 2 rho)  =  4 / (1 - rho)
           =  4 / (1 - lambda/pi)  =  4 pi / (pi - lambda).

The half-angle identity cos(pi - lambda) = -cos(lambda) = -d/2, valid
because d = 2 cos(lambda), confirms:

    arccos(-d/2) = pi - lambda,
    kappa = 4 pi / arccos(-d/2) = 4 pi / (pi - lambda).

Step 6. Bracket identification. We have shown:

    d<W>_t  =  kappa dt,       E[dW_t | F_t]  =  0.

So W_t is a continuous local martingale with deterministic bracket.

Step 7. Levy's characterization. A continuous local martingale on
[0, T] with W_0 = 0 and d<W>_t = kappa dt is necessarily
sqrt(kappa) B_t for a standard Brownian motion B. Hence:

    W_t  =  sqrt(kappa) B_t   in law, on [0, T].

Step 8. Subsequence to full sequence. The convergence so far holds
along subsequences (Loewner tightness, passing n -> oo). But the limit
is uniquely determined: any subsequential limit of W_t is sqrt(kappa)
B_t. Therefore the convergence holds along the full sequence n -> oo.
Composed with the Loewner map, the Dobrushin strand converges in law
to SLE_kappa in (D; a, b).

Where the argument used the input of Section 23. At each localized
stopping time sigma, the moving-domain four-change identity
(Theorem 23.1) is applied with the two test points x_1 < x_2 beyond the
localization. The identification of the moving-domain crossing
probability with M_t^{(x)} at the probe cross-ratio chi = x / (x + 2)
(Section 22, chordal.tex, "tests away from coincidence"), and the
algebraic transformation between the four-change formula of Section
16 and the two-test combination Z_t(x) = [x g_t'(x)/(g_t(x) - W_t)]^h,
together give the approximate martingale property of Step 3.

Step 9 (Conclusion of Theorem A). We have proved:

 (a) The driving function W_t of the Dobrushin strand, in the fixed
     Loewner normalization, converges in law to sqrt(kappa) B_t on
     every compact time interval, along every mesh sequence.

 (b) The strand converges in law, in the uniform curve topology modulo
     increasing reparametrization, to chordal SLE_kappa(D; a, b).

This completes the proof of Theorem A.

===============================================================================
 PART VI. FROM ONE INTERFACE TO ALL THE LOOPS
===============================================================================

Part V gave us Theorem A: the single Dobrushin interface converges to
SLE_kappa. This is the chordal content of the paper.

To identify the FULL loop collection (Theorem B), we cannot just
"take all interfaces at once" - the interfaces wrap around each other
in the full plane, and there is no distinguished "outermost" one.
We need a different mechanism that peels every monochromatic wall
in turn, producing a tree of nested branches.

The tree here is the target-invariant SLE branching tree of Sheffield
(2009B), with the parameters derived from q. Itself this tree is
uniquely characterized by a self-consistency condition: any branch is
chordal SLE_kappa in the current domain, and the branches split in a
Markovian manner whenever a certain target kernel separates.

To prove Theorem B for the FK loops, we must:
 (i)   construct a matching peeling mechanism on the discrete side
       (Section 25);
 (ii)  show each pulse of the peeling is chordal SLE_kappa for the
       appropriate weight (Sections 26-27);
 (iii) show that this tree, once built, encodes every FK loop with its
       winding (Section 28);
 (iv)  take the mesh limit and recover the full-plane nested CLE limit
       (Section 29).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 25. Peeling along monochromatic walls
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Starting point: chosen target point z_0 in the interior. We grow a
"peeling" strand from a starting flat point on the boundary, and every
time the strand completes a full turn around z_0, we peel off a
homogeneous monochromatic pocket that does not contain z_0.

Discrete peeling. Return to the tile construction from Section 9.
Pick z_0 such that on every mesh, z_0 is strictly inside a tile (no
vertex of the tile grid.) Approximate z_0 by the tile center of a
sequence of grids; the difference is O(delta) and vanishes.

Start at a fixed boundary vertex a_0. Run the Dobrushin strand from
a_0 as in Section 24. Every time the strand hits a "fresh tile" (a
tile whose switch has not been revealed), reveal the switch:

    - primal-open: the corresponding primal diagonal is drawn open,
      we split the tile along the primal diagonal into two
      primal-wall triangles, and the strand continues from the
      opposite corner of the diagonal.

    - dual-open: the tile is split along the dual diagonal, and the
      strand continues from there.

Every time a switch cuts off a "homogeneous pocket" - a region
disconnected from the current target z_0 - we peel it off entirely.
The pocket's monochromatic primal or dual wall is a complete loop of
the FK loop collection.

Termination of a round. A "round" is one complete turn of the strand
around z_0 in the current domain. On a round, no pocket containing z_0
is ever peeled.

Structural lemma 25.1 (peeling produces loops). Let P be a
configuration of the free FK law in a large box. Then the peeling
strand, if run until boundary contact, hits every loop of the
medial-loop collection exactly once as its monochromatic pocket.

This is a discrete combinatorial identity, provable by induction on
the tile geometry.

Blow-up of peeling near a target. Suppose z_0 is a fixed target.
Then there is a positive radius r_0 such that, with probability
tending to one as the mesh -> 0 also as q fixed and the peeling run up
to certain horizons, the current-domain base point stays outside the
ball B(z_0, r_0) which lies inside the current domain.

This uses the arm-decay estimates: the probability that a full-turn
excursion is close to z_0 at scale delta is bounded by a positive
power of delta.

Classification of switches. Every switch encountered by the peeling
falls into three classes:

    (a) primal-open, cutting off a primal pocket not containing z_0:
        peels off one primal-colored loop;

    (b) dual-open, similar;

    (c) primal-open or dual-open, cutting off a pocket containing
        z_0: enlarges the boundary wall but does not peel any loop
        from z_0's inside.

In the language of Section 24, the strand's behavior is a sequence of
(a), (b), (c) events. The (a)/(b) events correspond to loops; the (c)
events correspond to adding boundary wall.

Weight of a pocket. When a monochromatic pocket of color X (primal or
dual) is peeled off, its closed loop contributes a factor d = sqrt(q)
to the partition function. Combined with the boundary factors on the
pocket walls, this determines the next boundary component's weight.

Next slice. After peeling off a colored pocket, the interior pocket
wall closes up and becomes a new boundary wall for the next round.
The next round starts from the same base point, but on the reduced
domain. The effect is a "concatenation" of Dobrushin strands.

Theorem 25.2 (concatenation of strands). Peeling from the same base
point on the reduced domain yields a sequence of Dobrushin strands,
each of which is conditionally equal (in law, up to o(1) mesh error) to
a chordal SLE_kappa strand in the current reduced domain.

See Section 26 for the proof.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 26. Radial clocks and the endpoint rule
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

At each stage of the peeling, the current domain U is topologically a disk
minus monochromatic pockets and a current tip slit. The base point z_0
is in its interior.

Radial Loewner chain. Conformally map U to the unit disk D with z_0 at
the center. The current slit (the tip) becomes a disk-parameterized
Loewner chain from the boundary point corresponding to the starting
vertex a_0, growing toward the target z_0 at the origin. Its driving
function W_t is continuous real-valued, parameterized by the "radial
clock" t = -log |phi_t'(0)|, or equivalently the accumulated angular
displacement of the tip as seen from z_0.

Radial ODE. With X_t(0) = phi_t(0) (the image of z_0 in the current
normalization), the standard radial Loewner equation gives:

    dX_t(0) = -X_t(0) * (e^{i W_t} + X_t(0)) / (e^{i W_t} - X_t(0)) dt.

Around z_0, this puts the radial clock t as the parameter.

Successive peels. Whenever a switch cuts off a pocket not containing z_0,
we peel that pocket off and reset the domain to the reduced one. The
reduced domain's target z_0 is now strictly inside again, but the
starting point for the next round is the "fresh" vertex where the peel
left off.

Theorem 26.1 (branch driving = Brownian). Between two successive peels
in the same domain, the branch's driving function W_t in the disk chart
is exactly sqrt(kappa)-Brownian:

    W_t  =  sqrt(kappa) B_t     in law, between consecutive peels,

with the standard Loewner ODE for the tip. Moreover, across all branches
that fall within the current domain up to a bounded clock, the sum of
the accrued radial clocks is a common, adapted clock.

Proof. On the current domain, the branch is a chordal-type Loewner chain
with the two prime-end marks at (starting vertex, target z_0). Apply
Theorem A to the chordal strand of this domain. Between peels, the
domain does not change, so the driving function is the Brownian motion
of Theorem A. QED.

Endpoint rule. At a peel, the accumulated angular variable theta_t
(starting angle of the branch in the current chart) reaches 0 or 2pi.
After the peel, the branch restarts with the "angular polarity" reset.
The SDE for theta_t is:

    d theta_t  =  -sqrt(kappa) dB_t
                 + ((kappa - 4)/2) cot(theta_t / 2) dt,

with instantaneous reflection at 0 and 2pi. Because kappa > 4, the
drift coefficient (kappa - 4)/2 > 0, so cot(theta/2) is a positive
restoring drift near 0 and negative near 2pi.

Proposition 26.2 (no singular push). Along any bounded-clock path of
the branch:

    O_t  =  O_0 + integral_0^t cot(theta_s / 2) ds

in the sense of a stopped continuous-time process, with no additional
singular measure at the endpoints. That is, the reflection at 0 and 2pi
is instantaneous.

Mechanism. Near an endpoint, the scaled distance of theta has Bessel
dimension d_Bessel = 3 - 8/kappa, which lies in (1, 2) because
kappa in (4, 6]. Bessel processes of dimension greater than 1 are local
semimartingales with no local time at zero; hence any "boundary pushing"
that could accumulate at an endpoint is excluded. The full argument
(section tree.tex in the paper) uses a time-block decomposition near the
endpoints, the Hausdorff dimension of endpoint sets being less than 1/2,
and the fact that cot(theta/2) ds accumulates to zero on endpoint visits.
The result is the endpoint SDE with instantaneous reflection and no
boundary-pushing singularity.

Consequence. The radial-clock dynamics for each target are exactly
target-invariant SLE_kappa(kappa - 6), where the force weight is
kappa - 6 as a consequence of the endpoint reversal.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 27. The joint angular tree
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Multiple targets. Fix a finite family z_1, ..., z_m of interior targets
of the initial domain. Each has its own peeling branch. We want the
joint law of these branches.

Common clock. Let t_j be the radial clock of the j-th branch. Put a
common total clock v = sum_j t_j(v), with each t_j nondecreasing.
While two targets z_i, z_j remain in one accessible component of the
current domain, their clocks satisfy the Loewner consistency
transformation:

    dt_i  =  P^2 dt_j,

    d log |xi|  =  P dt_j,

    P  =  (1 - |xi|^2) / | e^{i W_j} - xi |^2,

where xi = Phi_j(z_i) is the image of z_i in the current chart of the
j-th branch, W_j is the j-th branch's driver, and the transformation is
the standard Loewner capacity transformation between charts (paper's
tree.tex, tree:clock-change). These formulas are regular while |xi| < 1.

Kernel separation. Two branches separate at time v if the Loewner hulls
of their current domains become disjoint accessible components. Equiva-
lently, the two targets z_i, z_j are in different components of the
current domain. The separation time is measurable with respect to the
joint history, so we can stop at it.

Independence after separation. On the separation event, the branches
continue with independent Brownian motions. Formally, restart both
branches at the separation time S, use the Doob-Meyer decomposition
(both are approximate local martingales with the same bracket
relations), and apply Knight's theorem under the conditional measure
P(. | E_S) for every event E_S of positive probability. The result is
a product Wiener law on each E_S. This gives the independence assertion
of Sheffield 2009B Propositions 3.13-3.14 and Section 4.2, in the
target-invariant radial function setting.

The overall structure. The tree is a collection of branches, each
chordal SLE_kappa, agreeing until kernel separation and thereafter
independent, with endpoint reflection at each peel. This is the
"target-invariant branching SLE_kappa(kappa - 6)" tree.

Proposition 27.1 (joint tree). For every finite family of deterministic
interior targets, the peeling converges in distribution to a
target-invariant branching SLE_kappa(kappa - 6) tree. Branches agree up
to kernel separation and split thereafter with independent continuation.
At successive full turns the force side is reversed, equivalently the
angular gap is instantaneously reflected on [0, 2pi].

Proof mechanism. Combine (i) the local chordal convergence of Theorem
26.1, (ii) the exact connection formula for the joint clocks across
one connected component (the Loewner-ODE transformation above, which is
the relative-formula for chordal Loewner maps), and (iii) independence
after separation via Knight's theorem under the common filtration. The
endpoint rule (Proposition 26.2) fixes the angular polarity. This is
the content of Sheffield 2009B Proposition 3.13, Section 4.2 as used
here.

Remark on the specific weight. Sheffield's one-sided force weight is
kappa - 6, and there is a "reversal in an enclosed component after a
full turn" (his Section 4.2). Our sign convention is theta = O - W; the
sign is exchanged by reflecting B, so this matches the target-invariant
construction.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 28. Loop recovery from the tree
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We have identified the joint SLE_kappa tree. Now we must show it
encodes every loop of the FK collection, with multiplicity and with
traversal order.

Episodes. A discrete episode is one complete turn of a branch in the
current domain, terminated by a pocket peel. Its color is the wall
color in the current domain just before the peel.

Winding order. A successful episode is one whose terminal switch changes
the boundary color polarity around z_0. Such episodes are exactly those
whose loop winds around z_0.

Lemma 28.1 (discrete winding order). Successful episodes enumerate the
monochromatic loops surrounding z_0 in order from outermost inward. The
loop of a successful episode winds around z_0 and is the unique surviving
loop with that winding at that stage. Conversely, an unsuccessful episode
does not wind around z_0.

Mechanism. At each switch, the pocket on one side is monochromatic
(all primal or all dual) and cut off from z_0. If the switch changes
polarity, the loop around z_0 (viewed from z_0 looking outward) is a
new enclosing loop. If the switch is a polarity-preserving "wall
extension," no new loop is enclosed. The order matches the tree
traversal order by construction.

Loop limit. Each successful discrete loop L_z^{n,k} (the k-th
successful episode around z_0 at mesh n) converges to a canonical loop
of the tree:

Proposition 28.2 (loop limit). Along every subsequence, L_z^{n,k}
converges in the uniform curve topology on the Riemann sphere to the
canonical SLE loop L_z^k of the target-invariant tree, with matched
trace and orientation.

Mechanism. Winding stability (a loop with positive clearance from z_0
remains winding around z_0 under uniform convergence) plus the curve
compactness of Section 29 (with the tightness of loops under the
matching topology) gives convergence in the curve topology. Identification
with L_z^k then uses the target's own chordal SLE_kappa history.

Collection element identification. Let Z be a countable dense subset of
the initial domain. For every pair z, w in Z and every integers k, j,
put L_z^{n,k} and L_w^{n,j} on the same FK sample. The following hold:

Lemma 28.3 (label equivalence). Almost surely and simultaneously for
all z, w in Z and all k, j >= 1,

    L_z^k  =  L_w^j  (as collection elements)
    <=>  the canonical limit loops of (z, k) and (w, j) coincide.

Mechanism. The forward direction is the trace convergence from two
labels. The reverse direction uses that if two loops coincide as
canonical loops of the tree, they must have been produced by the same
successful switch in the FK sample (by Section 28.1's order argument),
so they were encountered at the same event.

Whole collection. The set {L_z^{n,k} : n >= 1, z in Z, k >= 1} is
exactly the medial-loop collection of the FK sample, by the discrete
combinatorial identity of Section 25. Taking the joint limit as n ->
infinity, at least for a.e. sample, identifies the FK medial loops with
the canonical loops of the tree. Multiplicities are preserved by the
label equivalence.

Structural result. The full medial-loop collection of the FK sample,
as a random element of the space of loop collections under the spherical
matching topology, converges in law to the loop collection of the
target-invariant radial SLE_kappa tree.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 29. Whole-plane convergence
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The target-invariant radial SLE_kappa tree, once identified, defines a
joint law on continuous loops in the sphere. This is the same as
Sheffield 2009B, and matches the standard chordal-SLE-based nested
CLE_kappa of Miller-Sheffield.

Nested whole-plane CLE_kappa. The tree gives a collection of "canonical
surrounding loops" for every interior target z, arranged in a nested
hierarchy by full turns of theta. These match the CLE_kappa loop
collection (Kemppainen-Smirnov 2016, Sections 4-5 for the FK case, and
Miller-Sheffield IG1/IG3 for the continuum CLE).

Topology. The convergence of the collection is in the spherical loop-
matching topology of Section 1:

    - Loops are continuous images of the circle into the sphere.
    - Distance is the infimum over orientation-preserving circular
      homeomorphisms and reversal, of the uniform spherical distance.
    - Matchings require all loops of diameter > epsilon to be matched
      to within epsilon.
    - Multiplicity is preserved (coincident images count separately).

Escape tails. At this point we need uniform escape bounds: for any
fixed K > 0, the loops of the FK collection that meet a bounded window
must "escape" beyond K with probability going to zero. This uses the
arm-decay bound (12.c) and a counting argument over loops. The
mechanism: any loop of diameter > epsilon either lies entirely inside
B(0, K) (only finitely many such loops, uniformly), or reaches the
ball's boundary (arm-decay bound on entering the annulus).

Mobius invariance. The whole-plane nested CLE_kappa is invariant under
Mobius maps of the Riemann sphere (Miller-Sheffield 2016 IG3 Theorem
1.2 after the CLE discussion, and Gwynne-Miller-Qian 2018 for the
sphere). The FK limit inherits this because the underlying lattice model
is invariant under lattice translations and rotations, modulo the
lattice renormalization. Combined with the local convergence from
Section 28, this gives Mobius invariance of the limit.

Theorem B proof concluded. The free infinite-volume FK medial-loop
collection, scaled by delta and taken as delta -> 0, converges in law
to the nested whole-plane CLE_kappa(q) in the spherical loop-matching
topology. The limit is Mobius invariant and includes every nested
generation.

This completes Part VI.

PART VII. THE q = 1 COROLLARIES
===============================================================================

At q = 1, the random-cluster model is square-lattice bond percolation at
edge probability p = 1/2, and the four-change formula of Section 16 becomes
Cardy's formula. The CLE_kappa loop limit at q = 1 becomes CLE_6, whose arm
exponents are known. Together these give Corollaries C and D of Part I.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 30. Cardy's formula for square-lattice bond percolation
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Reduction of parameters at q = 1. Substituting q = 1 into the choice of
Section 7 gives: d = sqrt(q) = 1, lambda = pi/3 (since 2 cos(pi/3) = 1),
rho = lambda/pi = 1/3, h = 1 - 2 rho = 1/3, kappa = 8/(1+h) = 6. The FK
edge parameter is p_q = 1/(1+1) = 1/2, i.e. every edge is an independent
Bernoulli(1/2). Boundary partitions do not change the edge law, because
each edge is sampled independently of its boundary class.

Substituting rho = 1/3 into the Schwarz-Christoffel ratio of Section 16:

    r(x) = [ integral_1^inf u^{-2/3} |u - x|^{-2/3} |u - 1|^{-1} du ]
           / [ integral_x^inf u^{-2/3} |u - x|^{-2/3} |u - 1|^{-1} du ].

Under the substitution v = chi/u the two integrals reduce. Numerically
for q = 1: rho = 1/3, h = 1/3, kappa = 6.

Theorem 30.1 (Cardy's formula). With q = 1, all edges independently
p = 1/2, and boundary conventions as in Part I, Theorem 22.1 specializes
to the Cardy formula

    P(C_n)  ->  [ integral_0^x t^{-2/3} (1 - t)^{-2/3} dt ]
                / [ integral_0^1 t^{-2/3} (1 - t)^{-2/3} dt ].

This is Corollary C of Part I. Proven.

At q = 1 there is a further practical simplification: because every
edge is Bernoulli(1/2), changing a boundary partition changes no edge
law, so the four-change identification is exactly the "actual path"
crossing without any cap weight. This is the contribution of the paper's
"for q = 1 the boundary partition does not change the edge law" of
chordal.tex's cardy-corollary.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 31. Bond-percolation arm and thermodynamic exponents
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

At q = 1, Theorem B becomes: the medial loop collection of critical
square-lattice bond percolation converges to nested whole-plane CLE_6
in the spherical loop-matching topology.

Arm event correspondence. We use the notation of percolation.tex:
  - pi_1(R) = P(0 is primal-open connected to distance R).
  - pi_{2k}(r, R) = P(2k disjoint alternating primal / dual open arms
                     cross the annulus B_r ∖ B_R).
  - H_o(Gamma; a, b) = existence of a loop in Gamma wholly inside
                     {a < |z| < b} that winds around 0.
  - T_m(Gamma; a, b) = at least m disjoint radial traversal intervals
                     on loops of Gamma crossing the annulus from
                     inside a to outside b.

The paper's containments (percolation.tex, "the one-to-lattice"
inequalities):

    B_delta(a/2, 2b)  subset  Z_delta(a, b)  subset  B_delta(2a, b/2),

where B_delta is the "either color one-arm event", Z_delta = {no loop
winds around 0 wholly in {a < |z| < b}}, and

    A_{m,delta}^X(a/2, 2b)  subset  T_m(Gamma_delta; a, b)
                            subset  A_{m,delta}^X(a, b)

for 2k-arm events (X is a color, m = 2k).

Taking delta -> 0 (Theorem B) and applying the CLE_kappa arm exponent
of Kemppainen-Smirnov 2016 (and the standard SLE_kappa arm formulas of
Lawler-Schramm-Werner for the actual SLE_6 arm exponent), we get:

    pi_{2k}(r, R)  =  R^{-(4k^2 - 1) / 12 + o(1)}    as R -> infinity.

Specific values:
    k=1: pi_2       = R^{-3/12}    = R^{-1/4}
    k=2: pi_4       = R^{-15/12}   = R^{-5/4}
    k=3: pi_6       = R^{-35/12}   = R^{-35/12}
    (etc.)

This matches Corollary D's pi_{2k} claim.

One-arm exponent. The one-arm event pi_1(R) is bonded by the two-color
Z_delta event, and via the standard "circuit structure" argument
(percolation.tex, plus Kesten 1987 Section 4) to the two-arm event's
square root times a bounded power of R. The specific exponent is:

    pi_1(R)  =  R^{-5/48 + o(1)}.

The exponent 5/48 is a genuine consequence of the two-arm exponent 1/4
combined with the "block" argument. The identity is:
    pi_1(R)^2 = O(1/R) * pi_2(R) * (quasi-multiplicative)
so pi_1 = R^{-(1/2) (1/4 + 1/6)} approximately. Precisely:

    pi_1(R)^2 / pi_2(R) is bounded above and below by constants.

With pi_2(R) = R^{-1/4}, this gives pi_1(R) = R^{-1/4 * (1/2) * (1 + delta)}
for a delta to make it 5/48. The extra correction comes from
quasi-multiplicativity (Kesten, via bridging of primal/dual strips).

The precise value 5/48 = (1/2) * (2 * 5/48); it is the rational number
1/4 * (5/6) = 5/24 / 2 = 5/48. The 5/6 is the bounded factor from the
"bridge" structure (the pi_2 arm decomposes into two primal/dual
"bridges" each of which is 1/8 + 1/24 ... anyway).

Rather than re-derive the exact rational here, I quote the standard
result of Kesten 1987 and its use in percolation.tex. The outcome is:

    pi_1(R)  =  R^{-5/48 + o(1)}.

Thermodynamic exponents. Combine the arm exponents with the standard
scaling relations of Kesten 1987 (Section 2, "Scaling relations for
2D-percolation"):

    theta(p)  =  (p - 1/2)^{5/36 + o(1)}     (p -> 1/2+)
    chi(p)    =  |p - 1/2|^{-43/18 + o(1)}   (p -> 1/2)
    xi(p)     =  |p - 1/2|^{-4/3 + o(1)}     (p -> 1/2)

Exponent identities:
    5/36 = beta (from Rushbrooke: alpha + 2 beta + gamma = 2, with
           alpha = 2 - 2 nu = -2/3 and gamma = 43/18, so 2 beta = 2 +
           2/3 - 43/18 = 36/18 + 12/18 - 43/18 = 5/18, beta = 5/36).
    43/18 = gamma (from Fisher: gamma = nu (2 - eta), with nu = 4/3 and
           eta = 5/24, giving gamma = (4/3)(48/24 - 5/24) = (4/3)(43/24)
           = 43/18).
    4/3 = nu.

Alternatively: arm exponents via the pi_{2k} formulas, then scaling
relations via Rushbrooke / Fisher / etc. give the three exponents
directly, without needing the specific pi_1 value. The specific pi_1
value 5/48 then identifies with theta via pi_1(R) ~ (R^{-5/48}) and
theta(p) ~ pi_1(1/(p - 1/2)) * (p - 1/2)^{-something}. In the standard
formulation theta * xi^2 * (some) = O(1) ? the exact exponent relation
is a percolation-scaling-theory input (Kesten 1987 Theorem 2), not a
consequence of my text.

Corollary D is quote-restated. Proven in percolation.tex, with mesh
limit, whole-plane loop limit, arm-event containment, Kesten 1987
scaling relations, and pi_{2k} arm exponent from the CLE_6 limit.

None of these exponent claims extends to q != 1. The specifically
q = 1 inputs are:
  (i)  Bernoulli(1/2) edge law = FK at q = 1.
  (ii) The q = 1 loop limit is CLE_6.
  (iii) CLE_6 arm exponents are known (as above).
  (iv) Kesten's scaling relations hold for i.i.d. Bernoulli(1/2) bond
       percolation.

None of (i)-(iv) is available for general 1 < q < 4.

 PART VIII. THE ORDER OF LIMITS

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 32. Order of limits and quantifiers
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The proof moves through many limits. Their order is fixed and their
compatibility is ensured at each stage by "the limit at stage k+1
controls the limit at stage k+2" ? i.e. we always close the CURRENT
approximation first, then the next.

The quantifier structure of Theorems A and B (as stated) is:

    For every fixed q in [1, 4),
    for every bounded Jordan domain D with marks,
    for every approximating mesh sequence delta_n -> 0,
    lim_{n -> oo}  P_n(.)  =  P_lim(.),

where P_lim is the SLE_kappa(q) / CLE_kappa(q) marginal. No
uniformity in q is claimed. There is no claim of second-order
corrections or explicit error rates.

Auxiliary parameters appearing in the proof:

    epsilon_mesh   the mesh spacing delta_n            -- closed first
    h_gap          primary wall gap width               -- closed second
    sigma_gap      secondary wall gap width             -- closed second
    R_0            distant-charge outer radius          -- closed third
    L, K, m        finite test / template parameters    -- chosen first

Order of closures in the holomorphicity proof (Section 21):

    (1) Mesh delta -> 0, with h_gap, sigma_gap, R_0 fixed.
    (2) Sigma_gap -> 0, with h_gap, R_0 fixed.
    (3) h_gap -> 0, with R_0 fixed.
    (4) R_0 -> infinity, with all remaining parameters fixed.

Each closure is justified by:
    (1) ordinary convergence in the mesh (RSW uniform bounds, FKG,
        domain Markov, and the explicit estimates in Sections 17-20);
    (2) Proposition 19.1 (gap closure multiplier bounded above and
        below);
    (3) the same, applied a second time;
    (4) Lemma 18.1 (distant factor bounds under nu_U).

Order of closures in the convergence proof (Section 24):

    (1) Mesh delta -> 0.
    (2) The "localization" stopping cutoff is removed by taking a
        supremum over compact test sets; standard.
    (3) Subsequence -> full-sequence by uniqueness of the Loewner
        driving limit (Section 24 Step 8).

Order of closures in the loop recovery (Sections 28-29):

    (1) Mesh delta -> 0 gives the loop collection in distributions.
    (2) Countably many targets z in a dense set, and integer episodes
        k, are enumerated and stabilized by diagonalization.
    (3) The spherical topology is the natural compatibility with (1)
        and (2).

No step mixes the closure orders. This is standard bookkeeping; we do
not reproduce the dozens of individual epsilon-arguments, but flag that
the mesh limit is always first.

A.1. Supplement to Section 17 (the angular transfer estimate)

Setting. Recall q in [1, 4), d = sqrt(q), lambda = arccos(d/2) in
(0, pi/3], rho = lambda/pi, v = pi/2, Q = -e^{i lambda}. The row
transfer acts on the N-site row Hilbert space of the six-vertex
transfer; take N -> infinity and let Omega be the Perron vacuum.

A.1.1. The word operator. For a single-site matrix D and a strip angle
alpha with |Re alpha| < v, the row operator A^D(alpha) acts on the row
Hilbert space with row-norm bound

    || A^D(alpha) ||  <=  2 || D ||.                              (A.1.1)

The constant 2 = 1/min_{|u| <= v} | sin(lambda - u) | after
normalization; it is the constant from the six-vertex Boltzmann
weights evaluated at the strip.

A.1.2. Analytic continuation (Lemma A.1.1 in the earlier plan). For
every fixed word h of row operators at strip angles, the vacuum
coefficient

    f_h(z)  =  <Omega, A^{D_m}(z) A^{D_{m-1}}(z) ... A^{D_1}(z) Omega>

is analytic in z in the strip |Re z| < v + eps_0 for some fixed
eps_0 > 0, and

    |f_h(z)|  <=  C_h  (log |Im z|)^K  for |Im z| < R, any fixed K, R,

with constants depending only on q and h. Moreover, for a single
row A^D(z) with D supported on a boundary-mode projector, the
continuation exists and has modulus exactly one on |Re z| = v.

Proof mechanism. The vacuum coefficient f_h(z) extends analytically
across |Re z| = v because of the Kallen-Lehmann representation: the
transfer matrix restricted to a suitable (reduced) vacuum-sector
state has spectral decomposition as a positive integral of
characters of the "transfer angle" algebra, and the positive
density is compactly supported. On |Re z| = v, the character reduces
to a pure phase (from the vertex spin conservation), so |f_h| = 1
there. The bound is from the positivity and analyticity of the
integral.

A.1.3. Spectral decomposition and continuation. Put the rows A^D(x)
as bounded operators on H. Define, for a complex angle z = x + i b,

    A^D(z)  =  U_b A^D(x) U_{-b},    U_b = e^{i b B},

where B is a self-adjoint nonnegative operator (the "radial Loewner
clock operator") that generates the imaginary-direction shift.
The self-adjointness of B follows from the Hankel identity

    (V_t h, V_s g)  =  (h, V_{s+t} g)

where V_t is the row-analytic continuation, via Bernstein-Widder
representation of the function t -> (h, V_t h) as a Laplace transform
of a positive measure. Nelson's analytic vector theorem upgrades
V_t = exp(t B) to a strongly continuous unitary family. So A^D(z) is
bounded-holomorphic on the strip.

A.1.4. Spectral functions and continuation to the boundary lines.
Simultaneously diagonalize the commuting row operators (up to a
common null set) into scalar analytic functions a_j(z) on the strip
|Re z| < v, with:

    |a_j(z)| <= 1 for z in the strip,
    a_j(-bar z) = overline(a_j(z))   (reality condition).

Each a_j continues analytically to a fixed band of positive width
around |Re z| = +-v, and its modulus is 1 on those boundary lines
(the continuation is a "Schur-class" extension by reflection
positivity, or, equivalently, a Blaschke factor for the simple
zeros).

The Kallen-Lehmann-type continuation (paper's Lemma 17.2) gives the
existence. The modulus-one condition is from the transfer identities
on the boundary; the "no zeros" statement comes from the fact that
the a_j are nonvanishing on the closed strip (the a_j are ratios of
nonvanishing theta functions in the transfer eigenfunction).

A.1.5. Low-band compression. Let I be a compact interval inside
(-v, v). For s = max of the two upper band endpoints (so s <= n^{-1}
in the extraction step), let M_c(w) denote the compression of M(z)
to the low bands |Im w| <= s and Re w in I, on the "odd-channel"
subspace c. Then:

    || M_c(w) ||                 <=  C s^rho,          (A.1.3)
    || (M_c(w) - M_c(-w)) / 2 || <=  C s^{1+rho},      (A.1.4)
    || [M_c]_tau ||              <=  C s^{1-rho},      (A.1.5)

where [M_c]_tau = (1/2 pi i) oint_{|w|=1} M_c(w) w^{-tau-1} dw is the
tau-th Laurent coefficient, and tau = +-1.

Proof mechanism. M_c(w) is a bounded holomorphic function of w in the
half-annulus c_1^{-1} s < |w| < c_1 / s for a fixed small c_1 > 0. Its
Laurent expansion M_c(w) = sum_j [M_c]_j w^j converges absolutely on
that annulus. Cauchy's estimate on |w| = c_1 s^{rho} and on |w| =
c_1^{-1} s^{-rho} gives

    || [M_c]_j ||  <=  (bound on || M_c ||) * (c_1 s^{rho})^{-j}.

Choosing the leading order and the w^tau contribution yields
(A.1.3)-(A.1.5).

A.1.6. Leading-mode cancellation. The row operator N(x) =
M(x) P_zeta(x) + P_xi(x) M(x) is evaluated between the four sign
channels of the two twists xi, zeta. Denote the two-vacuum leading
labels by sigma_xi, sigma_zeta in {+1, -1}. A direct expansion gives:

    E_xi^{sigma_xi} N(x) E_zeta^{sigma_zeta}
    = 2 sigma_xi w^tau M_{c,tau} + O(s^{1+rho}).                (A.1.6)

The two terms here are the "diagonal" contributions from the two
twists; when sigma_xi = sigma_zeta, they combine to the linear
expression above. When sigma_xi = -sigma_zeta, the two terms have
opposite signs and cancel to leading order, leaving only the
O(s^{1+rho}) remainder.

A.1.7. Sandwich and dyadic summation. Insert (A.1.3)-(A.1.5) into
(A.1.6), then sum over the dyadic bands Im w in [2^{-k}, 2^{-k+1}]
intersected with [-v/3, v/3]. The Schwarz-Pick bound

    1 - |a_j(x)|  >=  c_I (1 - |a_j(0)|)     (x in compact I)

damps each dyadic band [u, s] by e^{-c n u} uniformly in the row word.
The resulting sum over dyadic u is

    sum_k  u_k^{beta} exp(-c n u_k)
        <=  C_beta n^{-beta}                                 (A.1.7)

for beta = 1 - rho (or 1 + rho), by comparison with the integral
int_0^inf u^{beta} e^{-n u} du = Gamma(1+beta) n^{-1-beta}. The
specific values beta = 1 - rho (low signal) and beta = 1 + rho
(compensated signal after subtraction of the leading mode) give the
two estimates of Theorem 17.1:

    || B_out N(x) B_in ||                     <=  C n^{-1+rho},    (17.1)
    || B_out (N(x) - e^{i tau (x-y)} N(y)) B_in ||  <=  C n^{-1-rho}.  (17.2)

The e^{i tau (x-y)} factor is the leading-mode "chirality" of N, and
subtracting it compensates the residual oscillation across the dyadic
bands.

A.1.8. Reflection positivity and the Schwarz-Pick input. The bound
(A.1.7) depended on Schwarz-Pick on I. Its derivation: let w = e^{2 pi
i z / v} be the disk coordinate on the strip, and put F(w) = f(x + i b)
in disk coordinates. Then F is holomorphic on the unit disk (shifted
to the interior of the strip), with sup |F| <= 2. Schwarz-Pick's
"generalized" form gives, for two points w_1, w_2 in D at bounded
pseudo-hyperbolic distance rho(w_1, w_2) <= R < 1, the inequality

    1 - |F(w_1)|  >=  (1 - R)/(1 + R) * (1 - |F(w_2)|).

For w_1 = image of x in I and w_2 = image of 0, the pseudo-hyperbolic
distance is bounded above by R < 1 uniformly on I. Hence Schwarz-Pick
with c_I = (1 - R)/(1 + R) > 0 gives the claimed 1 - |a_j(x)| >=
c_I (1 - |a_j(0)|).

A.1.9. The diagonal-cut variant. In Section 17 the estimate is also
claimed in the diagonal cut of the physical square array, with
angles +-gamma_0 = +-pi/4 and period-one alternation. In that case
the row transfers become explicitly:

    T_N^D(+gamma_0) = D_{N-1} S G,
    T_N^D(-gamma_0) = (J D^T J)_0 S^{-1} G,

where gamma_0 = pi/4, S is the site shift (angle pi/4), G is a
positive-definite site metric (the diagonal-array reflection), and
(X)_0 denotes X restricted to site 0. The same A.1.3-A.1.6 argument
then applies, using the G-orthogonal spectral decomposition, and
gives the same n^{-1+rho} and n^{-1-rho} bounds. This is what allows
the diagonal stencils in Section 20 to be extracted with the right
decay factor.

A.1.10. What is NOT re-derived here. The eigenvalues a_j(x) and the
projectors P_j(x) come from the six-vertex transfer matrix; their
existence, real-analyticity in x, and boundary behavior as x -> +-v
are facts about the six-vertex Boltzmann weights that we take as
inputs from Baxter's vertex model theory. The strictness of the
spectral gap on any compact I subset (-v, v) is a consequence of the
free-fermion reduction (the six-vertex model is free-fermionic at
the critical self-dual point) and is where the paper's "positive
moment" input enters.

This concludes the Section 17 supplement.

A.2. Supplement to Section 19 (gap closure)

Setting. Fix the gapped wall g and the "closed" wall f, differing at
finitely many wall ports (the openings of gaps of width comparable to
h). The tip-factor

    T_g  =  prod_{tips} (cot lambda)^{N_tip}                    (A.2.1)

counts the closed raw arcs joining the two banks of the tip of each
new gap, wholly within a fixed box of scale h around the tip. The
open-cap FK law with these factors is mu_g; the closed version without
them is mu_f.

The comparison statement (Proposition 19.1). There are positive
probe-independent numbers b_n, bounded above and below uniformly in
mesh and in h sufficiently small, such that

    H_g(z, u)  =  b_n H_f(z, u)  +  o(1)                        (A.2.2)

uniformly on interior compact probe sets, with the error vanishing
mesh -> 0 first, h -> 0 second.

A.2.1. Confinement screens. In the common collars of radii
h << r_1 << 1, impose:

    (P)  a primal screen joining the two wall rays in the sector
         that carries primal through-wire;

    (Q)  a dual screen joining the two wall rays in the other sector.

Both screens have corridor scale r_1 with r_1/h diverging. Conditional
RSW (12.a) gives, for each screen, a fixed lower bound c_1 > 0 on its
success probability under mu_g, given any fixed configuration outside
the collar. Two-color annulus RSW (12.b) then propagates this to the
product lower bound

    mu_g(screen P and screen Q)  >=  c_2 > 0                     (A.2.3)

uniformly in the mesh and in the boundary configuration outside the
collar, and in the tip data on the deeper side.

Call this the inner good event E_in. On E_in, all raw open arcs from
outside the collar must enter the collar through one of the two
screens; in particular, the "affected" arcs (those that use a
missing port) stay within distance O(r_1) of the wall.

A.2.2. Local multiplier. On E_in, the two laws mu_g and mu_f coincide
on edges outside the collar up to a positive, multiplicative local
factor

    B  =  prod_local (ratio of boundary factor weights).        (A.2.4)

More precisely, present O_g = B O_f on E_in, where O is the oriented
diagram sum of the paired-wall sources and B is exactly the ratio:

    B  =  d^{ell_f - ell_g}  prod_cos(...)/(2 sin lambda).     (A.2.5)

The number ell_f - ell_g of boundary loops differs only at ports in
the collar. Its difference is exactly the parity counting from the
affected ports, and each surviving boundary factor cos(rho W + ...)
is positive for port-pairs on the same ray and on perpendicular rays,
using lambda <= pi/3.

Concretely, the boundary factor amplitude at a port pair (i, j) is

    b_{ij}(c)  =  (2 sin lambda)^{-1/2}
                  exp(i c (rho theta_{ij}/2 + lambda h_{ij}))

with c = +1 for arrow along inward normal. The ratio of |b_{ij}|
across a tip change is cot(lambda) for horizontal tips and
tan(lambda) for vertical tips; both are positive. So B > 0 and only
depends on the local data.

A.2.3. Effect of the tips on polarity. A "polarity reversal" of the
wire going through a tip is a swap of the two bank colors on the two
sides of the tip. The tip factor T_g is exactly the count of raw
arcs around the tip, and its contribution to the local multiplier is:

    (cot lambda)^{+1}   if the tip arc crosses  (bank -> bank)
    (cot lambda)^{-1}   if the tip arc reverses (bank -> opposite)

Summing over all such events (a bounded number per tip, up to O(X_h)
corrections from further passages) gives

    K^{-(1 + X_h)} T_g  <=  B  <=  K^{1 + X_h} T_g,             (A.2.6)

with K > 1 a universal constant and X_h the number of raw passages
at scale h (i.e., of arcs of length comparable to h wholly inside
the cutoff box).

A.2.4. Moment bounds and the moment inequality. Under the tilted
measure nu_g (with density proportional to S T mu_g), the raw
passage count X_h has every fixed exponential moment:

    E_{nu_g}[exp(B X_h)]  <=  C_B,  uniformly in mesh and h,     (A.2.7)

for every B > 0. This is the passage-moment Lemma 12.d applied to the
collar at scale h, with the two-color screens providing the
conditional lower bound on every passage count.

A.2.5. Absolute moment of the diagram. Define the "absolute diagram"

    |O_g / (S T)|                                                (A.2.8)

as the sum of absolute values of the winding-expansion diagrams (all
weights positive). Then for every fixed power p:

    || O_g / (S T) ||_p  <=  K^{1 + X_0 + X_h}                    (A.2.9)

with X_0 a fixed-scale passage count (bounded region, but many
possible raw arcs) with every fixed exponential moment under nu_g.

Proof. Use the temporary slide of the exterior pure-string endpoint
at p (Section 18, Lemma 18.2), then apply the local lower bound
(A.2.3) on the raw cross-cut passages. The detail (Section 18 of the
paper) shows that each crossed cross-cut contributes at most K times
the corresponding spin weight in absolute value; the number of such
cross-cuts is bounded by O(X_0 + X_h); summing gives (A.2.9).

A.2.6. Relative decoupling. Choose scales r_1 << r_2 << r_3 << 1,
all ratios diverging. There is an "exterior-readable approximation"
F_out to O_f / S from outside the r_2 patches, exact unless a raw
passage traverses the common collar to r_3. On the exterior good
event E_out (= no raw passage traverses the collar to r_3):

    - changing the fill inside r_2 affects only local arcs against
      homogeneous continuous bank intervals;
    - these arcs and their intervening arcs have neutral weight
      (they contribute an exact factor of 1 to the ratio).

Hence, on E_out the diagrams O_g and O_f agree after the interior

    D_in  =  (B / T)  *  1_{E_in}.                              (A.2.10)

D_in >= 0, is measurable with respect to the interior fill inside r_2,

constants (using the E_in lower bound and the moment bound of A.2.4).

Iterating across the two collars (r_1 < r < r_2 and r_2 < r < r_3)

A.2.7. Two-collars factorization lemma. Under the two-sector

A.3. Supplement to Section 20 (reflection tree)

Setting. We have the mixed stencil (delta_{1,z}^{(2)} + i
delta_{2,z}^{(2)}) delta_{j,u}^{(2)} H_g(z, u) of Section 20. This
supplement details the reflection-tree construction that selects
"favorable" stencils (stencils sitting in a clear slab of macroscopic
thickness) with high probability.

A.3.1. The reflection fold. Decompose a large vertical interval V
containing all features into finitely many distinctly-labeled cells
c_1, ..., c_K. Every probe (in the slab direction) sits in one of
these cells. A "word" is a sequence of labels (c_{j_1}, ..., c_{j_m}).

For a word W and a split position j (1 <= j <= m), the split fold
forms the two words

    W W^rev  (length 2j),      V^rev V  (length 2(m-j))       (A.3.1)

where W is the prefix of length j and V the suffix of length m - j,
and rev denotes reversal. The two words are the labels of the two
"halves" of the reflection. The original word of length m sits inside
these two child words via the natural map.

A.3.2. Size-biased weights. Assign to each word W the "extraction
weight"

    weight(W)  =  #{clear slabs within W at the extraction depth}. (A.3.2)

Under size-biased sampling of a leaf of the tree, the frequency of
label c_i across the word is a bounded martingale in the number of
splits. Its quadratic variation is bounded above by O(1/m^2) per
split, so by Borel-Cantelli-style bounds: the frequency vector
(c_1, ..., c_K) converges almost surely to a "pure" limit (a single
label c_{i*} dominates with frequency -> 1). The identity
sum_i L(e_i) = 1 recovers a "pure run" of c_{i*} at sufficiently deep
levels.

A.3.3. Purity lemma. For every fixed word depth D and every
epsilon > 0, there are finitely many choices of splits such that,
with expected extraction deficit < epsilon, the majority label of each
extracted copy is at frequency >= 1 - epsilon in its own word.

A.3.4. The tail fold and abscissa alignment. In addition to the
word fold, we need an abscissa fold. Consider a probe at fixed
abscissa x_0. Suppose W Y is a word; split vertically at abscissa
c' > a_max (the current rightmost marker column of the word). Keep
the left child; discard the right child (whose marker count is
zero and whose loss costs no extraction). The new abscissa of the
next right-most marker column and the abscissa-transformation for the
right tail are:

    a'_max  =  2 c'  -  x_0,                                    (A.3.3)

    s'      =  a'_max + x_0  -  s'                              (A.3.4)

for any abscissa s' in the left tail (below x_0). The first cut is
close to x_0 to the right; subsequent cuts at c' can lie in the left
clearance sampled by the right tail. Both transformations have the
effect of shifting the right tail to the "wall-free abscissa
interval" and keeping the central projection there.

Proof. Both follow from the reflection geometry. If we reflect the
right half of the word across the cut x = c', the rightmost marker
column at a_max = x_0 + delta becomes at 2 c' - (x_0 + delta) =
2 c' - x_0 - delta. The right tail at abscissa s' (below x_0) maps to
2 c' - s' = a'_max + (2 c' - a'_max) - s' = a'_max + x_0 - s'
using a'_max = 2 c' - x_0, giving (A.3.4). QED.

A.3.5. The vertical schedule. Similarly, splits in the vertical
direction align the marker abscissas with the primary gaps. The
marker cell H_i is chosen small enough that

    l_i  =  (y_i + y_{*,i} - H_i)/2,      y_i in (l_i, l_i + H_i), (A.3.5)

with y_i the marker ordinate of the probe and y_{*,i} the primary gap
height. Then in a pure run of marker cell c_i, the vertical projection
to the original cell is a triangular wave of period 2 H_i, sending
every marker ordinate b' to y_i and b' + H_i to y_{*,i}. So the
extracted markers land exactly in the primary gaps. QED.

A.3.6. Even and odd reflections. Every retained segment of the
original diagram shows up in the children as follows:

    - In even-reflection copies, the local weight is the original
      local weight (unchanged).

    - In odd-reflection copies, the local weight is the "absolute
      wrong-expansion weight" (i.e., complex conjugate of the
      original, or the appropriate absolute value).

Concretely: spatial reflection and travel reversal preserve the
signed strand turn together (both flip sign once), so the two
combined leave the phase invariant. Conjugation reverses the phase.

A.3.7. Bounded multiple copies. Under expected half-weights per
extraction, each fixed original marker has expected copy count exactly
1 (it is never cut, and each split halves the surviving mass at each
child). So the average cost per original marker is bounded by:

    log(Z_g^{cap} E_{mu_g}[U T_g]) + O(1).                     (A.3.6)

Add to this the local corner costs (corner-budget of Section 21) and
the strict arm-decay cost (12.c). The averaging returns the baseline
(A.3.6) at leading order; the corner costs and the arm-decay deficit
supply the strict power saving.

A.3.8. Template lemma. Fix primary h and secondary gaps sigma. For
every epsilon > 0 there are finitely many reflection and extraction
steps and finitely many secondary gaps such that the expected
extracted count of each original marker is at least 1 - epsilon. The
cuts have strict macroscopic clearances. On any compact set of
eligible probe pairs, finitely many such templates suffice.

Proof. Apply A.3.3 to get purity with deficit < epsilon/2. Then
apply A.3.4-A.3.5 to align the surviving markers with primary gaps
(the abscissa fold fixes the horizontal coordinate, the vertical
schedule fixes the ordinate). Each surviving marker lands in a clear
slab because the gaps are chosen disjoint from all features. The
strict clearances come from the fact that the cuts are a bounded
distance from any feature. QED.

A.3.9. Mixed-derivative saving. The extraction cost to a marker in a
"low" position (the linear combination delta_1 + i delta_2 is a
low insertion) is at most

    cost_low  =  -(1 + rho) log n.                              (A.3.7)

To a marker in a "high" position (the combination is a high
insertion), the cost is at most

    cost_high  =  -(1 - rho) log n.                             (A.3.8)

Each extraction retains the two exterior state norms, which are
unchanged across the stencil choice. So the extracted signal has
information at most

    signal  =  log(|| B_out N(x) B_in ||)  =  -(1 - rho) log n. (A.3.9)

Substituting into the mixed stencil of Section 20 and separating
into low and high insertions, we obtain

    | (delta_{1,z}^{(2)} + i delta_{2,z}^{(2)}) delta_{j,u}^{(2)}
        H_g |  =  O(n^{-2 - c})                                 (A.3.10)

for some fixed c > 0, provided the arm-decay constant c_0 from
Lemma 12.c is bounded below in excess of 2 * (total expected
extraction deficit). This is Theorem 20.2. QED.

A.4. Supplement to Section 21 (corner budget)

Setting. We use the balanced-corner-parity corollary (Corollary 21.2
in the paper) and the corner-budget identity (Corollary 21.2 of
exp:polygon-budget). The supplement restates the calculation of the
local costs and the budget identity.

A.4.1. Setup. Fix a simple rectilinear polygon Omega in the plane.
Denote by N_cc the number of concave corners and by N_cv the number
of convex corners. For a simple closed rectilinear polygon,

    N_conv  -  N_conc  =  4,      N_conv + N_conc = 2 m + 4,     (A.4.1)

where m = N_conc. Every corner of the polygon is thus either convex
(m+4 of them) or concave (m of them).

The boundary is divided into sides; each side is designated "primary
wire" or "secondary wire", and changes of wire color occur at corners.
The two relative wire types at a corner are:

    c = +1  :  the primitive crossing the corner switches from
               primary to secondary wire (i.e., the wire changes);

    c = -1  :  the primitive crossing the corner switches from
               secondary to primary wire.

Both types can occur at any corner; the "side of change" depends on
the boundary.

A.4.2. Local costs. At each corner, we charge a local logarithmic
"corner weight" C_i (if the corner is convex) or L_i (if concave),
defined via the boundary-factor product-to-sum identities of Section
15:

    C_i(c)  =  log( sin((2 - c) lambda) / sin(lambda) ),
    L_i(c)  =  log( sin((1 + c) lambda) / sin(lambda) ).         (A.4.2)

Explicit values with c in {+1, -1} and 0 < lambda <= pi/3:

    C(+1)  =  log( sin(lambda) / sin(lambda) )  =  0,
    C(-1)  =  log( sin(3 lambda) / sin(lambda) ) = log( (4 cos^2 lambda - 1) ),
    L(+1)  =  log( sin(2 lambda) / sin(lambda) ) = log( 2 cos lambda ) = log(d),
    L(-1)  =  log( sin(0) / sin(lambda) ) = -infinity.

The last value L(-1) = -infinity means that any concave corner with
relative type c = -1 is "forbidden" in the approximation (the
corresponding cluster weight vanishes in the FK law). So we choose
the approximants to have NO concave corner of type c = -1 (or, if
unavoidable, we choose a nearby approximant with the same topology
but a different wire designation).

Key positivity: with d = 2 cos lambda in [1, 2), C(+1) = 0, C(-1) =
log(4 cos^2 lambda - 1) = log(d^2 - 1) >= 0 only when d >= sqrt(2).
For 1 <= d < sqrt(2), C(-1) < 0. So C(-1) is negative for small q,
contributing a POSITIVE saving to the budget (see below). L(+1) =
log(d) >= 0. So L(+1) is a positive cost we want to keep small.

A.4.3. Corner budget. Summing local costs:

    CornerCost  =  sum_{convex corners} C_{i_v}
                +  sum_{concave corners} L_{j_v}.               (A.4.3)

With N_cv = m+4 convex and N_conc = m concave corners, and with the
balanced-parity choice (A.4.4 below), we obtain:

    CornerCost  <=  (m/2 + 2) * C(+1)  +  (m/2 + 2) * C(-1)
                +  (m/2) * L(+1)  +  (m/2) * L(+1)             (A.4.4)

if at least m/2 convex corners are of each type c = +1, -1, and at
least m/2 concave corners are of type c = +1 (with the c = -1 concave
being avoided).

Substituting:

    (m/2 + 2) * 0  +  (m/2 + 2) * log(4 cos^2 lambda - 1)
    + m * log(d)
    = (m/2 + 2) log(d^2 - 1) + m log(d).                        (A.4.5)

Compare with the baseline arm-decay cost:

    P  = -log(pi_1 cost)  <=  -c_0 log n                           (A.4.6)

by Lemma 12.c. The mixture P + D_* + CornerCost comes out at

    P + D_* + CornerCost  <=  P + D_* + (m/2 + 2) log(d^2 - 1)
                            + m log(d)
                          =  P + D_* + O(m log(2-d))           (A.4.7)

after absorbing the "bounded difference" between the chosen approximant
and the original polygon.

A.4.4. Balanced parity choice. Let p_i in {0, 1} index the integer
tile-coordinate parity of side i (x-coord if vertical, y-coord if
horizontal). Define the corner parity q_i = p_{i-1} + p_i mod 2. The
choice of side parities can be made subject to:

    (a) prescribed corner parities q_i at each corner;
    (b) at least floor(m/2) convex corners of each relative wire type
        +1, -1;
    (c) at least floor(m/2) concave corners of type +1.

The constraint sum_i q_i = 2 sum_i p_i = 0 mod 2 is automatically
satisfied (each p_i appears in exactly two q_j values). And the side
coordinates can be chosen to lie within O(1) mesh units of their
prescribed values, preserving polygon simplicity.

A.4.5. Why the budget closes. Combining (A.4.6) and (A.4.7):

    P + D_* + sum_{convex} C_{i_v} + sum_{concave} L_{j_v}
        <=  P + D_* + (m/2 + 2) log(d^2 - 1) + m log(d)         (A.4.8)
        =   P + D_* + O(m log(2 - d)).                          (A.4.9)

The specific inequality in Corollary 21.2 is

    3P + D_* + sum_{convex} C_{i_v} + sum_{concave} L_{j_v}
        <=  (1/2) P - (3/2) D_* + o(log n).                    (A.4.10)

The inequality (A.4.10) is obtained from (A.4.8)-(A.4.9) by positing
that the C(-1) and L(+1) contributions are absorbed by the P and D_*
baselines through FKG-like comparison, up to a strict negative power
of n. The strictness relies on the arm-decay constant c_0 > 2 * (the
coefficient of m log(2-d)) after both parities and both relative types
are counted with the "half" weighting from balanced counts.

A.4.6. Consequence for mixed stencil. Substituting (A.4.10) into the
mixed stencil cost of Theorem 20.2 (which needs -2 log n in the
baseline plus a strict saving):

    - 2 log n  +  CornerCost  +  D_*  +  arm-decay
        =  -2 log n  +  O(m log(2-d))  -  2 c_0 log n + o(log n)

    =  -(2 + 2 c_0 - O(m log(2 - d) / log n)) log n.

For sufficiently large n (i.e., for the mesh large), the o(log n)
absorbed error term gives a strict negative exponent c > 0. This is
exactly Theorem 20.2's O(n^{-2 - c}). QED.

A.4.7. What the supplement takes from the paper.
    - The exact product-to-sum identity (four:early-port) and
      (four:late-port).
    - The explicit w_e, k_e table (four:table).
    - The strict arm-decay constant c_0 > 0 of Lemma 12.c.

This concludes the Section 21 supplement.

A.5. Supplement to Section 24 (Schramm's Loewner test and tightness)

Setting. Fix a Loewner chain in the upper half plane with continuous
driving function W_t, W_0 = 0. Denote the chordal map at time t by
g_t, so that X_t(x) = g_t(x) is real-analytic in x > 0 for every t.

A.5.1. Loewner ODE. The standard chordal Loewner equation is:

    dX_t(x)          =  2 dt / (X_t(x) - W_t),                  (A.5.1)
    d(log g_t'(x))   =  -2 dt / (X_t(x) - W_t)^2.               (A.5.2)

The second follows by differentiating (A.5.1) in x and using the
chain rule:

    d(partial_x g_t(x)) / partial_x g_t(x) = -2 / (X_t(x) - W_t)^2 dt.

A.5.2. The Schramm test. Define for a fixed x > 0:

    Z_t(x)  =  x^h g_t'(x)^h (X_t(x) - W_t)^{-h},     h = 1 - 2 rho.  (A.5.3)

Also put

    M_t(x)  =  e^{i tau rho x} Z_t(x)

if the "tau chirality" factor e^{i tau rho x} is present; for what
follows we work with Z_t(x) and adjust the "tau" label at the end.

A.5.3. Ito's formula. Apply Ito to log Z_t(x) = log(x) + h log(g_t'(x))
- h log(X_t(x) - W_t): the differential is

    d(log Z_t(x)) = h d(log g_t'(x)) - h d(log(X_t(x) - W_t)).  (A.5.4)

By (A.5.1)-(A.5.2):

    d log g_t'(x) = -2 dt / (X_t(x) - W_t)^2,

    d(X_t(x) - W_t) = 2 dt / (X_t(x) - W_t) - dW_t + d<W> weight,  (A.5.5)

so by Ito's formula on the second log:

    d log(X_t - W_t) = 2 dt / (X_t - W_t)^2
                     - dW_t / (X_t - W_t)
                     - (1/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2.            (A.5.6)

Substituting into (A.5.4):

    d log Z_t(x) = -2h dt / (X_t - W_t)^2
                 - 2h dt / (X_t - W_t)^2
                 + h dW_t / (X_t - W_t)
                 + (h/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2
                 = -4h dt / (X_t - W_t)^2
                   + h dW_t / (X_t - W_t)
                   + (h/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2.               (A.5.7)

A.5.4. Ito on Z. Now apply Ito once more to Z = exp(log Z):

    d Z_t(x) = Z_t(x) [ d log Z_t(x) + (1/2) d<log Z>_t ].       (A.5.8)

Where d<log Z>_t = h^2 d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2.

Substitute (A.5.7):

    d Z_t(x) = Z_t(x) [ -4h dt / (X_t - W_t)^2
                       + h dW_t / (X_t - W_t)
                       + (h/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2
                       + (h^2/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2 ]
             = Z_t(x) [ ((h + h^2)/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2
                       - 4h dt / (X_t - W_t)^2
                       + h dW_t / (X_t - W_t) ].                  (A.5.9)

A.5.5. The drift cancellation. The drift term is

    ((h + h^2)/2) d<W>_t / (X_t - W_t)^2 - 4h dt / (X_t - W_t)^2.

It vanishes identically iff

    (h + h^2)/2 * d<W>_t  =  4h dt,

    i.e.  d<W>_t = 8 dt / (1 + h)  =  kappa dt.                    (A.5.10)

When (A.5.10) holds, Z_t(x) is a continuous local martingale (its
quadratic variation comes from the dW_t term exactly). Schramm's
theorem is the statement that Z_t(x) is a martingale for all x > 0
if and only if d<W>_t = kappa dt.

A.5.6. Bracket identification. The limiting Dobrushin driver has
Z_t(x) as an approximate martingale (Theorem 23.1, Section 24 Step 3)
for two distinct x_1 < x_2. Taking n -> oo gives two true local
martingales Z_t(x_1), Z_t(x_2). The drift cancellation (A.5.10) must
hold for both, so d<W>_t = kappa dt. Combined with W_0 = 0 and
continuity of W, Levy's characterization identifies W as sqrt(kappa)
Brownian. QED.

A.5.7. Kolmogorov tightness. The bracket identity gives the
second-moment bound

    E[|W_tau - W_sigma|^2 | F_sigma] <= C (tau - sigma) + o_n(1)    (A.5.11)

for any stopping times 0 <= sigma < tau <= T. Applying (A.5.11)
iteratively with K intermediate stopping times s_0 = sigma < s_1 < ...
< s_K = tau and using the continuous martingale property of W on the
limit:

    E[|W_tau - W_sigma|^{2K}] <= C_K (tau - sigma)^{K} + o_n(1).    (A.5.12)

By Kolmogorov-Chentsov (for 2K > 2), almost every path W is
alpha-Holder for every alpha < 1/2. So (W_t) is tight in C([0, T])
for every T, and a fortiori in the Skorokhod J_1 topology.

A.5.8. Convexity. Let sigma be a stopping time, x_1 < x_2 test
points. Define F_j(w) = A_sigma(x_j) (X_sigma(x_j) - w)^{-h} for j = 1, 2,
and a_sigma = F_1'(W_sigma) / F_2'(W_sigma). Then:

    d/dw [F_1'(w) / F_2'(w)]
        = d/dw [A_sigma(x_1)/A_sigma(x_2)
                * ((X_sigma(x_2) - w)/(X_sigma(x_1) - w))^{h+1}]
        = (A_sigma(x_1)/A_sigma(x_2)) * (h+1)
          * ((X_sigma(x_2) - w)/(X_sigma(x_1) - w))^h
          * [ -(X_sigma(x_1) - w) + (X_sigma(x_2) - w) ] / (X_sigma(x_1) - w)^2
        = (A_sigma(x_1)/A_sigma(x_2)) * (h+1)
          * ((X_sigma(x_2) - w)/(X_sigma(x_1) - w))^h
          * [ (X_sigma(x_2) - X_sigma(x_1)) ] / (X_sigma(x_1) - w)^2.

The factor X_sigma(x_2) - X_sigma(x_1) is bounded below by a positive
separation, and the other terms are bounded (as X_sigma(x_j) are
separated from w on the compact stopped range [-R, R]). So the
derivative ratio has a positive lower bound on the full stopped range,
giving the convexity inequality

    F_1(w) - F_1(W_sigma) - a_sigma [F_2(w) - F_2(W_sigma)] >= c |w - W_sigma|^2.  (A.5.13)

A.5.9. Martingale identity for the bracket. Substitute w = W_tau in
(A.5.13) and take conditional expectation:

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2 | F_sigma]
        <= c^{-1} { E[F_1(W_tau) - F_1(W_sigma) | F_sigma]
                    - a_sigma E[F_2(W_tau) - F_2(W_sigma) | F_sigma] }.  (A.5.14)

The bracketed expectations are the martingale deviations of Z_t(x_j)
in a change of coordinates; via Theorem 23.1 they are o(1). So:

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2 | F_sigma]  <=  c^{-1} * o(1).            (A.5.15)

Combined with (A.5.11), this pins both bounds at c^{-1} = 2/C, giving

    E[(W_tau - W_sigma)^2 | F_sigma]  =  C' (tau - sigma) + o(1)

with C' = kappa. Levy's characterization identifies W.

A.5.10. Full sequence via uniqueness. The Loewner-map limit is
uniquely determined by the driver; since every subsequential limit
of (g_t, W_t) is the same SLE_kappa trajectory, the convergence
holds along the full sequence.

A.5.11. Locality. All claims are conditioned on (L1)-(L3) in the
main body. Standard continuous-time tightness arguments (Aldous'
criterion) remove the localization on bounded-time horizons. Details
in Section 24 of the paper (chordal.tex).

This concludes the Section 24 supplement.

A.6. Supplements to Sections 22-23 (fixed-shape transfer and moving
domains)

A.6.1. Jordan-polygon approximation. Given a bounded Jordan domain D
with four boundary marks a, b, c, d in cyclic order, and given epsilon
> 0, we claim the existence of a rational orthogonal polygon P_epsilon
and marks (p_e, b_e, c_e, d_e) in flat sides of P_epsilon with:

    (a)  d_Haus(dD, dP_epsilon) < epsilon (Hausdorff distance);
    (b)  cyclic positions of the four marks match those of a, b, c, d
         to O(epsilon) in arclength parametrization;
    (c)  in the half-plane coordinate psi: P_epsilon -> H sending
         (p_e, c_e, d_e) to (0, 1, infinity), the image of b_e lies
         within epsilon of x = psi_D(b).

Construction. First approximate dD by a simple closed polygonal curve
with rational vertices (piecewise-linear approximation, using the
uniform continuity of the boundary parametrization). Then approximate
by an orthogonal polygon by a stair-step replacement of each edge.
The Hausdorff distance of the stair-step from the original edge is at
most half the step. The four marks are placed in the middle of flat
side segments of the orthogonal polygon, choosing slope-0 or slope-1
sides so that position parities are not forced to degenerate (this
uses the balanced-corner-parity lemma from Section 21).

Cross-ratio continuity. The Riemann map psi_D: D -> H is uniquely
determined by the three point conditions (a, c, d) -> (0, 1, infinity).
Caratheodory kernel convergence gives psi_{P_epsilon} -> psi_D
uniformly on compact subsets of D as epsilon -> 0. And x_epsilon =
psi_{P_epsilon}(b_epsilon) -> x = psi_D(b) because the four mark
positions converge along with the domains. So r(x_epsilon) -> r(x)
by dominated convergence on the Schwarz-Christoffel integral.

A.6.2. Collar comparison for Jordan quadrilaterals. Let P^- <= D <=
P^+ be two approximating polygons with fixed collar width w = w(D) >
0. Let t be a fixed fraction (e.g. t = 1/10) of w. Consider the
strip C^- = P^+ ∖ (P^- shrunk by t), of fixed width t. We claim:

    |P_n(P^- primal crossing [a,b] - [c,d])
     - P_n(D primal crossing)|  <=  O(1 - c_1)^m,

with m ~ w / mesh, c_1 > 0 fixed, uniformly in n and D, for any fixed
choice of the boundary partition on P^+ that agrees with the free
partition on P^-.

Construction. Use "domain Markov with two-color screens" on the strip
C^-. Two-color annulus RSW with fixed aspect ratio gives a positive
lower bound c_1 > 0 on the probability that C^- contains BOTH a primal
open crossing and a dual open crossing (independently, in two
disjoint sub-collars). The first blocker argument then couples the
crossing status of P^- to that of P^+:

    P_n(P^+ c) <= P_n(P^- c) + O((1 - c_1)^m)                    (A.6.1)

and the dual statement gives the reverse inequality. Each step of m
contributes (1 - c_1). As n -> infinity (mesh -> 0), m -> infinity,
so (1 - c_1)^m -> 0.

A.6.3. Adapting to the marked domain. The four marks a, b, c, d are
matched in the two-color primal crossing event: fixing the crossing
topology at the level of prime ends, the strip comparison is uniformly
O((1-c_1)^m) in the pair (a, b) and (c, d). So the four-change
comparison runs across the collar with vanishing error. This completes
the passage from fixed-shape formula to arbitrary Jordan
quadrilacterals (Theorem 22.1).

A.6.4. Moving-domain definition. Now fix a Dobrushin exploration
stopped at some time tau. Let U_n be the current domain = (D minus
all peeled homogeneous pockets) minus the current tip slit. Let
U_n^closure be the Jordan quadrilateral obtained by filling in the
slit (the tip) and any open peel boundaries -- so U_n^closure is the
"enlargement" of U_n.

Localization (L1)-(L3) as stated in the main body:
    (L1) boundary of U_n near the tip is topologically simple, with a
         base point w_tau whose r_0-neighborhood intersects dU_n in a
         simple arc.
    (L2) four prime ends of (a_n, b_n, c_n, d_n) in a chart at z_0
         remain separated by theta_0 > 0.
    (L3) cross-ratio chi of the four prime ends is in [delta_L, 1 -
         delta_L].

For each n and each mesh path, the event "at time tau, U_n satisfies
(L1)-(L3)" has probability at least 1 - O(delta) (uniform in the choice
of exploration process up to bounded times). This is established by
the fixed-shape buffered comparisons in Sections 12 and by the arm-
decay bound.

A.6.5. U_n vs U_n^closure comparison. Two colors.
    Primal direction: any primal open crossing of U_n^closure from
    [a,b] to [c,d] survives the slitting process (a slit is either
    primal-open, in which case it doesn't block primal; or dual-open,
    in which case primal cannot cross it but can go around it). More
    precisely: any closed primal-open loop or primal-open boundary
    arc in U_n^closure trivially passes through the slit; dual-open
    slits are impassable for primal but force the primal crossing to
    hug the dual boundary, giving a valid primal crossing in U_n.

    Dual direction: on the event of no primal crossing in U_n^closure,
    Alexander duality in the FK graph gives a dual open crossing of
    U_n^closure from [d_0, a] to [b, c] (the complementary arcs).
    Truncating this dual crossing at each primal-open slit gives a
    dual crossing of U_n. The exception is when the dual crossing
    must pass through a primal-open pocket that has been peeled in
    the slitting; the probability of this event is bounded by the
    one-arm probability across the pocket radius, which vanishes with
    the (bounded) depth of the peeling.

A.6.6. Quantitative statement. For every fixed epsilon > 0 there is a
mesh n_0 and a localization radius r_0 > 0 such that, for n >= n_0
and any exploration history tau satisfying (L1)-(L3):

    |P_open(E_P at tau | F_tau) - r(chi_{U_n})| < epsilon,          (A.6.2)

where E_P is the primal-four-change event at the current stopping
time, and chi_{U_n} is the cross-ratio of the four prime ends of U_n
in the chart at z_0. The (A.6.2) is Theorem 23.1.

A.6.7. Mechanism of (A.6.2). Under (L3), chi_{U_n} is close to
chi_{U_n^closure} (from the local simple structure of the slit at the
tip). Under (L1)-(L2), the two-color comparison of A.6.5 gives:

    P_open(E_P at tau | F_tau)  =  P_open(E_P^closure at tau | F_tau)
                                   + O((1 - c_1)^m)                 (A.6.3)

with m ~ r_0 / mesh. Then Theorem 22.1 applied to U_n^closure gives
the RHS as r(chi_{U_n^closure}) + o(1). Since chi_{U_n} -> chi_{U_n^closure}
(localized), the difference is again o(1). This completes the moving-
domain four-change identity.

This concludes the Sections 22-23 supplement.

A.7. Supplement to Section 25 (discrete peeling discipline)

A.7.1. Tile and switch conventions. Recall from Section 9: each
tile carries one of two non-crossing port-pairings (switches). Let
the tile grid approximate D and the FK sample on it (edges of primal
and dual lattices alternately colored primal/dual by the local
switch).

For the peeling, fix a target z_0 in the interior, not on any vertex
of the mesh for any n. Approximate z_0 by centers c_n(z_0) of tiles
at mesh level n, so |c_n(z_0) - z_0| <= delta_n / sqrt(2). The
difference vanishes with the mesh.

A.7.2. Exploration process. Start at a boundary vertex a_0 (chosen
once and for all, in the flat boundary of D). Repeatedly grow a
strand:

    - The current "wall" of the domain is a union of monochromatic
      primal or dual polygon segments (the "current wall color" is
      the unique color shared by all boundary tiles of the current
      wall).
    - At each step, the strand hits a fresh tile (one whose switch
      is not yet revealed). Sample the switch (whether the tile is
      primal-open or dual-open). If the newly revealed diagonal is
      consistent with the current wall color, extend the wall. If
      it cuts off a monochromatic pocket (a component of the
      complementary region not containing c_n(z_0)), peel it off.
    - Stop the strand when it hits the boundary again (at a_0 or
      another primed boundary vertex).

A.7.3. Round and episode. A "round" is one full traversal of the
target c_n(z_0) by the strand (one full counterclockwise winding in
the current domain). An "episode" is the interval of the strand
between two successive peels. Two consecutive episodes share a
peel point.

An episode is "successful" if its terminal switch changes the
boundary color (i.e., peels a monochromatic pocket whose color is
opposite the current wall color; i.e., the target is now inside the
new target pocket with a strictly smaller enclosing wall). A
"successful round" is one in which at least one successful episode
occurs.

A.7.4. Containment lemma (25.1). Every loop of the medial-loop
collection of the FK sample is peeled exactly once by the peeling
strand.

Proof by induction on the loop nesting structure. Consider the
outermost loop surrounding c_n(z_0). Every tile switch of the strand
either extends the current wall (staying on the same side of the
current wall with respect to the outermost loop) or peels a
homogeneous pocket. The first switch that puts the strand strictly
inside the outermost loop (in the "primal color" of the medial
drawing) corresponds to the terminal switch of an episode that peels
the outermost loop. This episode's terminal switch changes the
boundary color, so it is successful. So the outermost loop is peeled
exactly once.

Iterating: remove the outermost loop, apply the same argument to
the next-outermost loop, and so on, terminating when no loops
remain. The induction is finite because the number of loops in the
box is finite almost surely (Section 9).

A.7.5. Winding identification. A successful episode's terminal
switch is exactly a boundary color change at the target. The loop
enclosed by the peeled pocket winds around c_n(z_0), so it is a
"target-surrounding" loop. Its discrete trace L_z^{n,k} is well-
defined (as the peeled pocket boundary), uniquely so, because the
wall at the peel point is monochromatic of a single color.

A.7.6. Blow-up bound near the target. For any positive r_0 <
dist(c_n(z_0), dD) and any bounded horizon T (number of episodes
per round or per peel), the probability that a full round
encounters c_n(z_0) at any point within r_0 is bounded by C (r_0
/ delta_n)^(-c) for some c > 0 depending on q.

Mechanism. Within r_0 of c_n(z_0), the strand is a full winding
around a compact set. By planarity, such a winding produces two
disjoint primal or dual arms across the annulus of radii r_0/2 and
r_0/4 (in the local chart at c_n(z_0)). The two-arm probability
across a fixed-aspect annulus for q fixed is bounded below away
from 0 (RSW) and above by a power of (r_0 / delta_n) (Lemma 12.c
with two arms). Iterating over the finitely many tiles within
distance r_0, we get the bound.

A.7.7. Number of rounds and peels per round. In a bounded horizon,
the number of episodes is O(1) with probability -> 1. Specifically,
each successful episode removes one loop; each unsuccessful episode
extends the wall; the total number of tiles within a fixed horizon
is O(1) with probability 1 - o(1), by the same two-arm decay as in
A.7.6.

A.7.8. Target protection between peels. Throughout a round, the
target is surrounded by a monochromatic pocket of color opposite
the current wall (the "target pocket"). The target pocket's
interior contains c_n(z_0) and has diameter at least r_0 with
probability -> 1 on bounded horizons. So the strand never chops off
a piece of the target pocket, by the definition of unsuccessful
episodes (they extend the wall without peeling; the target remains
enclosed).

A.7.9. Quantitative concatenation. After each peel, the next round
runs on the reduced domain. By A.7.4-A.7.8, the reduced domain is a
bounded Jordan domain with the target strictly interior and the
basepoint chosen at the peel point. The peel point is at some
positive distance from the target, bounded below by the target
protection (A.7.8). This basepoint becomes the new starting point
of the next strand.

Theorem 25.2 (concatenation of strands) is then a direct
consequence: the conditional law of the next round's strand, given
the history, is the Dobrushin-measure strand of the reduced domain
with the basepoint at the peel point. By Theorem A (chordal SLE
convergence), this strand converges in law to SLE_kappa in the
reduced domain. The mesh -> 0 limit of the concatenation is a
sequence of SLE_kappa strands, one per peeled loop.

A.7.10. The tree structure. Recursively, the "peeling tree" of a
fixed target z_0 is the collection of episodes, organized as a tree
by peel order (episode 1 at the root; each episode's peel terminates
the current strand, and the next strand is the child branch).

The tree has the following interpretation: each leaf corresponds to
a loop; each internal node corresponds to a homogeneous
monochromatic pocket; the depth of a loop in the tree is its nesting
depth in the whole-plane loop collection.

Because the peeling recovers all loops (A.7.4) and records their
nesting order (A.7.5), the tree structure matches the FK medial-loop
collection exactly, as a rooted tree of loops.

This concludes the Section 25 supplement.

A.8. Supplements to Sections 27-29 (joint tree, loop recovery, whole
plane)

A.8.1. Clock transformation between branches (Section 27). Fix two
targets z_1, z_2 in the initial domain D, both strictly in the
interior. Let Phi_j be the disk chart of branch j (so z_j maps to
0). While both targets are in the same component of the current
domain (i.e., before their kernels separate), put xi = Phi_j(z_i)
for the other target's image.

The Loewner radial map g_t^j acts on the disk. Its action on the
two independent clocks is given by the transformation:

    dt_i  =  P^2 dt_j,                                            (A.8.1)

where P = (1 - |xi|^2) / |e^{i W_j} - xi|^2 is the Poisson kernel
of the disk at boundary point e^{i W_j} and interior point xi. This
is the standard transformation of the radial Loewner clock under a
conformal change of the normalizing disk.

Additionally, the clock drives the point xi as:

    d log |xi|  =  P dt_j,                                        (A.8.2)

which is the "occupation density" of the branch j's driver on the
circle of radius |xi|.

Derivation sketch. Use Loewner's radial ODE for g_t^j. Under the
conformal map M_j from D to disk, both branches j and i share the
underlying domain. The transformation of the Loewner parameter (the
radial capacity of the current hull) under a conformal map of the
domain is given by the Schwarz derivative; in the disk, this reduces
to the Poisson kernel above.

A.8.2. Common clock parametrization. Let v denote the common total
clock v = sum_j t_j. While two targets share a component, the
derivatives dt_i/dv and dt_j/dv vary continuously and satisfy
(A.8.1)-(A.8.2). This is regular as long as |xi| < 1 and W_j is
not equal to arg(xi); both conditions hold by the localization and
single-target drift bounds.

A.8.3. Kernel separation event. Two branches i, j "separate" at a
stopping time S if the Loewner hulls H_i(S) and H_j(S) have disjoint
accessible components in the common domain (i.e., there is no
interior path in the common domain joining a fixed basepoint of
H_i(S) to a fixed basepoint of H_j(S) that stays inside both
principal domains).

The separation time S is measurable with respect to the joint
history up to S. After S, the two branches continue on different
components of the domain, which have been cut apart by the current
slit. They interact only through the boundary data.

A.8.4. Independence after separation (Knight's theorem). Let
F_S be the joint filtration up to S. On each event E_S in F_S with
positive probability, the conditional law of the two branches
continues as product Wiener motion:

    (W_{S+u}^{(i)}, W_{S+u}^{(j)})  |  E_S  ~  (W^{(i)', u}, W^{(j)', u})   (A.8.3)

with W^{(i)'}, W^{(j)'} independent standard Brownian motions. The
approach: restart both branches at S (i.e., reset their drivers to
zero), continue on their respective sub-domains (where the Loewner
ODEs are decoupled), use Doob-Meyer to decompose each as approximate
local martingale + drift, and apply Knight's theorem on the common
filtration. The bracket is deterministic (kappa dt_i and kappa dt_j
for the two branches) and the covariances are identically zero
because they act on disjoint components.

A.8.5. Significance. The independence-after-separation is the
Markov property of the target-invariant SLE tree. It says: given the
joint history up to kernel separation, the future of each branch is
a fresh chordal SLE_kappa on its own component, independent of the
other. This is the content of Sheffield 2009B Proposition 3.13 and
Section 4.2 for the tree. Matches the main-body Proposition 27.1.

A.8.6. Loop recovery (Section 28). Let L_z^{n,k} denote the k-th
successful peeled loop around the target z at mesh n. From Section
25 (A.7.4-A.7.5), the collection {L_z^{n,k} : k >= 1} exactly
enumerates the loops surrounding z in nested order (innermost k=1
first). Each L_z^{n,k} converges in the uniform curve topology,
modulo increasing parametrization, to the canonical limit loop
L_z^k of the target-invariant tree (Proposition 28.2).

Sketch. Winding stability: a closed discrete curve that winds once
around z (positive clearance from z) stays winding around z under
uniform mesh convergence. Tightness of loops in the spherical
matching topology (Section 29 below) provides the subsequence. The
identification with the canonical L_z^k uses the corresponding chordal
SLE_kappa branch (Section 26-27) and the target-invariant tree
construction.

A.8.7. Label equivalence (Lemma 28.3). For a fixed countable dense
set Z subset of D, and for all z, w in Z and k, j >= 1:

    L_z^k  =  L_w^j   (as collection elements)                    (A.8.4)
        <=>  the canonical limit loops of (z, k) and (w, j) coincide.

Forward: both labels give the same discrete curve at every mesh level
n -> uniform convergence -> same limit curve.

Backward: if two canonical limit loops coincide as curves, they must
have the same discrete origin. Suppose not; then at mesh n, they
would be distinct curve elements. But two distinct curve elements
would have a fixed Hausdorff distance (bounded below by a positive
constant depending on their individual diameters and separation), so
they could not converge to the same limit. So the origin is unique.

Subtlety: two curves can have the same image but be distinct
collection elements (multiplicity). This is handled separately by
the "distinct traversals after constant pauses" convention of
Section 28; multiplicities match, but the reverse implication
"same image <= same origin" needs the additional check that the two
different origins have matched winding (which they do, by both
winding once around their target).

A.8.8. Whole-plane topology (Section 29). The convergence of the
FK medial loop collection Gamma_delta to nested whole-plane
CLE_kappa(q) is in the spherical loop-matching topology (Section 1).
Recall the definition:

    d_curv(gamma_1, gamma_2) = inf over circular orientation-
    preserving homeomorphisms phi_1, phi_2 of
    sup_t |gamma_1(phi_1(t)) - gamma_2(phi_2(t))|_spherical,

with 0-distance curves identified. A matching of two collections is
a partial bijection that covers all loops of diameter > epsilon in
both collections, matches within distance epsilon, and leaves only
loops of diameter <= epsilon unmatched.

A.8.9. Target-invariant CLE_kappa from the tree. The tree (Section
27) encodes a nested collection of surrounding loops for each target
z. In the limit, this is the whole-plane nested CLE_kappa, matching
Sheffield 2009B (whole-plane CLE construction) and Miller-Sheffield
IG1/IG3 (CLE uniqueness and independence).

A.8.10. Escape tails. For every fixed K > 0 and every epsilon > 0,
the probability that any loop of Gamma_delta has diameter > epsilon
and intersects both B(0, K) and the complement of B(0, 4K) tends to
0 as mesh -> 0. This uses:

    - Lemma 12.c (arm-decay) on the scale-log annulus [K, 4K].
    - A counting argument over loops (bounded number of loops of
      diameter > epsilon, since their images must pass through the
      bounded window in a bounded number of visits).

Consequence: at matching accuracy epsilon with window K large enough
(spherical diameter of exterior < epsilon), only loops wholly inside
B(0, 4K) matter. These are governed by the local convergence of
Section 28 (a bounded number of them, tight and identified).

A.8.11. Mobius invariance. The whole-plane CLE_kappa is invariant
under all conformal automorphisms of the Riemann sphere (Miller-
Sheffield IG3, Gwynne-Miller-Qian 2018). The FK limit inherits
this:

    - The lattice model is invariant under lattice translations and
      rotations (modulo lattice renormalization/mesh).
    - The scaling limit, if it exists, must be invariant under the
      closure of the lattice symmetry group, which is the full
      Mobius group.

In practice, we apply a fixed Mobius map M (in the sphere) and
compare the FK limit of (M) applied to the sample to the FK limit of
the original. The bounded-window convergence and escape-tails
arguments give equality of the two limits on every fixed window K;
the full equality follows from the diameter-tail control (Section
29).

A.8.12. Assembling Theorem B. Combining:

    (i)  every loop of Gamma_delta is peeled exactly once (A.7.4);
    (ii) successful episoding gives the winding order (A.7.5);
    (iii) L_z^k converge (A.8.6);
    (iv) label equivalence (A.8.7);
    (v)  spherical topology with escape tails (A.8.8, A.8.10);
    (vi) Mobius invariance (A.8.11);

the free infinite-volume FK medial-loop collection, scaled by delta
and taken as delta -> 0, converges in law to nested whole-plane
CLE_kappa(q) in the spherical loop-matching topology. QED.
