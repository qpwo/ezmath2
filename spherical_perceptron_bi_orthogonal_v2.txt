THE PROBLEM, IN ONE SENTENCE
----------------------------

We take many random linear measurements of a point on a sphere, weight
each point by "how activated" its measurements are, and ask what the
log total weight per dimension converges to as the dimension grows.

THE ANSWER, IN ONE SENTENCE
---------------------------

It converges, to an explicit variational formula. The formula splits
into:
  (a) a term for how the input squared norm is divided among the
      singular-value groups of the measurement matrix;
  (b) a "block-angular" pressure that depends only on the group
      dimensions, their singular values, and the activation f.

BOTH the formula AND its proof are covered below. Parts 0-1 give the
setup and result; Parts 2-4 sketch the proof; Parts 5-6 say what
happens at the edges; Part 7 has the glossary.


PART 0 - THE STORY
==================

0.1 What is a perceptron?
-------------------------

In mathematics, a perceptron is a simple "decision rule" based on a
weight vector w.

  - Pick a weight vector w with N real coordinates.
  - For each pattern x (also a vector in R^N), compute the scalar
        (w . x) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + ... + w_N x_N.
  - Pass this scalar through a "response" (or "activation") f.

The classic example is f(z) = sign(z). In machine learning, f might be
a smooth sigmoid. In our paper, f can be any bounded continuous
function.

0.2 Why a sphere?
-----------------

Physics puts spins on a sphere of fixed length because their total
magnetization is fixed. Mathematics likes a sphere because it has no
boundary, so no boundary conditions are needed.

The sphere we use is
     S^N = { w in R^N : w_1^2 + w_2^2 + ... + w_N^2 = N }.
This is the ordinary unit sphere scaled up by sqrt(N). Every w in S^N
has squared length exactly N.

Uniform probability measure on S^N is written sigma_N.

0.3 The pressure
----------------

Fix a matrix A with M rows and N columns. Each row of A is one random
pattern. For a weight w, the measurements are
     (Aw)_r = (row r of A) . w,      r = 1, ..., M.

The pressure (also called "free energy per dimension") is

     F_N(A, f)
       = (1/N) * log( INT_{w in S^N}
                        exp( sum_{r=1}^M f((Aw)_r) )
                        d sigma_N(w) ).

How to read this:
  INT     = integral over the sphere S^N, using uniform measure.
  exp(.)  = the exponential function e^z.
  log     = the natural logarithm.
  sum_r f((Aw)_r)  is the "activation energy" of w.
  e^{sum_r f}      is the "Boltzmann weight" of w.
  The (1/N) is there so the result scales as per-dimension.

If some w gives large f-values on many rows, it gets exponentially more
weight. If f is negative on most rows, w gets exponentially less
weight. The partition function "counts" w, weighted by how activated
they are.

0.4 What is "the disorder"?
---------------------------

We do not choose A. A is a random matrix, chosen once and held fixed.
Only w is integrated over.

In statistical physics language, the matrix A is the "disorder", and
the integral over w is the "thermal" integral. The pressure
(1/N) log Z still depends on A. Our theorem says: for a suitable class
of A, the pressure concentrates around a deterministic limit.

0.5 What is bi-orthogonal invariance?
-------------------------------------

A square matrix O is orthogonal if O^T O = I. Orthogonal matrices
preserve lengths and angles: ||O v|| = ||v|| for every v.

Say a random M-by-N matrix A has "bi-orthogonally invariant law" if

     A has the same law as U A V

for every pair of independent uniform orthogonal matrices
U (size M-by-M), V (size N-by-N), independent of A.

The word "bi" means "both sides" (left and right). Every such A is
equivalent in distribution to

     A = U S V^T

with U, V uniform orthogonal and independent of each other and of S,
where S is the (rectangular) diagonal matrix of singular values. So
the randomness of A lives ONLY in the "angles" U, V; the singular
values S are fixed (or at least independent).

Below we do NOT require A to be Gaussian or any specific form. We
require only:
  - bi-orthogonal invariance, this strong symmetry of the law;
  - a mild assumption on the singular values (stated in 0.6).

0.6 The spectral hypothesis
---------------------------

Write the singular values of A as s_1, ..., s_r, where r = min(M,N).
Write delta_a for the Dirac mass at a point a: the measure that puts
mass 1 on the single point a and 0 elsewhere. Let

     mu_A = (1/r) sum_{j=1}^r delta_{s_j}

be the empirical singular-value distribution, a probability measure
on [0, infinity).

We assume there is a deterministic compactly supported probability
measure nu on [0, infinity) such that, as N grows,

     (a) mu_A -> nu weakly,
     (b) max_{r} dist(s_{N,r}, supp nu) -> 0.

(a) says the distribution of singular values converges. (b) says no
singular value strays far from the support of nu. This is our "no
outliers" assumption. It is what makes the formula below exactly the
answer: without it, a single far-away singular value could shift the
pressure (see Section 6.1 for a concrete example).

Also define alpha = lim M/N (positive, and we treat only finite
positive alpha below).

-----------

We will:
  1. State the main theorem precisely  (Section 1.3).
  2. Unpack its variational formula    (Section 1.4).
  3. Split that formula into two layers, "radial" and "angular"

  4. Sketch the proof                  (Part 4).
  5. Note extensions and boundary behavior (Parts 5-6).
  6. Provide a symbol glossary             (Part 7).


PART 1 - THE MODEL AND THE MAIN THEOREM
=======================================

1.1 Notation
------------

- N, M       positive integers.  alpha = lim M/N in (0, infinity).
- A          real M-by-N matrix (our measurement matrix).
- S^N        sphere { w in R^N : sum_i w_i^2 = N }.
- sigma_N    uniform probability measure on S^N.
- f          bounded continuous function from R to R (the activation).
- r_N        min(M, N).
- s_{N,1}, ..., s_{N,r_N}   singular values of A, sorted decreasingly.
- delta_a    Dirac mass at the point a: delta_a(S) = 1 if a in S, else 0.
- supp nu    support of the probability measure nu.

1.2 The pressure
----------------

     F_N(A, f)
       = (1/N) log( INT_{w in S^N}
                    exp( sum_{r=1}^M f((Aw)_r) )
                    d sigma_N(w) ).

Symbols:
  (Aw)_r  is the r-th coordinate of the vector A w in R^M.
  exp{}   is the exponential function.
  log     is the natural logarithm.

1.3 The main theorem
--------------------

THEOREM (Main theorem).

Fix alpha in (0, infinity) and M = floor(alpha N). Let f be bounded
and continuous. Suppose A_N is bi-orthogonally invariant and the
spectral hypothesis of Section 0.6 holds, that is:

     (1/r_N) sum_{r=1}^{r_N} delta_{s_{N,r}}  ->  nu  weakly
            in probability,
     max_{r <= r_N} dist(s_{N,r}, supp nu) -> 0  in probability,

for a fixed compactly supported probability measure nu on [0, infinity).

Then the pressure F_N(A_N, f) converges in probability AND in mean
to a deterministic number F_{alpha,nu}(f).

That is the theorem. The rest of the paper is (i) an explicit formula
for F_{alpha,nu}(f), and (ii) a proof that the formula is correct.

1.4 The formula
---------------

The formula comes in two layers.

LAYER 1 - Block approximation of the singular spectrum.
Approximate the singular values by finitely many positive groups
     s_1, ..., s_h  with  multiplicities  n_1, ..., n_h,
such that n_j / M -> p_j > 0.

Define v_j = alpha p_j. Then sum_j v_j = min(alpha, 1). To see why,
use sum_j p_j = lim (sum n_j)/M = lim r_N/M = min(1, 1/alpha), so
sum_j v_j = alpha * min(1, 1/alpha) = min(alpha, 1).

If alpha >= 1, sum_j v_j = 1.
If alpha < 1, sum_j v_j = alpha < 1.

In the second case, there are N - r_N = N - M = N(1 - alpha) rows of
A that are all zero (structural null input directions). So we
INTRODUCE an extra "null block" of size v_0 = 1 - alpha, with singular
value s_0 = 0. Now sum_{j=0}^h v_j = 1 again.

So the block approximation gives us:
     I   =  { 1, ..., h }             if alpha >= 1,
     I   =  { 0, 1, ..., h }          if alpha < 1.
And we have v_j / alpha = p_j (block proportions of output space) and
v_0 = 1 - alpha (the structural null input).

LAYER 2 - Radial formula.
Let rho_i be the FRACTION of input squared norm assigned to block i.
That is, rho_i >= 0, sum_{i in I} rho_i = 1, and the input has squared
norm N * rho_i in block i.

Then define
     F_{approx}(alpha, nu, f; (s_j, p_j))
       = sup over rho_i > 0, sum rho_i = 1, of
           { (1/2) sum_{i in I} v_i log(rho_i / v_i)
             + alpha * P_p^f( ( s_j^2 * rho_j / alpha )_{j=1}^h ) }.

Here (the key point):
  P_p^f(a) is the ANGULAR PRESSURE, defined in Part 2. It depends only
  on the block proportions p_j, the activation f, and the
  "per-output-coordinate squared norm per block" vector
     a_j = s_j^2 * rho_j / alpha.

Then
     F_{alpha,nu}(f)  =  lim   F_{approx}(alpha, nu, f; (s_j, p_j))
                        (spectral mesh -> 0).

The limit is over finer and finer approximations of the singular
spectrum nu by finite groups (s_j, p_j).

How to read the formula:
  - The first term  (1/2) sum_i v_i log(rho_i / v_i) comes from the
    Dirichlet distribution on how squared norm splits between blocks.
    It is a purely combinatorial entropy: information-theoretic cost
    of splitting a sphere across h+1 subspaces.
  - The second term  alpha * P_p^f(...) comes from the "directional"
    part of the sphere integral. Once the norms are fixed, the
    directions inside each block become independent and uniform on
    their own spheres. The angular pressure P_p^f is the leading
    contribution from the directions. The factor alpha converts a
    per-M pressure into a per-N pressure.
  - The supremum over rho says: the system "chooses" the best split
    of the input norm across the singular-value blocks, trading off
    entropy (Dirichlet combinatorial count) against activation.

1.5 About J_p and H_p
---------------------

The terms J_p and H_p that will appear in the angular formula below
are geometric normalizations, not activation effects:

  - J_p = (1/2) int_0^p log(1 - u) du  is the limiting logarithmic
    Jacobian of the Gaussian-orthogonalization from coefficients to
    fields. It is negative and purely geometric: it quantifies how
    much "extra space" the linear map w -> G w eats up when going
    from N coefficients to M measurements.
  - H_p(a) = (1/2) sum_j p_j log(2 pi e a_j / p_j)  is the limiting
    entropy per coordinate of a product of Euclidean shells of the
    given block radii a_j. It is the classical "volume of a sphere
    of squared radius M a in M dimensions" contribution.

Neither term depends on the activation f. For the trivial activation
f = 0 the pressure is exactly 0, because the integral is the total
mass of a probability measure. The formula reproduces this: the
J_p - H_p terms and the Gaussian-limit terms combine to cancel.
For a first reading, remember only this: J_p and H_p are volumes of
spheres and Jacobians of linear maps. They are needed for
bookkeeping, not because the activation does anything interesting.

1.6 Unpacking the angular pressure
----------------------------------

The angular pressure P_p^f(a) is defined in Part 2 as the pressure of
a Gaussian-reference model on fixed Haar subspaces of R^M of sizes
p_j M. It has its OWN variational formula (given in Part 3 and 4).

For now, the structure is:

     F_{alpha,nu}(f)
       =   SUP over (rho_i) of
              [ Dirichlet entropy + alpha * (Angular pressure) ]

and        Angular pressure  = (J_p - H_p(a)) + (Gaussian limit).

So there are three "insides":
  (i)   the Dirichlet entropy on rho,
  (ii)  the J_p - H_p(a) term,
  (iii) the Gaussian-limit term.

The main theorem above says these three combined together give the
right answer. The rest of the paper is really about computing (iii)
rigorously.

1.7 Why this is hard
--------------------

Three difficulties stand out.

DIFFICULTY 1 - Fixed singular values make the matrix entries
dependent.
If A is Gaussian i.i.d. (the classical perceptron), then rows of A are
independent and the covariance of every measurement is easy to
compute. With fixed singular values and Haar angles, different rows
are NOT independent. In particular, the covariance of row r with row
s depends on which blocks they belong to. The "replica-symmetric"
calculations of Gardner, Derrida, Shcherbina, Tirozzi etc. do not
directly apply.

DIFFICULTY 2 - The angular pressure is not a classical spin-glass
formula.
Classical spin-glass formulas (Parisi, Sherrington-Kirkpatrick,
p-spin) are for Gaussian i.i.d. disorder. Our angular pressure is on
FIXED orthogonal subspaces, and the disorder is a Haar matrix acting
between them. Adapting "cascade" methods from parabolic to spherical
constraints requires care.

DIFFICULTY 3 - Bounded continuous activations, not just hard
thresholds.
The classic Gardner problem uses f = step function (hard constraint).
General bounded continuous f needs a full variational analysis; even
smoothness is not enough, because the pressure is a genuine
limit-of-limits.

The paper's technical contribution is to handle all three. In Part 4
we sketch HOW.


PART 2 - THE ANGULAR PRESSURE
=============================

In Part 1, the main theorem's radial formula (equation for F_{approx})
reduces the whole problem to a single object:

     P_p^f(a)      the ANGULAR PRESSURE.

This is the pressure of a Haar-random model on fixed subspaces of
R^M. It contains only the "directional" part of the sphere integral.
This part defines P_p^f and states its explicit formula. Part 3
provides the Gaussian engine that computes it. Part 4 sketches why
that engine is correct.

2.1 Splitting the sphere into blocks
------------------------------------

Suppose for now M = N. Any w in S^N can be written, block by block, as

     w  =  sqrt(N rho_1) theta_1 + ... + sqrt(N rho_h) theta_h

where:
  - rho_j >= 0, sum_j rho_j = 1,   the fraction of squared norm in
    block j;
  - theta_j is a UNIT vector in the j-th block, so ||theta_j|| = 1.

For a uniformly random w, the joint law of (rho_1, ..., rho_h) and
(theta_1, ..., theta_h) has a product form:
  - the theta_j are independent uniform unit vectors in their blocks;
  - the vector rho is Dirichlet(n_1/2, ..., n_h/2), the standard
    Dirichlet distribution from statistics. Its density on the simplex
    is proportional to rho_1^{n_1/2 - 1} ... rho_h^{n_h/2 - 1}.

Under this split, the measurement A w becomes

     A w  =  G( sum_j sqrt(N rho_j) theta_j )
          =  sum_j sqrt(N rho_j) G theta_j.

Block j has image subspace E_j (a Haar random subspace of R^M of
dimension n_j). So G theta_j is a vector in E_j, and we can write

     y_j  =  s_j sqrt(N rho_j) G theta_j = sqrt(M a_j) u_j

with a_j = s_j^2 rho_j / alpha and u_j a UNIT vector in E_j. So the
measurement vector A w is exactly sum_j y_j with y_j in E_j and
||y_j||^2 = M a_j.

Crucially, the y_j are INDEPENDENT "directional" random variables once
rhos are fixed. Because:
  - the theta_j are independent uniform vectors;
  - the Haar right rotation V^T acts on theta_j by turning it into a
    uniform unit vector in E_j (by rotation invariance).

So the directional integral factorizes block by block:

     INT  exp( sum_r f( sum_j y_{j,r} ) )  prod_j d sigma_{E_j,M a_j}(y_j).

This is EXACTLY the definition of the angular pressure below.

2.2 The formal definition
-------------------------

Fix proportions p_1, ..., p_h > 0 with sum_j p_j = p in (0, 1]. Choose
n_j such that n_j / M -> p_j.

Let E_1, ..., E_h be MUTUALLY ORTHOGONAL HAAR subspaces of R^M with
dim E_j = n_j. "Haar" means their joint law is uniform over all
orthogonal flags of dimensions (n_1, ..., n_h).

For a_j >= 0, let

     sigma_{E_j, M a_j}

be the uniform probability measure on the sphere {y in E_j : ||y||^2 = M a_j}.
(When a_j = 0 this is the Dirac mass at y = 0.)

The angular pressure is

     P_M^f(a)  =  (1/M) * log( INT_{y_j in E_j, ||y_j||^2 = M a_j}
                                 exp( sum_{r=1}^M f( sum_j y_{j,r} ) )
                                 prod_j d sigma_{E_j, M a_j}(y_j) ).

Symbols:
  - y_j = sum_j y_{j,r} e_r is a vector in E_j of length M (the
    "field in block j"). Only p M of its entries are nonzero, because
    E_j sits inside a p M-dimensional subspace.
  - sum_j y_{j,r} is the r-th measurement, summed over blocks.
  - prod_j d sigma = product of independent sphere measures, one per
    block.
  - (1/M) log = per-output-coordinate pressure.

The E_j are HELD FIXED inside the integral; their randomness is outside.
So P_M^f(a) is a random variable. As N grows it concentrates.

2.3 The angular theorem
-----------------------

THEOREM (Angular formula).

Fix p_1, ..., p_h with sum p_j <= 1 and f in C_b(R). Then P_M^f(a)
converges in probability, uniformly on compact sets of a in
[0, infinity)^h, to a deterministic continuous function P_p^f(a).
Its explicit value is (2.4)-(2.5) below.

2.4 The angular formula
-----------------------

Set

     s_a     =  sum_j a_j
     H_p(a)  =  (1/2) sum_j p_j log( 2 pi e a_j / p_j )
     J_p     =  (1/2) int_0^p log(1 - u) du

Here e = 2.718... is Euler's number, pi = 3.1415... is the usual
circle constant, log is the natural logarithm.

The formula is

     P_p^f(a)
       =  J_p - H_p(a)
          + lim_{lambda -> infinity}
            lim_{B     -> infinity}
            lim_{theta -> 0}
            B_{p,a}(kappa, lambda, B, theta),

where kappa is a positive "coefficient radius" (any large enough fixed
value works; the formula is independent of kappa in the appropriate
range) and B_{p,a} is defined in 2.5.

The three limits are in the order theta -> 0, then B -> infinity, then
lambda -> infinity. The point is that all three limits exist, and
together with the J_p - H_p(a) term they reproduce the right angular
pressure.

2.5 The soft-coefficient pressure
---------------------------------

To define B_{p,a}(kappa, lambda, B, theta), let d_j = h - j + 1.

For each j, let R_j be an upper-triangular d_j-by-d_j matrix with
positive diagonal and column norms at most kappa.

Let xi_j be a vector in R^{d_j}. (Picture xi_j as ONE sample of a
d_j-dimensional row field.) Given R_1, ..., R_h and a row sample
(xi_1, ..., xi_h), define two scalars:

     y    =  sum_j (xi_j^T R_j)_{x,j}
     u_i  =  sum_{j <= i} (xi_j^T R_j)_{z_i,j}   for i = 1, ..., h-1

where:
  - xi_j^T R_j is a row vector of length d_j.
  - (xi_j^T R_j)_{x,j} means its FIRST entry. Call this the
    "x-coordinate".
  - (xi_j^T R_j)_{z_i,j} means its (i - j + 1)-th entry, for i >= j.
    Call this the "z_i-coordinate".
  - d_j = h - j + 1 gives exactly enough entries for one x plus
    (h - j) z_i's, for i = j, ..., h-1.

Define the ROW FUNCTIONS g and constants c by

     g_1  =  y^2                             c_1  =  s_a
     g_2  =  -min(y^2, B)                    c_2  =  -s_a
     g_{i+2}  =  (y - u_i)^2  (i=1..h-1)     c_{i+2}  =  sum_{j>i} a_j

So we have h+1 "row tests", each a scalar function of (y, u_1, ...,
u_{h-1}).

The B functional is

     B_{p,a}(kappa, lambda, B, theta)
       =  sup_{R_1, ..., R_h}
          [  sum_j p_j * (  log( kappa * sqrt(2 pi e / p_j) )
                          + log( R_j,11 / kappa )
                          + theta * sum_{l=2}^{d_j} log( R_j,ll / kappa )  )
           + theta * (  min_{0 <= t_l <= lambda/theta (each l)}
                         [ t . c
                           + G_{theta,p}( f(y)/theta - t . g )  ]
                     )
          ].

How to read the terms:
  - The FIRST COLUMN of R_j (index l = 1) has weight p_j (no theta
    factor). This is the "outer" or "shared" coordinate.
  - The REMAINING columns (l = 2, ..., d_j) have weight p_j * theta.
    These are "inner" or "visit" coordinates; they shrink as theta -> 0.
  - t = (t_1, ..., t_{h+1}) is a Lagrange multiplier vector with
    entries in [0, lambda/theta].
  - t . c  means  sum_l t_l c_l.
  - t . g  means  sum_l t_l g_l  (applied per row).
  - f(y)/theta - t . g  is the "penalized terminal" (one value per
    row).
  - G_{theta,p}(.) is the Gaussian functional, defined in Part 3.

The B functional is a "soft" version of the constraint problem: we
sup over coefficient matrices R_j (choice of frame) and min over soft
penalties t_l (Lagrange multipliers for the row-average constraints
g_l <= c_l). Sending lambda -> infinity forces the constraints to be
hard; sending B -> infinity lets y be unbounded; sending theta -> 0
kills the coefficients' entropy.

2.6 The special case sum p_j = 1
--------------------------------

When sum_j p_j = 1 (the h blocks already exhaust the whole p-dimensional
output space, so p = 1), the formula P_p^f(a) as stated above would
diverge, because H_p(a) blows up as any a_j -> 0. The theorem handles
this by a limit:

     P_p^f(a)  =  lim_{eta -> 0}  P_{(1 - eta) p}^f(a)     (a fixed).

That is: drop a tiny fraction of the output dimension to make sum p_j
strictly less than 1, apply the formula, then let eta -> 0.

2.7 What the formula says in words
----------------------------------

The formula converts a hard integral over Haar subspaces into a
Gaussian problem with linear coordinate formulas and soft constraints.
Every piece has a clean meaning:

  - The outer sup over R_j is the entropy of the Haar-triangular
    frame parameters. It is the same type of expression as the volume
    of "how many frames fit in a ball of radius kappa".

  - The inner min over t_l is the Lagrange dual of the constraints
    g_l <= c_l (on average). The constraints encode:
      * y^2 = s_a (the field has the right squared norm),
      * -min(y^2, B) = -s_a (also: y^2 does not exceed s_a, as a
        one-sided constraint),
      * (y - u_i)^2 <= sum_{j>i} a_j for every i (the "witness"
        u_i successfully certifies that y's projection into the tail
        flag is small).

  - The limits theta -> 0, B -> infinity, lambda -> infinity strip
    away all the softness, recovering the EXACT hard Haar pressure.

For a first reading, you can skip the details of B_{p,a} and just
remember:
     P_p^f(a) is a well-defined limit, computed via a Gaussian
     reference model with projection witnesses.



PART 3 - THE GAUSSIAN ENGINE
============================

3.1 Why Gaussian?
-----------------

The key fact is due to Mezzadri (2007): you can build Haar flags with Gaussian randomness.

     GENERATE a rectangular Gaussian matrix G with i.i.d. N(0, 1/M)
     entries, of size M x n where n = n_1 + n_2 + ... + n_h.

     ORTHOGONALIZE the first n_1 columns (Gram-Schmidt) -> subspace E_1.

     Continue with columns n_1+1, ..., n_1+n_2 -> subspace E_2.

     And so on.

The resulting (E_1, ..., E_h) has exactly the Haar uniform law.

Why it helps: spheres in E_j become Gaussian vectors in the columns of G.
Anything we can compute with Gaussians becomes accessible. In particular,
the "row field" x -> A x becomes a Gaussian field.

3.2 Two audiences
-----------------

A reader who wants the formula without the derivation can skip to
Section 3.6. A reader who wants to see HOW the Gaussian model arises
from the sphere geometry should read 3.3-3.5.

3.3 The powered partition function
----------------------------------

The angular pressure P_M^f(a) is (1/M) log Z with Z a product of sphere
integrals. The trick to smoothly turn this into a Gaussian problem is to
use a FRACTIONAL power.

Fix a positive exponent theta in (0, 1). Define

     Z_{M, theta}(H)
       =  [ INT_{X in Stiefel} ( INT_{A in inner-Stiefel}
                                  e^{H(X, A)}
                                  d mu_in(A) )
             ^theta
             d mu_out(X) ]
         ^{1/theta}

where:
  - H is a Hamiltonian of the form sum_{r=1}^M (a terminal function
    of the rows of (X, A));
  - d mu_out is the uniform Stiefel measure on X (outer columns);
  - d mu_in is the conditional uniform Stiefel law on A given X
    (inner columns);
  - The ^theta is on the inner integral; the ^{1/theta} is on the outer.

The "natural tilted path law" is: first sample X with density
proportional to (inner integral)^theta, then sample A from the tilted
inner Stiefel law with weight e^H.

Why theta? Three jobs:

  1. It encodes the SHARED-COORDINATE structure. The first column of
     X is shared at level theta; subsequent columns are visit
     variables. The fractional power encodes exactly the L^theta
     combination of outer and inner.

  2. It SMOOTHS the theta -> 0 limit. Witness entropy vanishes as
     theta -> 0, which is critical for the B functional's limit.

  3. It AVOIDS ANALYTIC CONTINUATION. There is no "replica" whose
     number is formally continued to a non-integer. The zeta_i (Section
     3.4) are all in (0, 1]; the theta-entry is a specific grid point.
     This is a genuine fractional-power argument.

3.4 Matrix paths
----------------

Fix a grid

     0 = zeta_0 < zeta_1 < ... < zeta_k = 1

with zeta_m = theta for some m. Define a PATH in each block j:

     0  <=  q_{j,0}  <=  q_{j,1}  <=  ...  <=  q_{j,k} = I_{d_j},

a nested sequence of symmetric positive-semidefinite matrices, with
the PINNING condition

     q_{j,i} e_1 = e_1  whenever zeta_i >= theta,

i.e., the first coordinate stays fixed at and above theta. ("Sharing"
the outer coordinate across visits.)

The active space F_{j,i} matches the "unshared / shared" split:

     F_{j,i}  =  R^{d_j}  for zeta_i < theta,
     F_{j,i}  =  e_1^perp  for zeta_i >= theta,
     F_{j,k}  =  {0}.

(Once zeta_i = theta, the first coordinate e_1 is pinned, so the
"active" space can only vary in e_1^perp. At the very top, k, active
space is trivial.)

The susceptibility and precision are

     K_{j,i}  =  sum_{r = i+1}^{k} zeta_r (q_{j,r} - q_{j,r-1})
     P_{j,i}  =  (K_{j,i} |_{F_{j,i}})^{-1}

We extend P_{j,i} by zero outside its active space. A path is ELIGIBLE if
K_{j,i} |_{F_{j,i}} is positive definite on every nonempty F_{j,i}.

Define also the "reference" path q_j^0 with q_{j,i}^0 = 0 on active
coordinates and q_{j,i}^0 = I on shared coordinates, and the
corresponding K_{j,i}^0, P_{j,i}^0.

The ENTROPY of a path is

     2 S_theta(q_j)
       =  tr( P_{j,0} q_{j,0} )
        +  sum_{i=1}^{k} (1/zeta_i) log(
             (det K_{j,i-1} / det K_{j,i})
           * (det K_{j,i}^0 / det K_{j,i-1}^0)
           )

where each determinant is taken on the active space at its own index.

Intuition: S_theta is the "entropy cost" of the path, i.e., the
log-volume of tilts q_{j,i} that the field can take. It comes from
differentiating Gaussian densities at each cascade level and
integrating. The determinant ratio at each level measures how much
"volume" the tilt uses up.

3.5 The Gaussian functional
---------------------------

Let D: R^{sum_j d_j} -> R be a terminal function satisfying

     -C(1 + |z|^2) <= D(z) <= C   for some C < infinity.

Let Z_0, ..., Z_k be independent centered Gaussian vectors with
block-diagonal covariance matrices

     cov(Z_i)  =  diag_j ( q_{j,i} - q_{j,i-1} ).

So Z_i is a Gaussian "increment" at level i.

Define the BACKWARDS RECURSION:

     U_k(z)  =  D(z)
     U_{i-1}(z)  =  (1/zeta_i) log E[ exp( zeta_i U_i(z + Z_i) ) ]
                   for i = k, k-1, ..., 1.

Here E[.] is the expectation over the Gaussian Z_i. Then

     V_D(q)  =  E[ U_0(Z_0) ],

the last expectation over the root Gaussian Z_0.

The GAUSSIAN FUNCTIONAL is

     G_{theta, p}(D)
       =  inf_q  {  sum_j p_j S_theta(q_j)  +  V_D(q)  }

over all eligible path families q = (q_1, ..., q_h).

Intuition:
  - sum_j p_j S_theta is total "path entropy", weighted by block
    fractions p_j.
  - V_D is the EXPECTED value of D along a recursive cascade: D is
    the terminal, and each step "goes up" the cascade by adding a
    Gaussian increment Z_i.
  - The infimum over q chooses the best trade-off between path
    entropy and terminal evaluation.

3.6 The Gaussian theorem
------------------------

THEOREM (Gaussian formula and global penalties).

Fix theta in (0, 1), column counts d_j, and n_j / M -> p_j > 0. Note
that p_j can be any positive numbers; sum_j p_j need not be <= 1.

Let D be bounded continuous, or the sum of a bounded continuous
function and a NONPOSITIVE quadratic form.

Then

     (1/M) log Z_{M, theta}( sum_{r=1}^M D(xi_r) )
       ->  G_{theta, p}(D)

in probability and in expectation.

More generally, let g_1, ..., g_s be bounded continuous or NONNEGATIVE
quadratic forms, let gbar_l = (1/M) sum_r g_l(xi_r), and fix d > 0 and
c in R^s. The pressure for the soft-penalized Hamiltonian

     H  =  sum_r D(xi_r)  -  M d * sum_{l=1}^s (gbar_l - c_l)_+

converges in probability to

     min_{t in [0, d]^s}  {  t . c  +  G_{theta, p}( D - t . g )  }.

There is NO restriction on sum_j p_j; the soft-penalty extension is
exactly what the angular formula needs.

3.7 About "bounded or nonpositive quadratic"
--------------------------------------------

The condition "-C(1 + |z|^2) <= D(z) <= C" covers plenty:

  - Bounded continuous D (the base case).
  - Bounded continuous plus a negative-definite quadratic form. This
    is exactly what appears when enforcing "y has bounded squared
    norm" via a soft penalty (a negative quadratic is a "shrink"
    penalty).
  - NOT a positive quadratic. The behavior at infinity matters: a
    positive quadratic in D gives exponential growth that would break
    the variational formula. The theorem is capturing the fact that
    the terminal must pull probability IN, not let it grow.

The extension in the second paragraph of 3.6 is exactly the form used
by the angular formula: the row tests g_l in B_{p,a} are nonnegative
quadratics (y^2 and (y-u_i)^2), and their negative contributions to
the terminal are what the min over t handles.

Taking an infimum with a nonpositive quadratic form gives "negative
quadratic tails" that push probability OUT, without having exponential
growth that would break the variational formula.

In the specific angular setting, the terminal is

     D  =  f(y)/theta  -  t . g

which is bounded: f is bounded, g is a sum of quadratic and clipped
terms, and t is in [0, lambda/theta]^s. The soft constraints bring
their quadratic terms in with negative multipliers, so the overall
terminal fits the "bounded or bounded-plus-negative-quadratic" pattern.

3.8 What theta is doing, once more
----------------------------------

For a high-school reader, the exponent theta in Z_{M, theta} has three
"jobs":

  1. It encodes the shared-coordinate structure. At theta, the first
     column of each Stiefel is "shared"; at later visits, it's fixed.
     This lets us test the "projection onto the flag" with witnesses.

  2. It makes the limit theta -> 0 smooth. The witness entropy
     vanishes as theta -> 0, allowing us to strip witnesses away at
     the end (as in the B functional in Part 2).

  3. It sidesteps the usual "replica symmetry breaking" machinery:
     no analytic continuation in the number of replicas is used. Only
     a genuine fractional power at positive theta.

This last point is a real conceptual simplification: the paper avoids
replica-parity-like continuation. Instead of "n copies, analytically
continued to non-integer n", it uses "one fractional root at positive
theta". The mathematical object is a genuine L^theta norm, not a
formal interpolation.

PART 4 - HOW THE GAUSSIAN THEOREM IS PROVED
===========================================

The Gaussian theorem of Part 3 is nontrivial. This part gives the shape
of the proof. No attempt is made to fill in all details; the goal is
that a reader can follow WHAT is being shown and WHY each piece works.

4.1 The two-sided plan
----------------------

The proof has two halves:

  - UPPER BOUND: comparison with an "enriched" free energy. Adapt
    Mourrat's supersolution method. Add extra Gaussian linear fields
    and Poisson-count patterns, take a "contact-point" minimization,
    and use the Ghirlanda-Guerra identities to build a cascade-shaped
    bound.

  - LOWER BOUND: cavity method. Use the Aizenman-Sims-Starr variational
    approach. Restore patterns one at a time (or a large but bounded
    chunk), compare the empirical overlaps of new spins to physical
    overlaps of the enlarged system, and use a Gaussian computation
    on a "thick shell" to match the two.

  - SOFT DUALITY: once the unpenalized formula is proved, add soft
    penalties via standard convex duality.

4.2 Upper bound: comparison and contact point
---------------------------------------------

Fix a trial path family q. Enrich the pressure with independent linear
Gaussian fields (with covariance levels tied to q). Also Poissonise the
number of patterns: use Poisson(M r) patterns for r in [0, 1],
interpolating between 0 patterns (no disorder) and M patterns (the real
problem).

Add small random perturbations of the pattern Hamiltonian (the
"scalarization kernels") designed to enforce the Ghirlanda-Guerra
identities in the limit.

Then define a "contact-point functional"

     A_M(r, b, u)  =  -E P_M(r, b, u)
                     + entropy of q
                     + r ( V_D(q) + delta )
                     + penalty terms,

where P_M is the (perturbed, enriched) pressure and "penalty terms"
keep the perturbation parameters bounded.

If the desired upper bound FAILS at the trial q, then:

  - at r = 1 (all patterns), A_M(1, 0, u) is negative for large M;
  - at r = 0 (no patterns), A_M(0, b, u) is nonnegative (no constraint
    from missing patterns).

So there is an interior minimum with r > 0.

At the minimum:

  - Differentiating in r gives minus the expected log-pressure of one
    fresh pattern, bounded by -V_D(q) - delta. That is the UPPER bound
    we want.
  - Differentiating in b gives a "tail contact inequality" that, after
    summation-by-parts and using the covariance comparison
    V_D(q*) <= V_D(q), implies V_D(q*) <= V_D(q).

Together these contradict failure of the upper bound. The heavy lifting
is in proving the Ghirlanda-Guerra identities at the minimizing
parameters; this uses concentration (the pressure concentrates on a
bounded window of the perturbing variables). The scalarization kernels
provide the "test functions" needed.

The argument is closely related to Panchenko's vector-spin
synchronization theory, adapted to the present nonlinear pattern
Hamiltonian (matrix overlaps rather than scalar overlaps).

4.3 Lower bound: cavity and thick shell
---------------------------------------

The lower bound compares the model with M patterns to the same model
with M + ell patterns, where ell is a fixed "cavity size" (large but
very small compared to M). The goal is to show the pressure increment
is at least ell * G_{theta, p}(D), up to lower-order terms.

Steps:

  1. Fix an independent batch of ell fresh patterns. In the enlarged
     model, the spin overlaps of new "block spins" should concentrate
     around a "shell" where their empirical covariance is close to
     the identity.

  2. On the shell, the "new-coordinate overlaps" are related to the
     "whole-overlap" quantities R_j^{12} (whole-vector overlaps). By
     spatial rotation invariance, the empirical correlations between
     residuals approximate the physical correlations.

  3. Use a Gaussian computation on the shell to show that these
     empirical overlaps also match the CANONICAL PATH q*_j from
     Lemma 5.2 (the solution of the canonical duality equation).

  4. Match physical and canonical predictions: on the same thick
     shell, both the "physical overlap" prediction (from rotation
     invariance) and the "canonical overlap" prediction (from
     Gaussianity) refer to the same empirical quantity. Therefore q
     (physical) must equal q* (canonical) on the thick shell.

  5. Sum the pieces: the pressure increment equals ell times the
     canonical value, which equals ell * V_D-path with entropy
     accounted. This matches G_{theta, p}(D).

Key technical device: a HAAR PROJECTION VARIANCE bound showing that
physical and residual samples differ by O(ell^{-1/2}) in expectation
when ell is the cavity size. This is why "thick shell" (large ell) is
needed.

4.4 Soft duality
----------------

The Gaussian theorem in 3.6 has a "soft-penalty" version: the pressure
of

     H  =  sum_r D(xi_r)  -  M d * sum_{l=1}^s (gbar_l - c_l)_+

converges to

     min_{t in [0, d]^s}  {  t . c  +  G_{theta, p}( D - t . g )  }.

The proof is the standard convex-differential argument:

  Step 1 - Introduce the Lagrange multiplier. By the convex dual of "x_+
  is the sup over t >= 0 of t x", we can rewrite the soft penalty as

       -M d * sum_l (gbar_l - c_l)_+
       =  min_{0 <= t_l <= d}  M * (  t . c  -  t . gbar  ).

  This is the standard trick: an infimum over t of an expression linear
  in t with the "0 <= t <= d" bound.

  Step 2 - Substitution into the terminal. The resulting Hamiltonian is
  bounded (D is bounded), so the SOFT form of the Gaussian theorem
  applies with terminal D - t . g. This yields the "min over t" formula.

  Step 3 - Matching. Standard uniqueness of the minimizer at the
  optimum ensures the two sides agree.

4.5 The extra ingredient: weighted shell comparison
---------------------------------------------------

There is a subtle third step. Because we are replacing "sphere in
E_j" by "Gaussian coefficients", we lose the exact "shell" structure
of the sphere. The paper compensates with a weighted shell comparison
lemma.

Statement: Let b_j and a_j be nonnegative vectors with

     sum_j b_j  =  sum_j a_j             (same total)
     sum_{j<=i} b_j  >=  sum_{j<=i} a_j  for i=1, ..., h-1
                                        (prefix dominance)

after re-indexing so a_j/p_j is nonincreasing. Then the WEIGHTED shell
volume of b is at most that of a. In other words, moving weight toward
earlier blocks can only shrink the pressure.

Proof sketch: randomly subdivide Haar subspaces to redistribute the
mass (this is the transport equation of the original). A fixed vector
in the b-shell then concentrates into a slightly enlarged a-shell, with
a uniform failure bound per fixed vector. Using Fubini, this transfers
the weighted volume, even when the weight is concentrated. The
subdivision uses Dirichlet concentration of squared norms, which
applies uniformly to a fixed vector (no need for a simultaneous
concentration).

This is what allows the one-sided projection tests to recover the
angular pressure without needing a regular activation.

4.6 Summary of the angular proof
--------------------------------

Collecting the four pieces:

  - By Mezzadri, Haar subspaces <=> Gaussian matrix.
  - By the coefficient integral, Haar pressure = Gaussian pressure
    with explicit triangular frames.
  - By the Gaussian theorem, the Gaussian pressure equals
    G_{theta, p}(.) under soft constraints.
  - By the soft-duality limit (lambda -> infinity), soft constraints
    become hard.
  - By the weighted shell comparison, the hard constraints recover
    the correct sphere.
  - By theta -> 0, B -> infinity, we recover P_p^f(a).

Then, in a separate step, the radial decomposition (Section 1.4)
converts the main theorem's F_{alpha, nu}(f) into an optimization over
Dirichlet-distributed rho, with angular pressure P_p^f(a) as the
"inside". Combining the two gives the main theorem.


PART 5 - EXTENSIONS AND BOUNDARIES
==================================

The main theorem has some assumptions. Some can be relaxed; some are
unavoidable. This part collects the extensions. Part 6 gives concrete
examples showing why certain hypotheses cannot be dropped.

5.1 Arbitrary dimension sequences
---------------------------------

The main theorem uses M = floor(alpha N). The proof works for any
sequence (M_N, N_N) with M_N / N_N -> alpha in (0, infinity).

The only interesting case is alpha = 1 with N > M and k = N - M = o(N).
Here there are k "structural null input directions". These do not
contribute to the output. The same radial formula holds, provided we
use M (not N) in the denominators and let the "extra" null input space
take a Dirichlet share.

Idea of proof: compare the sphere in R^N to a BALL in R^M, using a
radius parameter. The measured field at radius sqrt(N v) has squared
norm approximately alpha s_N^2 v, uniform over the sphere. The two
endpoints, v small and v near 1, contribute negligibly. An integration
by parts over the ball radius then converts the sphere integral into a
ball integral, which is handled by the same machinery as before.

5.2 Edge intervals and sublinear outliers
-----------------------------------------

The assumption "every singular value stays in supp nu" (no outliers)
can be weakened. What is actually needed is control on the two edges
of the spectrum:

     s_-  =  min supp nu
     s_+  =  max supp nu

and the requirement that all but sublinearly many singular values lie
in [s_-, s_+]. That is, one needs:

     max_{r <= min(M,N)} dist(s_{N,r}, [s_-, s_+])  -> 0 in probability.

This is the edge-interval version of the main theorem.

Why the edge interval is enough: use the "extra Haar direction" trick.
If a sublinear number of singular values leak outside [s_-, s_+], one
or two extra Haar directions act as "fake blocks". The leaked weight
gets assigned to them, and the negative Dirichlet entropy
(1/2) v_i log(rho_i / v_i) with v_i = o(1) absorbs the effect as
rho_i -> 0.

Extra provision for alpha < 1: the interval can be [0, s_+], because
the structural null input space (of macroscopic dimension
N - M = (1 - alpha) N) already provides a legitimate zero endpoint.
At the other end, forced output zeros do NOT provide such an endpoint
(see Example 6.1 for what goes wrong).

Degenerate case: if both edges are zero (s_- = s_+ = 0), the operator
norm goes to zero and the pressure converges to alpha * f(0). The
formula handles this by specializing H_p(a) to its small-a behavior.

5.3 Unbounded activations with subquadratic growth
--------------------------------------------------

The main theorem assumes f bounded. It can be relaxed:

PROPOSITION (Activation extension, i).

Let f be continuous and satisfy |f(z)| / (1 + z^2) -> 0 as |z| ->
infinity. Then the pressure F_N(A_N, f) converges in probability to
the "final bounded-cutoff limit" of the main formula: the limit of
F_{alpha, nu}(f_R) as R -> infinity, where f_R = f * chi_R is a
bounded cutoff equal to f on [-R, R] and supported in [-2R, 2R].

If, in addition, the spectrum is uniformly bounded by a fixed
deterministic constant, convergence in mean also holds.

Idea of proof: take a bounded compactly-supported cutoff f_R and
compare to f. The error at any input x is at most

     (1/N) sum_r |f((Ax)_r) - f_R((Ax)_r)|
   <= epsilon_R * ( M/N + ||A||_op^2 )

where epsilon_R = sup_{|z| > R} |f(z)| / (1 + z^2) -> 0. On good
spectral events ||A||_op is bounded, so the pressure difference is
small. For mean convergence, a deterministic bound on ||A||_op is
needed, which is what "spectrum uniformly bounded by a deterministic
constant" provides.

Note: continuity alone is NOT enough. Example 6.4 shows f = z^8 can
diverge even with the spectrum converging to zero.

5.4 Bounded Borel activations
-----------------------------

The main theorem assumes f continuous. It can be relaxed:

PROPOSITION (Activation extension, ii).

Let f be bounded Borel and let nu != delta_0 (so nu has some mass away
from zero). Then the pressure F_N(A_N, f) converges in probability and
in mean. The limit is the limit of F_{alpha, nu}(g_k) over bounded
continuous g_k that approximate f in Lebesgue measure on compact
intervals, with |g_k| <= ||f||_infty.

Idea of proof: under nu != delta_0, good spectral events have
||A||_op bounded AND at least a positive fraction of singular values
bounded below by some s_* > 0. So the small-field set has
exponentially small mass. Concretely, for any L > 0 there is a small
lambda_0 > 0 such that:

     sigma_N{ x : ||Ax|| < lambda_0 sqrt M }  <=  e^{-LM}

for all large good spectra (this forces too much norm in the s_* >
subspace, and a Beta tail bounds that probability).

Conditional on input coefficients and singular values, Haar left
rotation makes the field a uniformly random vector on the sphere of
radius ||Ax|| sqrt M. Explicit densities for k small entries then
give:

     P( #{ r : (Ax)_r in B } >= k )  <=  binom(M, k) (C_0 delta / lambda_0)^k

for any measurable B of Lebesgue measure at most delta and any
k = ceil(gamma M) with 0 < gamma < 1/2.

So a bounded Borel f can be approximated by continuous g, and the
exceptional inputs where the approximation fails have exponentially
small probability. This is enough for probability and mean
convergence.

Note: nu = delta_0 is problematic. Example 6.3 shows that even a
simple indicator {z != 0} can jump between two different values along
the sequence.

5.5 Summary of Part 5
---------------------

The main theorem survives:
  - arbitrary (M_N, N_N) with M_N / N_N -> alpha,
  - sublinear spectral outliers in [s_-, s_+] (edge interval),
  - continuous subquadratic activations (|f(z)| / (1 + z^2) -> 0),
  - bounded Borel activations when nu != delta_0.

It does NOT survive:
  - sublinear outliers that hide in output zeros (5.2's edge interval
    with s_- = 0 must come from an INPUT null, not an OUTPUT zero),
  - unbounded spectral support with outliers,
  - nu = delta_0 with discontinuous f,
  - superquadratic continuous f with vanishing spectrum.

Part 6 gives four worked examples, one for each failure mode.


PART 6 - EXAMPLES SHOWING HYPOTHESES MATTER
===========================================

This part gives four worked examples. Each shows why one hypothesis
in the main theorem is ESSENTIAL: without it, the pressure can fail to
converge, or can converge to the wrong value.

6.1 Forced output zeros can hide a small singular-value outlier
---------------------------------------------------------------

Setup. Let M = 1000 N, so alpha = 1000. On alternating values of N,
choose the singular spectrum:

     EVEN N:  all N singular values equal sqrt(alpha).
     ODD  N:  same, but set ONE of the singular values to 0.

Everything else (Haar orientations) is as in the theorem.

Activation. Let f(z) = -c * min(z^2, 1), with c >= 1.

Analysis on even N. Since all singular values equal sqrt(alpha) and
orientations are Haar, A = sqrt(alpha) * U for U a Haar orthonormal
M-by-N frame. For a unit input u, the measurement sum is

     sum_r f((Au)_r)  =  -c sum_r min( M (Uu)_r^2, 1 )

(the M^{-1/2} scale of U means each (Uu)_r is typically 1/sqrt(M),
so M (Uu)_r^2 is typically of size 1). A spreading estimate (Gaussian
tails plus a small net on the unit sphere) gives

     P( inf_u (1/M) sum_r min(M (Uu)_r^2, 1) < 1/80 )  <=  e^{-M c_0}

for a universal c_0 > 0. Therefore on even N,

     pressure  <=  -c * alpha / 80  =  -12.5 c.

Analysis on odd N. One input direction is "null": the image of A is
only (N-1)-dim even though the input space is N-dim. Any unit u
decomposes as

     u  =  sqrt(V) * u_live + sqrt(1 - V) * u_null

where u_live is a unit vector in R^{N-1}, u_null is a unit vector in
the 1-dim null direction, and V is the squared norm fraction in the
live direction. Because u is uniform, V ~ Beta((N-1)/2, 1/2).

For fixed v in (0, 1), the event V <= v has log-probability per N
tending to (1/2) log v. On this event, at most v of the field survives,
so the energy is at least -c * v * M / M = -c v. Taking v = 1/160:

     pressure  >=  -0.5 * log 160  -  6.25 c
               ~  -2.5  -  6.25 c.

Comparison. For c = 1:

     even-N pressure  <=  -12.5
     odd-N  pressure  >=  -8.75

The limits differ. The theorem does NOT apply. But look at the
spectra: on both parities, the RECTANGULAR singular-value measure
(excluding the structural zeros) still tends to delta_{sqrt(alpha)}.
So it seems the "no outlier" hypothesis is satisfied!

The outlier HIDES in the output zero masses. The output eigenvalue
measure of the odd-N models is

     (1 - 1/alpha) delta_0  +  (1/alpha) delta_alpha,

and every output eigenvalue is EXACTLY in the support of this limit.
So support control on output eigenvalues is insufficient: the outlier
"hides" in the zero masses at the output.

Moral. No-outlier control must be on the RECTANGULAR SINGULAR SLOTS
(s_{N,r} for r = 1, ..., min(M,N)), not on the output eigenvalues.

6.2 Unbounded limiting spectrum
-------------------------------

Setup. Let d_k = exp(8^k) for k = 1, 2, ... and

     nu = sum_{k=1}^infinity 2^{-k} delta_{d_k}

so nu has total mass 1 but UNBOUNDED support (d_k -> infinity).

For large N, choose K = floor(log_2 log N). Set multiplicities

     n_j = floor(N 2^{-j})  for j < K,
     r = n_K = N - sum_{j<K} n_j,

so the top block is about N/2-dimensional (because
2^{-K} N / 2 <= r <= N/2 for all large N). Use these multiplicities
with Haar orientations. Every singular value is in nu's support.

Activation. Let f be any continuous function in [0, 1] that equals
c_K in {0, 1} on the ABSOLUTE-VALUE interval [d_K^{1/2}, d_K^2],
alternating in c_K with K, and interpolates in the gaps. The
intervals [d_k^{1/2}, d_k^2] are disjoint because
d_{k+1}^{1/2} = d_k^4.

Analysis. The top block, of dimension about N/2, has singular value
d_K = exp(8^K). Its field values are typically of size d_K, so on the
top block f evaluates to c_K almost surely. Smaller blocks contribute
negligibly because their singular values are dwarfed by d_K.

So the pressure, as N grows along the sub-K sequence, is:

     K even -> pressure -> c_K in {0, 1},
     K odd  -> pressure -> the other value in {0, 1}.

The pressure oscillates between 0 and 1 and does NOT converge.

Moral. Weak convergence to nu is not enough if nu has unbounded
support. Compact support of nu is essential.

6.3 Bounded Borel at zero spectrum
----------------------------------

Setup. Let M = N. Choose

     EVEN N:  A_N = 0 (all singular values zero).
     ODD  N:  A_N = s_N O_N, where s_N > 0, s_N -> 0, and O_N is Haar
              orthogonal.

Activation. Let f(z) = 1_{z != 0}, the indicator that z is nonzero.

Analysis.

     EVEN N:  A_N x = 0 for all x, so f(0) = 0 and sum_r f = 0.
              Pressure = 0.

     ODD  N:  for almost every x, A_N x has all coordinates nonzero
              (it's a scalar multiple of O_N x). So f((A_N x)_r) = 1
              for every r, and sum_r f = N. Pressure = 1.

Both spectral sequences converge to delta_0 (the typical singular
value is 0), with no outliers. So the main theorem's spectral
hypothesis is satisfied. But f is not continuous: it has a jump at 0.

Moral. Bounded Borel activations (with nu = delta_0) can fail. This is
why the extension in Section 5.4 requires nu != delta_0.

6.4 Continuous activation without a growth bound
------------------------------------------------

Setup. Let M = N. Choose

     EVEN N:  A_N = 0.
     ODD  N:  A_N = N^{-1/4} O_N, where O_N is Haar orthogonal.

So on odd N, all singular values equal N^{-1/4} -> 0. On even N they
are all exactly 0.

Activation. Let f(z) = z^8.

Analysis.

     EVEN N:  A_N x = 0 for all x, so f(0) = 0 and sum_r f = 0.
              Pressure = 0.

     ODD  N:  ||A_N||_op = N^{-1/4}. Let OMEGA = O_N x, a uniform
              vector of squared norm N. Then

                   (A_N x)_r  =  N^{-1/4} OMEGA_r,
                   sum_r (A_N x)_r^8  =  N^{-2} sum_r OMEGA_r^8.

              If OMEGA_1^2 is in [N/4, N/2], then OMEGA_1^8 >= N^4/256,
              so sum_r OMEGA_r^8 >= N^4/256 and hence

                   sum_r (A_N x)_r^8  >=  N^2 / 256.

              The probability of this coordinate event is at least
              (1/8) 2^{-(N-3)/2}. So by taking expectations:

                   pressure
                     >= (1/N) [ N^2 / 256
                                + log( (1/8) 2^{-(N-3)/2} ) ]

                     =  N/256 - (1/2) log 2 - (3 log 2) / (2N)

                     ->  +infinity.

So the pressure DIVERGES on odd N, while it stays 0 on even N. The two
spectral sequences both converge to delta_0.

Moral. Continuity alone is not enough; even on a spectrum that
converges to zero, a superquadratic f can make the pressure diverge.
This is why the extension in Section 5.3 wants |f(z)| / (1 + z^2) -> 0.

6.5 Summary of Part 6
---------------------

Four failures:

  6.1  alpha = 1000 with one zero singular value -> outlier hides in
       output zeros -> pressure jumps ~ -12.5 c to ~ -8.75 c (c=1).
       Fixed by requiring rectangular singular slots to stay in
       [s_-, s_+].

  6.2  nu = sum 2^{-k} delta_{exp(8^k)} (unbounded support) ->
       pressure oscillates 0 <-> 1. Fixed by requiring compact support.

  6.3  nu = delta_0, f = 1_{z != 0} (bounded Borel) -> pressure
       jumps 0 <-> 1. Fixed by requiring nu != delta_0 (Part 5.4).

  6.4  nu = delta_0, f = z^8 (superquadratic continuous) -> pressure
       jumps 0 <-> +infinity. Fixed by requiring |f|/(1+z^2) -> 0
       (Part 5.3).


PART 7 - GLOSSARY OF SYMBOLS
============================

Dimensions and the model
------------------------
  N              input dimension
  M              output dimension (number of patterns)
  alpha          lim M/N, in (0, infinity)
  r_N            min(M, N), number of singular slots
  A              M-by-N measurement matrix
  A w            the M-vector of measurements
  S^N            sphere of squared norm N
  sigma_N        uniform probability measure on S^N
  f              activation, bounded continuous on R by default
  F_N(A, f)      pressure: (1/N) log INT exp(sum_r f((Aw)_r)) d sigma_N

Singular values and spectrum
----------------------------
  s_{N,r}        r-th singular value of A
  delta_a        Dirac mass at a
  mu_A           empirical (1/r_N) sum delta_{s_{N,r}}
  nu             limiting probability measure on [0,infinity),
                 compact support, weak limit of mu_A
  s_-, s_+       min supp nu, max supp nu
  spectral hypot (a) mu_A -> nu weakly, (b) max_r dist(s_{N,r},
                 supp nu) -> 0

Block decomposition
-------------------
  h              number of spectral blocks
  s_j            representative singular value in block j, j=1..h
  n_j            output dimension of block j
  p_j            n_j / M
  v_j            alpha * p_j
  v_0            1 - alpha (structural null input block, alpha < 1)
  I              (1..h) if alpha >= 1, else (0, 1, ..., h)
  E_j            Haar subspace of R^M with dim E_j = n_j
  rho_i          squared-norm fraction in block i, sum = 1,
                 Dirichlet(n_1/2, ..., n_h/2) under uniform sphere
  a_j            s_j^2 * rho_j / alpha (per-coordinate squared norm)
  F_{alpha,nu}   the deterministic limit of the pressure

Angular pressure and B formula
------------------------------
  P_M^f(a)       angular pressure on the E_j
  P_p^f(a)       limit of P_M^f(a)
  J_p            (1/2) int_0^p log(1 - u) du
  H_p(a)         (1/2) sum_j p_j log(2 pi e a_j / p_j)
  kappa          coefficient radius (any large fixed positive value)
  d_j            h - j + 1
  R_j            upper-triangular d_j-by-d_j coefficient matrix
                 in B_{p,a}, positive diagonal, column norms <= kappa
  xi_j           row coefficient in R^{d_j}
  y, u_i         scalar row functionals (i = 1..h-1)
  g_l, c_l       row tests and targets (h+1 of each):
                   g_1 = y^2                    c_1 = s_a
                   g_2 = -min(y^2, B)           c_2 = -s_a
                   g_{i+2} = (y - u_i)^2        c_{i+2} = sum_{j>i} a_j
  B_{p,a}        sup over R_j, min over t_l <= lambda/theta, of
                 t.c + G_{theta,p}(f(y)/theta - t.g)
  lambda         soft-penalty multiplier cap (limit -> infinity)
  B              bound in min(y^2, B) (limit -> infinity)
  theta          fractional exponent in (0, 1) (limit -> 0)
  eta            small dimension drop when sum p_j = 1 (limit -> 0)

Gaussian engine
---------------
  St(n, d)       Stiefel manifold of n-by-d matrices X with X^T X = n I_d
  X_j            outer column of block j (Stiefel point)
  xi_{r,j}(X)    row field (X_j^T g_{r,j}) / sqrt(n_j); std. Gaussian
  Z_{M,theta}    powered partition function
                 [INT_X (INT_A e^H d mu_in)^theta d mu_out]^(1/theta)
  zeta_i         cascade grid: 0 = zeta_0 < ... < zeta_k = 1,
                 some zeta_m = theta
  q_{j,i}        covariance path in block j (0 <= q <= I, pinning)
  K_{j,i}        susceptibility: sum_{r>i} zeta_r (q_{j,r} - q_{j,r-1})
  P_{j,i}        precision: K restricted to F_{j,i}, then inverted
  F_{j,i}        active space: R^{d_j} before theta, e_1^perp after
  S_theta(q_j)   half-entropy of the path
  Z_i            Gaussian increment with cov diag_j (q_{j,i} - q_{j,i-1})
  U_i            backwards recursion terminal
  V_D(q)         E U_0(Z_0), expected root terminal
  G_{theta,p}    Gaussian functional: inf_q sum p_j S_theta(q_j) + V_D(q)

Conventions
-----------
  log = natural log. exp = exponential.
  | . | = Euclidean norm; || . || = operator norm.
  A <= B (matrices) means B - A is positive semidefinite (Loewner).
  A^T = transpose of A.

Closing note
------------

The main theorem answers a natural question about spherical perceptrons
with a broad class of disorder spectra: the pressure converges to an
explicit variational formula. The formula factors into a Dirichlet
entropy over how input norm splits between singular-value blocks, and
an "angular pressure" that is itself given by a Gaussian variational
problem with a shared-coordinate trick.

The proof is a long sequence of approximations: Gaussianization of
Haar spaces, a fractional-power trick, a supersolution upper bound,
a variational cavity lower bound, and a weighted shell comparison to
make the sphere structure exact.

If you followed Parts 0 through 3 and Parts 5 through 6, you have seen
the essential ideas. Part 4 and Part 8 provide the technical apparatus
and a reading list.
