MAXIMUM MATCHING IN ALMOST LINEAR TIME




If you only read one paragraph, read this: a matching is a set of
pairs. Finding the largest set of pairs in a general graph has been
studied for sixty years. The best bound from 1980 (Micali-Vazirani)
is about m times the square root of n. This paper claims the
square-root factor can be eliminated almost entirely, using a
sophisticated new data structure and a randomized analysis.

Two honest notes for the reader.
  (1) The argument is long, technical, and in a few places tight. Even
      specialists would want to read it carefully; if they tried to
      implement it, they would find the constants terrible. This guide
      focuses on WHAT the argument does and WHY each piece is there,
      not on the exact constants.
  (2) The proof carefully separates "proved on every path" from "with
      high probability" from "eventually, for large enough sizes." A
      real proof must keep these three apart. The guide will call them
      out whenever they matter, because slipping between them is how
      careful algorithm analysis goes wrong.


PART I. WHAT A MATCHING IS, AND WHY IT IS HARD


1. Matchings
============

Draw a dot for each person at a party. Connect two dots with a line if
the corresponding two people would be willing to be paired up. The
resulting picture is a GRAPH: the dots are VERTICES, the lines are
EDGES.

A MATCHING is a choice of edges, no two of which share a vertex. In the
party picture, a matching is a set of "sitting together at dinner"
pairs, each person in at most one pair. People not in any pair are
UNMATCHED.

A MAXIMUM MATCHING is a matching with as many pairs as possible. It is
a theorem (Berge, 1957) that if a matching is not maximum, there is an
"augmenting path" you can flip to improve it by exactly one pair. That
criterion is the entire basis of matching algorithms: repeatedly find
augmenting paths, flip them, and stop when none remains.

Examples to internalize.

  Two people, one line. Matching number 1.
  Four people, every pair connected (K_4). Matching number 2. Two
    pairs cover everyone. You cannot have three pairs, since that
    would need six people.
  Five people, every pair connected (K_5). Matching number 2. One
    person is left over. The odd count forces an unmatched vertex.
  One center connected to n leaves (a "star"). Matching number 1. The
    center pairs with only one of its many neighbors.
  Four people in a line, 1-2-3-4. Matching number 2, via the pairs
    {1,2} and {3,4} (or {2,3}, and then 1 and 4 are unmatched).

Matching is not a puzzle from a book. When a hospital assigns residents
to rotations, when a phone network pairs antennas without interference,
when a chemist matches bonds under constraints, it is finding a
maximum matching in some graph. Faster matching algorithms speed up
all of those applications.


2. Why matching is not easy
===========================

If the graph is BIPARTITE -- its vertices can be colored black and
white so that every edge has one black end and one white end -- then
matching has a clean characterization. Hall's theorem (1935) says a
perfect matching (one that covers every vertex) exists exactly when
"for every set X of black vertices, X has at least |X| white
neighbors." That is a real theorem, and it makes BIPARTITE matching
relatively tractable: search for a set of black vertices with too few
neighbors, and you find a certificate that no perfect matching exists.

For general graphs, Hall's theorem fails. The smallest counterexample
is the TRIANGLE -- three vertices, with all three edges between them.

  Try to pair all three. You cannot: a matching of size 1 covers two
  of them; the third has no partner. So a triangle has no perfect
  matching.

  But look at any single vertex. It has two neighbors. Hall's
  condition "for every subset X, |N(X)| >= |X|" is trivially
  satisfied here; no subset has fewer neighbors than its size.

The obstruction is the ODD CYCLE. Two odd cycles crossing, three odd
cycles whose pairwise intersections are small, or any combination of
odd cycles, all act as obstructions.

Tutte's theorem (1947) makes this precise. A graph has a perfect
matching if and only if, for every subset S of vertices, the number of
odd-sized connected components of "G with S removed" is at most |S|.
The odd components are exactly the generalized triangles.

Tutte's theorem gives a criterion but not an algorithm. Turning the
criterion into a fast algorithm is the entire history of matching.


3. Edmonds and the blossom
==========================

Tutte's theorem is an existence criterion. It does not directly give
an algorithm. The algorithmic breakthrough came in 1965 from Jack
Edmonds.

Edmonds' insight was that the odd-set obstruction is "shrinkable."
When you find an odd cycle of a particular kind during your search, you
can literally SHRINK it to a single artificial vertex ("a blossom")
and run your algorithm on the shrunken graph. At the end you "unshrink"
and read off the matching.

Three operations form the whole algorithm.

  Search. Build an "alternating tree": start from an unmatched vertex,
    and alternately add an unmatched edge (which enters a new vertex)
    and then a matched edge (which continues from it). Every vertex in
    the tree is either an "outer" vertex (at even depth, so a free
    edge can emanate) or an "inner" vertex (at odd depth, whose only
    tree-edge-with-lookahead is the matching edge).

  Augment. If the search finds an unmatched edge from an outer vertex
    to a free vertex, the tree plus this edge is an "augmenting path".
    Flip every edge along it (unmatched edges become matched, matched
    edges unmatched). The matching size goes up by one.

  Shrink. If the search finds an unmatched edge joining two outer
    vertices, the tree cycle that results has odd length. Contract it
    to a single "blossom" vertex and continue. Blossoms cannot be
    avoided; without them, augmenting-path search is not correct on
    general graphs.

Edmonds' algorithm runs one search per augmentation, and there are at
most n/2 augmentations. Since each search costs O(m) elementary steps,
the running time is O(mn). That is polynomial, which was a famous
achievement in the 1960s -- but it is not fast.

The reason each search costs O(m) and not less: you may have to look at
essentially every edge to be sure no augmenting path exists. Batching
searches is what Micali and Vazirani did next.


4. Micali-Vazirani and the modern baseline
==========================================

In 1980 Micali and Vazirani batched augmenting paths. Instead of
finding one augmenting path at a time, they find a MAXIMAL SET OF
SHORTEST augmenting paths at once, then "grow" the matching by that
whole batch.

The trick is to do the whole set of shortest augmenting paths with the
same O(m) search, and then prove that after the batch the next
shortest augmenting paths are strictly longer. That gives phases: in
phase k, find all shortest augmenting paths of length 2k+1, augment by
all of them, and move on. There are at most sqrt(n) phases, because
after sqrt(n) phases the shortest remaining augmenting path must be
long -- and once the path is longer than sqrt(n), a "shortcut" argument
lets you finish the rest of the algorithm with a simpler O(m) pass.

Total: O(m sqrt(n)). This has been the best published bound for
general graphs for more than four decades.

To summarize the state of the art in a compact table.

  For BIPARTITE graphs:
    1955, 1973: O(mn) and O(m sqrt(n)) -- matching is "easy" here.
    2013-2022: even faster, using electrical flows and continuous
      optimization. The current bound is essentially (n+m)^{1+o(1)}
      (Chen-Kyng-Liu-Peng-Probst-Sachdeva, 2022), which was a
      breakout result.

  For general graphs:
    1965: O(mn) -- Edmonds.
    1980: O(m sqrt(n)) -- Micali-Vazirani.
    1991-2018: weighted matching gets O(m sqrt(n) log W); cardinality
      stays near O(m sqrt(n)).
    2024: algebraic algorithms at matrix multiplication time.

Until this paper, the gap between bipartite and general graphs was
substantial: bipartite had an almost-linear algorithm, general did
not.


PART II. THE NEW ALGORITHM'S SHAPE


5. The main theorem, stated plainly
===================================

Here is the paper's central claim, in plain language.

  THEOREM. There is one fixed randomized algorithm (no hidden
  parameters) such that the following holds. Given any simple
  undirected graph on n vertices and m edges, run the algorithm with
  fresh random bits. With probability at least 2/3, it outputs a
  maximum matching. On every possible random choice it halts in at
  most  C * p^(1 + eta(p))  elementary steps, where p = n+m and C is a
  fixed constant. The function eta(p) is nonnegative, decreasing, and
  tends to 0 as p tends to infinity.

The "elementary steps" are counted in the standard word-RAM model: a
word of `c_0 log(n+2)` bits, and standard arithmetic, comparison, and
integer-division operations on a word cost one step. Random bits cost
one step each. This is the usual model for algorithmic results.

Two things about this theorem deserve emphasis.

  (1) It is a SINGLE algorithm. There is no input-dependent switching.
    The algorithm does not "detect that the input is easy and take a
    shortcut". It is one fixed program, with a fixed random-bit
    source, that happens to be correct on all inputs and to be fast
    on all inputs up to the 1 + eta(p) factor.

  (2) The "on every path" qualifier is important. This is a worst-
    case time bound, in the worst case over random choices. Even if
    the algorithm gets bad random bits, it still halts in C p^(1+eta)
    steps. It might return the wrong answer (with probability at most
    1/3), but it will not run too long.

For technically clean statements the paper also establishes an
"f-factor" corollary (Section 20 below): given one integer f(v) per
vertex, find a subgraph in which each vertex v has exactly f(v)
incident edges, or correctly certify that no such subgraph exists.
Same running time, same success probability, same single program.

Now: how do you actually get matching to run in almost-linear time?
The paper's strategy is an ecosystem of reductions and data
structures. The next several sections unpack that ecosystem.


6. Two critical reductions
==========================

The algorithm does not directly attack "maximum matching in general
graphs". It attacks a simpler-looking problem (perfect matching in a
graph of maximum degree three), and it does so via two reductions that
are important enough to describe carefully.

Reduction 1: from cardinality to perfect matching.

  Suppose you know how to decide, for any graph H, whether H has a
  perfect matching. Fix the original graph G, and fix a target k. How
  would you decide whether G has a matching of size k?

  The naive way: add n - 2k new vertices, each adjacent to every
  original vertex of G. Then a size-k matching of G becomes a perfect
  matching of the new graph, and vice versa. But the new graph can
  have n times (n - 2k) new edges, which is potentially n^2. That
  destroys any (n+m)-linear running time.

  The clever way, this paper's approach: use a PERMUTATION NETWORK.
  A permutation network is an arrangement of "switches" (small
  routing subcircuits) that can route any assignment of inputs to
  outputs along vertex-disjoint paths. The famous construction is
  Benes' (1964). It has O(n log n) switches, and it can route any
  permutation in O(log n) "layers".

  Replace the "each-of-n-original-to-each-of-n-2k-new-vertices"
  complete connection by a Benes network with n inputs and n outputs.
  Each original vertex is wired to one network input; the target
  vertices get the network outputs. A perfect matching in the
  resulting graph forces exactly n-2k original vertices to take a
  network path, and any choice of n-2k original vertices can be
  routed through the network.

  The resulting graph has O(n log n) vertices and O(n log n) edges.
  That is linearithmic, not quadratic. Good.

Reduction 2: from arbitrary degree to degree three.

  Suppose G has a vertex v of degree d. Replace v by a "path gadget":
  a chain of 2d - 1 new vertices, with v's d edges attached to the
  odd positions of the chain. There are d odd positions and d-1 even
  positions. Every even position of the chain must be matched to one
  of its two adjacent odd positions; this forces all but one of the
  odd positions to be matched internally, leaving exactly one odd
  position available for an external edge.

  So each gadget "selects" exactly one of the d original edges, in
  any perfect matching of the expanded graph. The expanded graph has
  d odd positions and d-1 even positions per gadget, for a total of
  O(sum d_v) = O(m) new vertices. Each new vertex has degree at most
  3. And crucially, the transformation is EXACT and REVERSIBLE: a
  perfect matching of the new graph gives back exactly one matching of
  G, and vice versa.

Between the two reductions, we get: for any k, there is a subcubic
graph H_k of size O(n log n) + O(m) that has a perfect matching if and
only if G has a matching of size k. The size is O(n log n + m) -- not
quadratic -- which is essential.

Since G's maximum matching size is between 0 and n/2, the algorithm
does a binary search on k. Each test calls the subcubic perfect
matching procedure on H_k. That is the whole reduction. Log-many
tests, and each test is on a subcubic graph of linearithmic size.


7. Subcubic perfect matching: the modern view
=============================================

So the problem becomes: perfect matching in a graph of maximum degree
three. Even here, the best bound before this paper is O(m sqrt(n)).
The reason is embarrassingly simple: an augmenting path can have length
up to n, and Edmonds' search for it can cost n per augmentation. To
beat O(m sqrt(n)) you need to avoid expanding long augmenting paths at
every step.

The new paper's strategy is to not expand long augmenting paths. It
uses a different, non-explicit, representation of the current matching
state, in which an augmenting path of length n only costs O(log n) to
represent and O(log n) to reason about.

Think of it as the difference between two ways of storing the seating
chart at a dinner party.

  Explicit representation. Keep a list of every pair, and an "open"
    list of everyone not yet seated. When a new person arrives, find
    someone to pair with them by scanning the open list. Cost is
    proportional to how many pairs you may have to rearrange.

  Compressed representation. Keep a summary of "here are the groups
    of people who are happy with any of several different seating
    arrangements", plus some count of how many spare seats each group
    has. When a new person arrives, look at the summary, adjust the
    arrangement, and only expand the summary into an actual seating
    chart when you need to output the final answer.

The compressed representation is what makes the new algorithm fast.
It is much harder to maintain than the explicit one, but its storage
cost does not grow with the number of augmenting paths.

Formally, this is described in the paper as a "matching state" that
contains "bins", "rows", and "ledgers". The next section explains what
each of those is.


8. The bin-and-row state
========================

The representation is a partition of the current matching into a
"compressed" part and an "explicit" part. The compressed part contains
the pieces that will be expanded only at the very end.

A BIN is an odd-sized set of vertices. It is factor-critical:
no matter which one of its vertices you nominate as the "exposed" one,
there is a matching that covers all the others internally. A bin of
weight w_d = (|d| + 1)/2 means it has 2 w_d - 1 vertices.

  Example. A single vertex is a bin of weight 1. A triangle is a bin
  of weight 2 (its 3 vertices, one exposed, two matched by the
  remaining edge). A 5-cycle is a bin of weight 3.

A ROW is a single vertex outside all the bins. Each row is assigned
to a distinct bin, along one of the graph edges between them. When a
bin is "assigned", its exposed vertex is matched to its row. When a
bin is "free" (no row assigned to it), its exposed vertex is
unmatched.

The rest of the vertices form a set U of explicitly-matched pairs.
They are the "fat" part of the state: matched pairs whose interior
detail is available if we need it, and that we don't bother compressing.

The state also comes with three useful counts.

  |M| is the number of pairs in the current matching.
  K = n/2 - |M| is the number of augmentations we still need.
  Ledgers are the "budgets" that let us reason about who can
  borrow from whom.


9. The three actions: gain, expand, price
=========================================

The phase algorithm proceeds by taking actions. Each action changes
the state slightly, and the state is always maintained to be a true
matching. The three actions are:

  GAIN. Match a free bin to a free bin joined by a physical edge.
    When you find an edge between two free bins, you can rearrange
    both bins' internal matchings to expose exactly the endpoints of
    that edge, then match along the edge. This increases |M| by one.
    It is the whole point of the algorithm: gains are progress.

  EXPAND. When a free bin has an edge to a matched vertex inside U,
    break that matched pair into (a new row) + (a new singleton bin),
    and use the edge to make the singleton bin that bin's assigned
    row. Then the vertex in U and the free bin are no longer touching;
    they have been "absorbed" into the compressed state. This does
    not increase |M|, but it exposes more of the graph to inspection.

  PRICE. If there is no free-bin edge and no free-bin-to-U edge, find
    a "priced direction" -- a small circulation that increases a
    potential function. The pricing step uses a data structure (the
    "engine") that we'll meet in Section 14.

The algorithm insists on taking gains as often as possible. Between
gains, it takes expansions and pricing steps. The claim is:
  - If the graph HAS a perfect matching and we haven't reached it
    yet, then one of these actions always applies.
  - The number of expansions and pricing steps between two gains is
    controlled by a ledger-based accounting argument.

The next two sections set up the ledgers.


PART III. LEDGERS, DEBT, AND PRICING


10. The ledger
==============

The most distinctive idea in the paper is the ledger. Let's motivate
it before we define it.

You are maintaining a matching in a compressed form. At any moment,
some bins are free (contributing one unmatched vertex each) and some
are assigned to rows (whose exposed vertices are matched to the rows).

A natural question to ask: after some small change, can I still match
everything? The answer depends on the bipartite "row-to-bin"
incidence graph: each row r is adjacent to whichever bins it has
physical edges to. The classical Hall condition (imported to this
bipartite setting) says the current assignment can be rearranged to
any other injective assignment if and only if for every set X of
bins, the number of rows whose neighbors are all in X is at most |X|.

So we want a surplus: |X| - (number of captive rows of X). This
surplus is called h(X), the "deficiency of X". We need h(X) >= 1 for
all nonempty X, at every "settled" state.

Actually we need something stronger. We need to be able to not only
maintain the current assignment, but to handle a whole extended
sequence of changes without doing a global scan for the Hall
condition each time. The BUDGET for how much slack we have is what
the ledger measures.

A LEDGER is like a bank account per bin. Each row r "puts" some
amount of budget, L units in the current parameterization, into its
incidence graph. The budget is spread across the bins that r has
edges to. Each bin d has a "load" from all rows allocating to it, and
its "residual" is L minus that load. Even bins with small or zero
load have a minimum: that minimum is c * w_d for some "floor
parameter" c.

There are a + 1 ledgers simultaneously, with different floors:

  Ledger 0 (top):    floor C * w_d = (a+1) * w_d, and slack; used
                     for the SMOOTH PROGRESS OBJECTIVE.
  Ledger 1:          floor a * w_d
  Ledger 2:          floor (a-1) * w_d
  ...
  Ledger a (bottom): floor 1 * w_d, no debt allowed at settled
                     states; used for the HALL CERTIFICATE.

The gap between adjacent floors is one unit. That gap is the
"operational margin" the algorithm uses to amortize its work: when a
repair has to move budget, the adjacent ledgers permit moving one
unit, and the total movement across the whole family is bounded.

The ledger with the smallest floor is the "bottom" ledger. Its
debt-free state is what gives the Hall certificate:

  BOTTOM SURPLUS. At a settled state, for every nonempty set X of
  bins, L * h(X) >= w(X). In particular h(X) >= 1 for all non-empty X.

So if you can maintain the bottom ledger's bounds without going into
debt, you have proved that at least one augmenting path exists. That
is the correctness guarantee for the phase algorithm.


11. Debt
========

In practice you cannot always maintain the ledger exactly: some
change forces you to overspend in the short run. The ledger allows
this as "debt": a debt p^j_d >= 0 at ledger j bin d means that the
ledger is effectively short by p^j_d at bin d.

The ledger invariant is: y^j_d + p^j_d >= c_j * w_d, where y^j_d is
the residual at bin d in ledger j and c_j is the ledger's floor
parameter. So debt is "shorted capacity", and it accumulates:

  - EXPAND adds debt c_j at the newly installed assigned bin.
  - GAIN (with ledger repair) can add at most 2L of net debt per gain.
  - CONTRACTION preserves total debt (or reduces it).

Debt is a temporary bad thing: at every settled state, the bottom
ledger's total debt is zero. The bottom-most period is short enough
that the debt never accumulates on it, and the coarser ledgers
"carry" the debt while the finer ones take over the Hall proof.

The trick is: because the bottom floor is c_a = 1 and the next floor up
is c_{a-1} = 2, a projection peel that removes weight w(R) from the
bottom decreases the next-coarse ledger's debt by AT LEAST w(R). This
is the "payment" argument: every peel costs weight, and weight is
what finances future peels.


12. Projections: the local repair problem
=========================================

Between the current state and the next settled state, we may have
accumulated debt. To return to a settled (debt-free on bottom)
state, we run a PROJECTION at some ledger level.

A projection is a flow problem. On the row-bin graph we set up a
network with

  - source -> deficit bin d, capacity (c w_d - y_d)_+ (how far d is
    below its floor),
  - surplus bin d -> sink, capacity (y_d - c w_d)_+ (how far d is
    above its floor),
  - for each incidence e=(r,d), bin -> row with capacity x_e (current
    allocation) and row -> bin with capacity L+1.

A MAXIMUM FLOW of value beta = total deficit "cures" all deficits
by rerouting allocations. The new allocation has all residuals at
least their floor. Done.

But if the maximum flow is LESS than beta, some deficits remain. Then
the algorithm identifies all residual-reachable bins from the source,
which form a set R, and PEELS that set. Peeling means: unfreeze the
assigned rows of R, remove their bins from the compressed state, and
turn their free exposed vertices into unmatched vertices (they go
onto the "blocker list"). This trades compressed structure for
concreteness at those specific bins.

The key lemma is that if R is nonempty it makes PROGRESS. Its weight
w(R) is at least as large as the coarse-ledger debt it retires. So
peels are always paid for.

The clever part is that this projection network is HUGE: O(n) arcs,
O(n) vertices. Doing it globally would defeat the whole point. So the
algorithm does it locally: it starts from the "seeds" that are close
to deficit, and only "explores" bins that become reachable through
positive allocations. Every expanded bin PERMANENTLY LOCKS IN some of
its own capacity that it consumed from the flow. This bounds the
region size by O(P) where P is the total debt, not by O(n).

  Cost of one local projection test: (P + B) * S^{O(1/a) + o(1)}.
  Where P is the total debt (O_a(S) for j > 1), B is a "padding" term,
  and S is the master size bound.

That is the heart of the algorithm's local work bound.

The local projection test uses the PRICED STEP primitive that we're
about to define. Each threshold test runs on a small local network,
calls the engine O(log S) times, and returns either "yes the flow is
big enough" (in which case it locked in enough to move the state
forward) or "no the flow is not big enough" (in which case the state
is peeled). The exact result is recovered by one more static flow
computation on the small local network, of size O(P).

This is where the mysterious parameter a comes in: a bigger a means
more ledger levels, more amortization, and more slack, at the cost of
more bookkeeping per step. The algorithm's running time has an
"exponent = O(1/a)" term, meaning larger a gives a nicer exponent. The
paper picks a >= 1000 to make the exponents comfortably small, but
each specific a is a FIXED choice. Different a values give different
algorithms; the uniformization step at the end combines them all.


13. Progress: why the algorithm always makes some
=================================================

The crucial correctness lemma is: if the graph has a perfect matching
and the algorithm is not done, then one of the three main actions
applies.

If a free-free edge exists, GAIN applies. If a free-bin-to-U edge
exists (with enough slack), EXPAND applies. Otherwise: no such edge
exists, and the algorithm should be able to find a PRICED DIRECTION
that increases the potential.

This is the "no false stall" lemma. Its proof (from the paper, with
annotations for the reader) goes as follows:

  Realize the matching conceptually (mentally, not algorithmically).
  Take the symmetric difference with a perfect matching. This
  decomposes into K-i* vertex-disjoint "augmenting chains".

  Because no free-free edge and no free-bin-to-U edge exists, each
  chain from a free bin must eventually exit through a specific
  structure. The paper shows that these chains let you simultaneously
  free a set of bins Z of size at least K/2 (where K is the deficit
  at the phase start). Rearranging the ledger reallocations so that
  these newly-free bins each receive L units, while pushing other
  columns to their capacities, produces an ALTERNATIVE allocation x'
  that differs from the current x by a CIRCULATION.

  This circulation has more positive derivative of the log objective
  than negative, by a comfortable margin (at least 3x). That
  circulation is the "priced direction" the engine looks for.

  So the engine cannot return LOW, unless it reports stochastic
  failure. Otherwise it returns a direction and takes a step, which
  changes state.

The pricing step ALSO cannot happen infinitely many times without a
structural action, because each successful step increases the
objective by at least Omega(q^2), and there is only so much objective
in the system.

So: as long as there is no false stochastic failure and no
suspension, the algorithm always makes progress.


14. Pricing, and the engine
===========================

Pricing is what the algorithm does when nothing else works. We have a
current allocation x; we want to nudge it in a direction that improves
the top-level logarithmic objective Phi = sum_e log x_e + sum_d log s_d.

The engine (the "priced step primitive") is the operational part.
Given the current point and a small positive budget q, the engine
either:

  - Returns LOW (meaning: no acceptable direction exists). It can
    only do this if no circulation direction d satisfies
    (g . d) / ||lambda * d||_1 > 1/2, where g is the gradient and
    lambda is the sum of absolute individual derivatives. And even
    then, it must not return LOW if stochastic failure happens; that
    is reported separately.

  - Returns a circulation increment h on the exact epsilon-grid, with
    ||lambda * h||_1 <= q/100 (small absolute motion) and g . h >=
    q^2/1000 (real objective gain). This increases Phi by Omega(q^2).

The engine also exposes CACHED REGISTERS f_hat_e ~ f_e, accurate
enough that lambda_e(f_hat_e) * |f_e - f_hat_e| <= q/100. The cache
is refreshed whenever a coordinate has drifted too far, and the total
number of refreshes is O(number of successful steps) * polylog.

That last piece is important. The naive approach -- recompute the
exact f_e for every coordinate before every pricing step -- would cost
O(n) per step, defeating the whole purpose. The engine instead keeps a
CACHE of f values and refreshes lazily. "Refreshes lazily" is the
technical way of saying: it keeps track, using a separate "absolute
motion" accumulator, of how much a coordinate has actually changed
since it was last cached. When that absolute motion exceeds a
threshold, the coordinate is refreshed. Because every pricing step
only moves each coordinate a small total amount, the total absolute
motion is bounded by the number of steps times polylog.

That is what makes the whole pipeline work.

There is one more subtlety that matters for correctness: the pricing
step is done on a "priced" network whose PRICES are derived from the
cached values. The engine must ensure that the prices are close enough
to the exact derivatives that the ratio test is still correct. The
analysis shows that the cached-value error, the rounding error, and
the pricing accuracy all fit within the allowed slack q, and that the
test "ratio >= 2q" exactly matches the "ratio >= 1/4" condition in
the algorithm.

We will not give the derivation here. The point to take away is:
pricing is correct, at a cost of polylogarithmic work per successful
step, and each successful step increases the objective by a fixed
polynomial in the accuracy parameter q. Since the total objective
range is O(S log S), at most O(S q^{-2} log S) successful pricing
steps occur, and the whole pricing phase costs S^{1 + O(1/a) + o(1)}.

Next, the engine's real implementation needs the cycle backend. That
is the subject of Part IV.


PART IV. THE CYCLE BACKEND


15. Why the backend exists
==========================

Pricing needs a circulation. But finding circulations is not a trivial
operation, especially when the graph is changing (edges added, removed,
prices retargeted) and there are millions of edges.

The "cycle backend" is the data structure that answers this need. It
maintains a dynamic graph, and it answers queries of the form: "give
me a short circulation with a good gradient-to-length ratio". The
interface is precise:

  Input:
    - a dynamic undirected graph on at most M vertices and edges
    - positive integral lengths l_e on each edge
    - integral gradients gamma_e on each edge
    - a master size bound S and parameters a and sigma
    - M must satisfy S^{1/a} <= M <= S^2

  Operations:
    - insert an edge (with its length and gradient)
    - delete an edge (or replace with a new length/gradient; a price
      change is modeled as delete + insert)
    - query: return a circulation as a list of individual actual
      edges and forest paths

  Quality guarantee: let OPT = max over nonzero circulations d of
  (gamma . d) / ||l * d||_1. If OPT > 0, the query must return a
  circulation c with:
    - raw length V > 0 (raw length counts traversals with multiplicity)
    - (gamma . c) / V >= M^{-10/a} * OPT

  Time: initialization is M^{1 + O(1/a) + o_a(1)}. Each operation is
  amortized M^{O(1/a) + o_a(1)}.

  Reliability: stochastic failure detected with probability at most
  2^{-sigma} * S^{-60} per backend instance. It does not fail on any
  path, only reports failure.

A few things worth noticing.

  - The QUALITY factor is M^{-10/a}, not 1. The backend is an
    APPROXIMATE cycle finder. In the pricing step we saw that we
    only need OPT/4 or better, so as long as M^{-10/a} > some
    small constant we are fine. For large a and moderate M this
    is satisfied.

  - The RAW LENGTH counts every traversal with multiplicity. This is
    the "raw-motion" convention from Section 14. Without it, an
    adversarial query that mostly canceled out would appear short
    but would actually have consumed many steps.

  - The output is not just "an edge set". It is a
    specifically-chosen COMBINATION of actual edges and forest
    paths. The reason is that the engine's state depends on the
    exact sequence of edge traversals, not just the net circulation.

The backend is built recursively: at the outer level, we handle a
graph of size at most M. At an inner level i, we handle a graph of
size at most n_i = M^{1 - i/(2a)}. There are d = 2a - 4 levels. The
outer level has n_0 = M, the bottom has n_d = M^{2/a}.

Between levels, each parent edge "becomes" a forest path in the
parent (via a segment decomposition) plus a single child edge. The
child edge's coefficients are aggregated from the parent path. So a
circulation at the child level "expands" via those segments into a
circulation at the parent. Each level costs a factor of b^2 = M^{2/a^2}
in quality. Over d levels, total loss is M^{(4a - 8)/a^2}, which for
a >= 1000 is well below the M^{-10/a} bound stated in the interface.

The construction is in three parts. First, the ROUTING HELPER
(Section 16): given a graph and "resource demands" on it, find a
sparse subgraph that still meets the demands. Second, the FROZEN
DECOMPOSITION (Section 17): at each level, "freeze" the graph
structure at the start of a "phase", precompute some pre-built
forests (called "covers"), and then any query can be answered by
looking at a bounded number of those pre-built forks. Third, the
LIFTED PUBLIC FOREST (Section 18): the child graph's forest is lifted
back to a parent forest using "arm slots", so we can hand the parent
a circulation using the child's answer.


16. The routing helper
======================

The routing helper is a purely combinatorial object, used as a
building block.

  Input:
    - an undirected multigraph F with identified edge copies
    - for each edge f, a sparse set of "resource counts" m_xf in
      {1, ..., 10}, where x is a "resource label"
    - the constraint sum_f m_xf <= C_0 for every vertex and every
      resource x (this is a LOCAL sparsity condition: at each vertex,
      each resource is spread across at most C_0 edges)

  Output:
    - a subgraph F' subset F
    - for every edge f=vw in F, a walk Q_f in F' from v to w

  Guarantees:
    - |F'| is small: at most P_* * v_+, where v_+ is the number of
      distinct vertex labels and P_* is a fixed-parameter logarithmic
      factor
    - |Q_f| is short: at most P_*
    - resource preservation: for every resource x,
        sum_f sum_{e in Q_f} m_xe  <=  P_* * C_0
      That is, even after all the routing, every resource is only
      used P_* times more than the local pre-routing bound.

  Runtime: O((input size + 1) * b * M^{o_a(1)}) where b = M^{1/a^2}.
  Failure probability: 2^{-sigma} S^{-800}.

The resource preservation is the crucial innovation. Without it, the
routing helper would be just a typical sparse routing sparsifier. The
key lemma is that ADDITIONAL resource usage is proportional to the
pre-routing LOCAL usage (at each vertex). So a vertex that appears in
few edges' routes stays "cheap" after sparsification.

The construction has three rounds.

ROUND 1: SAMPLING CUTS.

  Sample edges with probability inversely proportional to vertex
  degrees. Retained edge e gets weight 1/p_e. Compute a maximum flow
  between two equal halves of the vertex "stubs". The max flow's
  deficiency (matched pairs short of a perfect bipartite matching)
  identifies a sparse cut, if one exists. If so, split the piece into
  two, defer the crossing edges to later stages, and recurse on the
  halves.

  The random sampling ensures accuracy: with overwhelming probability,
  the sampled cut weights are close (multiplicatively) to the real
  ones. This is a "matrix Bernstein inequality" result: the sampled
  cut-weights form a sum of independent matrix-valued random
  variables, and the concentration is exponential.

ROUND 2: MATCHED OVERLAY.

  If a piece survives r_m rounds without a further cut, it must be
  "expanding" (conductance at least 1/(4 r_m)). The algorithm now
  uses the max-flow matchings it computed in each round to build a
  "regular overlay graph" on the stubs. The matching edges have short
  real embedding lengths and the overlay has bounded degree. This is
  the "median rule": the overlay's edges are chosen by the median of
  some random projections.

ROUND 3: RECURSIVE ROUTING.

  Now we need to route the actual DEMAND STUB PAIRS (each demand edge
  wants to be routed as a path connecting its two stubs) through the
  overlay. Do this recursively:
    - Group virtual labels (stubs) into "buckets" of size ~b
    - Route each demand through its bucketed graph (with bounded hop
      length by conditioning)
    - Recursively route on the bucketed graphs
    - Substitute back to get short paths in the overlay

  Crucially, each packet uses O(t * log(1/eps)) hops per level, and
  there are O(log_b(M)) levels. Total hop count: polylog. After
  substitution, the real path uses only a polylog factor more length
  than the overlay path. Every packet's path is verified before
  accepting.

The resource-preservation check is a separate step at the end: after
substituting real edges for overlay edges, we count actual traversals
on each resource. If any exceeds the allowed bound, the attempt is
rejected (retried with fresh randomness). If it succeeds, the sparse
support and short paths are guaranteed.

This is heavily inspired by the "cut-matching" framework of
Khandekar-Rao-Vazirani (2006) and by the hierarchical routing of
Ghaffari-Kuhn-Su (2017). The specific contribution of this paper is
the explicit resource-preservation accounting, needed because the
backend's downstream utilization is "resource-aware".


17. Freezing and decomposing the graph
======================================

The backend maintains its graph in epochs. An epoch is a fixed number
of input edits. At the start of an epoch, the current (nonloop) graph
is FROZEN: its structure and prices are fixed, and it serves as the
"reference" graph for the rest of the epoch. New edges inserted
during the epoch are tracked separately.

The algorithm precomputes a family of FORESTS on the frozen graph,
called COVERS. A cover F_j at layer j (j = 1, ..., r) is a set of
K_h partitions of the frozen vertices into cells. Each cell has a
shortest-path tree of radius at most Gamma * R_j (where R_j = b^j and
b = M^{1/a^2}). The key coverage property: for every vertex v, SOME
partition has a cell containing its entire closed R_j-ball.

  How the covers are built: independent random "delays" X_c from a
  geometric distribution (Pr[X_c >= t] = 2^{-t}), with each center
  scoring R_j * X_c - dist(c, v). A vertex v picks the center with
  maximum score. The runner-up gap being > 2 R_j means the coverage
  holds. Random shifts (Miller-Peng-Xu) and verified padding
  guarantee this with probability > 1/2 per attempt; retry up to
  2 (sigma + 1000 log S + 10) times.

Now, the DECOMPOSITION LEMMA (which is the workhorse):

  FIX any epoch state and any current circulation Delta. Write Delta
  = Delta_s + Delta_n (surviving frozen part + new-edge part).

  Then there exist coefficients alpha_f on live "fundamental cycles"
  C_f^0 (one per surviving frozen chord) and a forest vector z on the
  "raw forests" H_j such that:

    sum_f alpha_f * pi(C_f^0)  =  Delta_s + pi(z)         (identity)
    sum_f |alpha_f| * w_f      <= A_* * ||l * Delta_s||_1  (coefficient bound)
    ||z||_length               <= A_* * ||l * Delta_s||_1  (residual bound)

  where pi(C) is the "projection" of the cycle to the actual edges.

The proof: decompose each surviving edge's "two-arm" walk (one arm to
a common representative, then back) into fundamental cycles of the
frozen forest. The "common representative" is the highest layer at
which the two arm choices agree; below that, the arms cancel; above
that, they don't (and the local "chord" structure covers them).

The RESIDUAL z is the leftover forest vector that isn't quite a
circulation yet. It has divergence only at "new edge endpoints" and
"hole endpoints" (where the frozen forest's edges were cut).

Then the algorithm COMPRESSES z into a smaller graph (the child graph
J) by:
  - taking the Steiner subtree of the terminal set
  - suppressing degree-2 nonterminals
  - assigning summed length and gradient to each compressed segment

The compression is exact for the length norm. So a child circulation
of length ||l^J * Xi||_1 expands to a parent circulation with length
at most that (times the arm-slot lifting factor, which is 1).


18. The lifted public forest
============================

The child graph has its own forest structure. When the child returns
a circulation as a forest path (or a piece of one), we need to expand
it back to the parent. If the child path is short, the parent path is
short. If the child path is long, we need to make sure it isn't
suddenly expensive to expand.

The solution: PRE-BUILT ARM SLOTS. Every child vertex q has, in its
corresponding pool of "occurrences" at the parent, a set of ARM SLOTS.
Each arm slot has the shape of a path (the compressed segment of the
parent that q corresponds to), oriented outward from q's image in the
parent. Up to D_{i+1} = b^{2(d-i-1)} copies of this path are
pre-built, one per child-forest occurrence.

When the child's forest is used to answer a query, we need to lift
each child forest path. We choose a fixed endpoint of the corresponding
parent segment (say q), grab its arm slot in the correct occurrence,
and connect the far tip of that arm to the OTHER endpoint's center
(via a null connector). This is a "lift" of one child edge.

The problem: when the child's forest structure CHANGES (because a new
query created a new child-forest edge, or a deletion cut one off), the
arm slots need to be reassigned. But reassignment could break the
engine's accumulated values.

The solution: PERSISTENT EDGE LIFETIMES. Each arm edge has a unique
identifier that reflects its physical endpoint, pool index, and
segment position -- NOT which specific occurrence uses it. When an arm
slot is reassigned to a different occurrence, its internal edges keep
their identities. Their accumulations (signed motion h_c and absolute
motion A_c from Section 14) are transferred intact. So the engine's
cached values remain correct.

The critical claim: LIFTING PRESERVES THE SCORE. If a child
circulation has score (gamma . c) / ||l^J * c||_1 = rho, then after
lifting, the parent circulation has:
  gamma . c_lifted = gamma . c         (same gradient, propagated)
  ||l * c_lifted||_1 <= ||l^J * c||_1  (no larger raw length, because
                                          each child length is a sum of
                                          parent constituent lengths)
  score >= rho                         (immediate)

So the parent's problem is at least as good as the child's problem.
And the child's problem is at least b^2 smaller. That is the
recursion's quality guarantee.

Two more properties of the lift are worth stating because they allow
the engine's per-coordinate accuracy tracking:

  - STABILITY: a forest path that existed before the lift continues
    to exist after (with the same identifier and same endpoints). The
    engine can reuse its cached value without a re-fetch.
  - INVARIANT SUPPORT: reassignment of dormant arm slots does not
    change any active identifier. The engine's "current edge set"
    only changes when new edges are explicitly inserted.

This is what makes the whole thing work at the engine level. Without
the persistent lifetimes, the cached values would drift on every
forest edit, and the engine would have to fall back to O(S) rebuilds
per operation. With them, the engine's cost per step stays
polylogarithmic.

SECTION: what we skipped

The BACKEND-UPDATES section of the original paper is the one that
maintains the child graph dynamically: how many edits happen per
parent-edit, how many child slots are consumed, how to reuse slots,
how to bound the number of new edges selected as "central" chords
at each rebuild. It is the implementation-heavy core of the argument.
Some of its parameters:

  - Each epoch has h_i = n_i / k edits, where k = M^{1/a}.
  - Inner snapshots at periods s_j = b^{z_0 - j}, j = 0, ..., z_0
    where z_0 = ceil(log_b h_i).
  - Each inner snapshot has path-hop bound p(j) = (10 P_*)^{j+1} and
    visit bound C(j) = D_l * (100 P_*)^{j+1}.
  - At a rebuild, the routing helper is called on a core graph with
    O(E_0) incident labels (where E_0 = number of logical edits
    since the last snapshot). It returns support of size O(P_* E_0).
  - Total additional support per epoch prefix of t' edits is at most
    b t' times a fixed log factor.

The upshot: each parent edit triggers at most b = M^{1/a^2} child
edits, and the recursion depth is O(a). So the total recursive work
is dominated by the top level, with the sub-linear exponents adding
up to O(1/a).

This is where the "almost linear" in the title lives: the algorithm
has a cost of
    S^{1 + O(1/a) + o(1)}
where the O(1/a) exponent is what we're paying for the recursive
structure. Choosing a larger a gives a smaller exponent. The
parameter a is fixed per-branch in the uniformization step (Section
19), and larger branches have asymptotically better bounds.


PART V. WRAPPING UP


19. One uniform program
=======================

Every algorithm so far has two parameters: a and sigma. Different
choices give different algorithms.
  - Larger a gives a better time bound (exponent O(1/a), tending to
    zero as a tends to infinity). But the constants get worse.
  - Larger sigma gives better success probability (>= 1 - 2^{-sigma}),
    at the cost of more random bits and more retries.

The main theorem claims a SINGLE algorithm, with FIXED constants and
a success probability of at least 2/3. How do you get one algorithm
out of a family?

Answer: universal search, or "dovetailing" (Levin, 1973).

  Set up a schedule of rounds j = 0, 1, 2, ... In round j, give
  branch t (t = 0, ..., j) a budget of 2^{j-t} elementary steps.
  A branch that finishes halts the whole schedule; a branch that
  doesn't finish is paused, with its state saved, and resumes at
  its next scheduled time.

  Branch 0 uses Edmonds' deterministic O(n^3) algorithm. It is slow
  but guaranteed to finish with a correct answer. So the schedule
  ALWAYS halts within polynomial time.

  Branch t (t >= 1) is the paper's algorithm with a = 1000 + t and
  sigma = t + 20. It costs p^{1 + O(1/a) + o(1)} on any n+m = p
  input, with success probability 1 - 2^{-(t+20)}.

Now the interesting part: what is the running time of the schedule?

  Fix a target size p. Some branch t is "the first to finish"; call
  its actual step count T_t(p). The schedule's wall-clock time is at
  most sum_{j=0}^{ceil(log_2 T_t(p)) + t} 2^{j - t'}, where the sum
  is over all branches t' = 0, 1, 2, ..., and this sums to at most
  2^{t + 2} * T_t(p).

  So the schedule's actual cost is at most 4 * 2^t * T_t(p) for the
  branch that finishes first.

  For each fixed t, branch t's upper bound is p^{1 + O(1/(1000+t))
  + o(1)}. So the schedule's cost is
  at most min over t of { 4 * 2^t * p^{1 + O(1/(1000+t)) + o(1)} }.

  Take t = O(log log p). Then 2^t = (log p)^O(1) = p^{o(1)}, and
  1/(1000+t) = O(1/log log p) = o(1). So the total cost is
  p^{1 + o(1)}.

Formally, the paper does not assert "p^{1 + o(1)}". It asserts
"C * p^{1 + eta(p)}" for some nonnegative, nonincreasing function
eta that tends to 0. The function eta is DEFINED by the schedule:

  eta(p) = sup over all inputs and paths of size p of
           max(0, log_p(T(that path) / p) - 1).

This supremum is finite: the polynomial fallback branch bounds it.
This supremum tends to zero: the fixed branches have bounds whose
rate tends to 1. And it is nonincreasing in p because the supremum
is over larger sets as p grows.

That is the construction. The schedule is what makes it ONE program.

  Is the schedule really one algorithm?

  Yes, with care. Every branch runs in the same fixed word-RAM
  model. Every branch's parameters are FIXED integers (a = 1000+t,
  sigma = t+20), not floating-point. The schedule uses a fixed
  round structure and a fixed doubling schedule. All the coroutine
  state is integer state. So the schedule is one finite program,
  and the word size c_0 is a single fixed constant.

  The one subtlety is physical word size. The schedule's intermediate
  sizes are bounded by the polynomial fallback branch; c_0 is chosen
  once at compile time to cover that bound. It does NOT depend on
  the input.

Random bits: each branch has its own independent fair-bit stream,
revealed on-demand. Coupling all branches to independent streams,
and taking a union bound on their failure probabilities, gives
    sum over t >= 1 of 2^{-(t+20)} = 2^{-19} < 1/3.
So the final answer is correct with probability at least 2/3.


20. Prescribed-degree factors (f-factors)
=========================================

Let us finish with the "second" problem the paper solves. Given a
graph G and an integer f(v) for each vertex v -- with
0 <= f(v) <= deg(v) -- an F-FACTOR is a subgraph in which each vertex
v has exactly f(v) incident edges. So a perfect matching is the
special case f(v) = 1 for all v, and a 1-factor is
"everyone gets paired exactly once."

The classical reduction (Tutte 1954) is: build an auxiliary graph H_f,
and find a perfect matching in H_f. The naive version of H_f would
have quadratic size. This paper's version is linearithmic.

Here is the construction.

  For each host edge e = {u, v}, create two PORT vertices x_{u,e}
  and x_{v,e}, joined by a CROSS EDGE labeled e. (Each host vertex
  u gets one port for each incident edge.)

  For each host vertex v with degree d_v and target f(v), create a
  permutation network of size s_v = smallest power of two >= d_v.
  The network has d_v inputs and s_v outputs. Assign distinct inputs
  to the d_v ports at v.

  Let b_v = d_v - f(v). Select b_v outputs of v's network. Add a
  SINK vertex adjacent only to the output endpoint of each selected
  output.

  Split every network position a into two vertices a_in, a_out,
  joined by an IDLE EDGE. For every directed arc a -> a' in the
  network, add a TRANSITION EDGE from a_out to a'_in.

The whole graph H_f is simple (no self-loops, no multi-edges). Its
size is O(m log(n+2)).

  Claim: H_f has a perfect matching if and only if G has an
  f-factor.

  Proof sketch (=>): take a perfect matching of H_f. At every split
  position, either its idle edge is used (so the position is
  "internally covered") or both endpoints are matched by non-idle
  edges. So the number of "externally matched" inputs equals the
  number of "externally matched" outputs in every network. Since
  selected transition edges and network-internal edges balance, the
  imbalance comes only from port attachments (which contribute one
  input) and sink attachments (which contribute one output). All b_v
  sinks are covered, so exactly b_v ports use sink attachments, and
  exactly d_v - b_v = f(v) ports use cross edges. Every host edge's
  two ports either both use the cross edge (so the host edge is in
  the f-factor) or neither does (host edge excluded). So the f-factor
  consists of the labels of the cross edges used.

  Proof sketch (<=): given an f-factor F, at each vertex v, exactly
  b_v port attachments are outside F. Route these b_v input ports to
  the b_v selected outputs, and extend this to a permutation of all
  s_v inputs and outputs. The permutation network realizes this as
  vertex-disjoint paths. Select these paths, plus the appropriate
  cross edges, idle edges, and sinks. It is a perfect matching of
  H_f.

Now apply the almost-linear matching algorithm to H_f. Its size is
O(m log(n+2)) = O(p log p). Its word length fits in O(log(n+2)) bits.
So the corollary is: prescribed-degree factors in p^{1 + o(1)} time,
single program, probability >= 2/3. Same as matching itself.

Notice what the corollary buys us. The paper's main theorem is about
matching; the corollary shows the technique extends to a much larger
class of problems. Prescribed-degree factors have their own classical
Tutte reduction, but the naive version is quadratic. The permutation
network is what makes it linearithmic. That is a nice piece of
algorithm engineering that is somewhat orthogonal to the main result.


21. What the paper actually proves
==================================

Let us step back and say what the paper establishes, in one place.

  THEOREM (main). There is one uniform randomized word-RAM
  algorithm, with fixed constants c_0 >= 2 and C >= 1 and a
  nonincreasing function eta(p) -> 0, such that for every graph G on
  n vertices, m edges, p = n + m, the algorithm halts in at most
  C * p^{1 + eta(p)} steps on every computation path, and with
  probability at least 2/3 outputs a maximum matching of G.

  For sparse graphs (m = O(n)), the bound is n^{1 + o(1)}. For dense
  graphs (m = Theta(n^2)), the bound is n^{2 + o(1)}.

  COROLLARY (f-factors). Same time and probability guarantees for the
  problem of deciding whether G has an f-factor, and of returning one
  when it does.

  Special cases of prior work (all subsumed in this paper's results):
    - Bipartite matching: the earlier (2022) almost-linear
      algorithms of Chen et al. and van den Brand et al.
    - Algebraic matching: the O(n^omega) algorithms of Mucha-Sankowski,
      Harvey, and others.
    - Micali-Vazirani's O(m sqrt(n)) bound.

Some honest notes.

  - THE PROOF IS LONG. The paper's formal argument is dense. The
    matrix concentration inequalities in the routing helper, the
    recursive edit accounting in the backend, and the adaptive
    probability analysis of the engine are all nontrivial. This guide
    explains what each layer does, but a reader who wants to verify
    every step will want to look at the original.

  - SOME PARTS ARE "FOR ANY FIXED a". The paper's constructions have
    parameters a and sigma. The exact-time bounds apply for
    FIXED a and sigma, and then the uniformization step aggregates
    over all such choices. This is standard but worth being explicit
    about: "for every fixed a, there is a program that..." is what
    the paper actually proves for most of its intermediate lemmas;
    the "single program" statement comes from dovetailing.

  - WHAT IS RIGOROUS. The classical results (Tutte, Edmonds,
    Micali-Vazirani, Hall, Benes) are all cited to their sources and
    used correctly. The paper builds on Chen et al.'s 2022
    almost-linear flow algorithms, and takes the static scheduling
    (the deterministic flow theorem of van den Brand et al. 2023) as
    a primitive. Given those inputs, the paper's own contributions
    are:
      * The bin/row matching state with the a+1 ledger levels.
      * The local projection algorithm with weight-locked boundary
        exploration, converting a global repair problem into a local
        O(P) flow problem.
      * The frozen decomposition with resource-preserving routing.
      * The persistent-arm-identity public forest, which keeps the
        engine's per-coordinate accumulations stable under forest
        edits.
      * The uniformization schedule that turns the parameterized
        family into a single program with a single envelope.
    Each of those is a nontrivial new contribution of this paper.

  - WHAT IS NOT PROVEN. The paper does not claim to be
    implementable. Its constants are astronomical: a >= 1000,
    S = O(p log p), M up to S^2, and so on. A real implementation
    would need to be re-parameterized. The PROOF is asymptotic; the
    implementation would be a separate engineering project.

  - WHY IT MATTERS. Maximum matching is a fundamental problem with
    applications in scheduling, allocation, biology, and complexity
    theory (its polynomial-time decidability is one of the original
    results in algorithmic graph theory). Matching is one of the
    very rare problems where the "right" data structure has resisted
    the natural O(p) bound for DECADES. If the present paper's
    arguments are correct, matching joins the very small group of
    fundamental problems (like spanning tree, testing connectivity,
    and matrix-rank-ish tasks) whose exact version admits an
    almost-linear algorithm. That would be a major step.

  - ONE CAUTION. The paper was written in 2026 and is very recent.
    Whether these arguments survive detailed scrutiny by the
    community is a good question to keep in mind. The ideas are
    old and correct at the top level (bin/row states, ledgers,
    circulations, dovetailing are all known techniques); the NEW
    assembly in this paper is what has to be examined carefully.





GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS

  Augmenting path. A path alternating between unmatched and matched
    edges that starts and ends at unmatched vertices. Flipping it
    increases the matching size by one.

  Bipartite graph. A graph that can be two-colored so every edge
    joins different colors. Matching is easier here.

  Blossom. An odd cycle (or odd-length alternating structure) that
    can be "contracted" during matching search.

  Bin. A factor-critical odd-sized vertex set in the compressed
    matching state.

  Cross edge. In the f-factor reduction, the edge connecting two
    adjacent ports.

  Debt. A temporary (paid-off-later) drop in a ledger's residual.

  Factor-critical. A set of vertices such that removing any one
    leaves a graph with a perfect matching (of the remainder).

  Fundamental cycle. The cycle formed by a chord plus the unique
    path between its endpoints in the frozen forest.

  Gain. An operation that increases |M| by 1.

  Gradient. In the pricing engine, the partial derivative of the
    logarithmic objective in the direction of each edge.

  Ledger. An allocation of L units per row to nearby bins. Used to
    measure and control slack.

  Low. The engine's "no good direction exists" response.

  Matching. A set of edges, no two sharing an endpoint.

  Odd component. A connected component of G - S having an odd number
    of vertices. Tutte's theorem is about these.

  Peel. A projection operation that removes a set of bins from the
    compressed state.

  Permutation network. A fixed arrangement of switches that can route
    any input-output permutation.

  Priced step. A small circulation that increases a potential
    function by at least a fixed amount.

  Projection. A local flow computation that either repairs the
    ledger exactly or reveals a peelable set of bins.

  Raw length. Total traversal count, with multiplicity.

  Resource preservation. The routing helper's guarantee about how
    often a physical vertex is used across all virtual routes.

  Row. A single vertex in the compressed state, assigned to a bin.

  Subcubic. A graph where every vertex has degree at most three.

  Uniformization. Making a family of algorithms (parameterized by a,
    sigma) into one algorithm via dovetailing.

  Unit. A single elementary step in the word-RAM model.

  Weight (of a bin). (|d| + 1) / 2, for bin d of |d| vertices.

  y^j_d, p^j_d. In ledger j at bin d: the residual and the debt.
