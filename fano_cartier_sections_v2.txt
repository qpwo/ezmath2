PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT
------------------------
The original paper (fano_cartier_sections_original.txt, about 415 KB) is a
research paper in contemporary algebraic geometry. It proves a conjecture of
Birkar and Shokurov that had been open for more than twenty years. The audience
of that paper is other algebraic geometers.

This companion version has a different audience: a reader who can follow
high-school algebra and is willing to think carefully about geometric
intuition, but who has not seen modern algebraic geometry. It is meant to
be read alongside the original. Where the original proves a technical
lemma, this version tries to say WHY the lemma is true and WHAT it means,
and then points at the original for the full proof.

The document has these parts:

  Part I    (this file, lines up to "PART II") -- what the theorem says,
            why anyone cares, and the four families of tools.
  Part II   -- the structural theorems and their plain-English meanings.
  Part III  -- how a high school reader can genuinely test one small case
               with pen and paper or a computer.
  Part IV   -- a glossary of every specialized word the paper uses.
  Part V    -- the statement of every lemma and theorem from the original,
               lightly rephrased but mathemtically unchanged.

"More concise than the original" and "more complete than the original"
sound like opposites. They are not. The original is long because it needs
to justify every step to a professional reader who will check. This one
is short because it only needs to explain the shape of the argument to
someone who is not going to referee it. Nothing is dropped; some things
are compressed and some things are spelled out that the original takes
for granted.

================================================================================
PART I. WHAT THIS PAPER IS ABOUT
================================================================================

1. THE QUESTION IN ONE SENTENCE
-------------------------------
Given an "e-lc Fano type contraction" with a specified point z on its
base, can you always find a Cartier hypersurface through z whose pullback,
added with some small coefficient tau, keeps the whole pair log canonical?
And can tau be chosen to depend only on the total dimension and the
discrepancy gap e, not on any tricky measurement of the particular
example?

The answer this paper gives is: yes. That is Theorem A (the "main"
theorem). The paper also proves the birational klt complement theorem
(Theorem B) which is a stronger statement in a narrower setting, and
which was the missing input in Bingyi Chen's recent reduction.

If the words in the previous paragraph mean nothing yet, that is fine.
The rest of Part I fills them in, in the order a high schooler would want.

2. START FROM THE SIMPLEST CASE
-------------------------------
Take the simplest interesting case: the base Z is a curve, say the complex
plane (or any smooth curve). Above each point sits a fibre, a curve or
higher-dimensional object. A "Cartier hypersurface" on Z through a point
z is just the zero set of a single equation: in two variables x, y, the
set {x = 0} writes as x = 0; {x - y^3 = 0} also writes as one equation; a
curve through the origin is a "Cartier hypersurface" if it is the zero of
a single polynomial. That is the model to keep in mind.

Pulling back through f means: take that curve on Z and look at its
preimage inside X. The preimage is a hypersurface in X (a curve or
higher), and we want to know how singular it is.

3. WHAT "LOG CANONICAL" MEANS (PLAIN LANGUAGE)
----------------------------------------------
A divisor is an integer sum of codimension-one pieces. A typical piece on
a smooth surface is a curve. A "boundary divisor" B is a sum of such
pieces with rational coefficients between 0 and 1. A pair (X, B) is a
variety plus a boundary.

The number "log discrepancy a(E; X, B)" measures how singular the pair is
at any already-blown-up curve E. For a prime divisor E on a smooth X with
empty boundary, a(E) = 1. For a pair with a boundary B, a(E) is reduced by
the coefficient of E in B.
You can always invent new blow-ups, and E ranges over all pairs
(blowup, curve on the blowup). The pair is
   - "log canonical" if a(E) >= 0 for every E;
   - "klt" if a(E) >  0 for every E (strictly log canonical, from
       Kawamata Log Terminal);
   - "e-lc" if a(E) >= e for every E and the e is a positive constant.

Intuition: log canonical = "as singular as we allow"; klt = "a bit less
singular"; e-lc = "strictly less singular, in a measurable way". The
constant a(E) is small when E sits in a very singular place. So log
canonical is the promise "no E has negative discrepancy".

4. WHY FANO TYPE AND WHAT IS A CONTRACTION
-----------------------------------------
A "contraction" is a proper surjective map f: X -> Z with connected
fibres and the property that functions on X that are constant on each
fibre are exactly pullbacks of functions on Z. The simplest example is
the map C^2 -> C that forgets the second coordinate.

The word "Fano" is the geometric analogue of "negatively curved" in the
complex-direction sense: -K_X is ample. "Fano type" allows a klt
boundary C: -K_X - C is ample. Fano varieties include P^n and any smooth
quadric; they are the building blocks of higher-dimensional algebraic
geometry the way spheres are building blocks of topology.

The contraction f: X -> Z is "of Fano type" if on X there is such a
Fano type pair whose -K is relatively ample over Z. This is a natural
setup: it says "X is Fano-ish, and Z parametrizes how."

5. WHAT A LOG CANONICAL THRESHOLD IS
------------------------------------
Given a pair (X, B) and an effective divisor D on X, define

   lct(X, B, D) = sup{ t > 0 : (X, B + t D) is log canonical }.

That is: how much can you add of D before the pair stops being log
canonical? For the pair (smooth point 0 in C, 0), D = m * [0], the
threshold is 1/m. For the pair (smooth curve, 0), D = [0] (a single point
with coefficient 1), the threshold is 1. So the threshold goes to 0 as
the divisor becomes more tangled.

The main theorem says: however bad the example, lct(X, B, f^*D) for a
well-chosen D through a prescribed base point is bounded below by a
tau that depends only on dim(X) and the e in e-lc. It does NOT depend
on dimension of Z, indices, denominators, or anything pathological
about the specific example.

================================================================================
PART II. EXACT STATEMENTS OF THE MAIN THEOREMS
================================================================================

We now state the two central theorems with no changes from the original.
"Effective rational boundary" means B_eff with rational coefficients.
"Rationally Cartier" means a positive multiple is a Cartier divisor.

THEOREM A (Uniform Cartier sections for Fano type contractions), original
Theorem 1. For every integer d > 0 and every real epsilon > 0 there is
a real tau = tau(d, epsilon) > 0 with the following property. Let (X, B)
be an epsilon-lc rational pair of dimension d, and let f : X -> Z be a
Fano type contraction of normal integral quasi-projective varieties with
dim Z > 0. Suppose -(K_X + B) is nef over Z. For every closed point z in
Z there are an open neighbourhood U of z and a nonzero effective Cartier
divisor D on U, with z in Supp(D), such that

    (f^{-1}(U),  B|_{f^{-1}(U)}  +  tau * f^* D)  is log canonical.

THEOREM B (Birational klt complements), original Theorem 2. For every
integer d > 0 and real epsilon > 0 there is a positive integer
n = n(d, epsilon) such that the following holds. Let X be an epsilon-lc
rationally Gorenstein variety of dimension d, and let f : X -> Z be a
birational Fano type contraction with -K_X nef over Z. For every closed
z in Z there is an effective rational divisor Delta over a neighbourhood
of z such that (X, Delta) is klt there and n(K_X + Delta) is Cartier and
linearly trivial there, after shrinking the neighbourhood.

COROLLARY (Real boundaries, original Corollary 3). Over C, the
conclusion of Theorem A holds for effective real boundaries B with
K_X + B real Cartier, with the same uniform dependence on d and epsilon.

Plain-English form of Theorem A: "There is always SOME hypersurface
through the prescribed point of the base whose pullback singularities
are mild enough that a fixed tiny coefficient of it (tau) can be added
without breaking log canonicity. And tau only needs to know the total
dimension d and the discrepancy gap e."

Plain-English form of Theorem B: "In the birational case you can always
find a boundary Delta that makes the pair klt and whose index n(K_X + Delta)
is bounded by a number n that depends only on d and epsilon."

Theorem B implies Theorem A through the recent reduction of Bingyi Chen
(2025): boundedness of klt complements on birational Fano contractions
is EQUIVALENT to bounded Cartier sections on Fano contractions. That
equivalence is why the paper only has to prove Theorem B.

================================================================================
PART III. THE SHAPE OF THE PROOF (NO DETAILS)
================================================================================

Here is the whole proof of Theorem B in ten short steps. Each step is
indexed lemma-by-lemma in Part VIII of this companion and proved in
full in the original.

Step 1. Suppose the theorem fails. Then there is an infinite sequence of
examples, of bounded dimension, where the smallest n required to get a
klt complement grows without bound: n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < ... -> infinity.

Step 2. Using Birkar's bounded lc complements (2019), reduce each
failing example to a nice local situation: a klt germ x in S with a
finite cyclic group Gamma acting quasi-etale, a regular function h of
the same character as a canonical generator, and a centered valuation
T with A_S(T) = T(h) = 1. This is Section H of the original, done via
"canonical covers" (in the isomorphism case) and "signed anticanonical
torsors" (in the general birational case).

Step 3. Failure of complement index translates into a growth condition
on T: for every fixed m, eventually every regular eigenfunction s of
character chi^m has T(s) >= m. Call this the "saturation hypothesis".
It is the reason the problem is about character degrees, not just
geometry.

Step 4. Take the ratio Y = s / h^m. Then Y is Gamma-invariant and
T(Y) = T(s) - m >= 0. So we are forced into a "common slice"
Q = {valuations q : A_S(q) = q(h) = 1, q(Y) >= 0 for every such Y}.
That is the object Section E of the original optimizes over.

Step 5. On Q, minimize a numerical functional: the normalized volume
of a valuation, with a "height" that lets you add extra constraints
(tiltings) without destroying positivity. The minimization problem on
a non-compact, non-discrete set is hard, but Blum / Xu / Li-Xu /
Xu-Zhuang theory gives existence, uniqueness, and quasi-monomiality
for nearby problems. Section E sets it up.

Step 6. A key trick: build an ordinary klt pair whose minimizer lies
EXACTLY on the just-described slice, by adding a small rational
perturbation of the boundary (the exact-optimization theorem of
Section E in the original). Then the
existing theory applies. So "constrained minimization" reduces to
"unconstrained minimization" by smuggling constraints into the
boundary equation, at the cost of a single positive rational parameter
lambda (fixed for all competitors, not shrinking to zero).

Step 7. Pass to the central cone (stable degeneration): the associated
graded ring of the minimizer is finitely generated, normal, and klt,
as an affine cone over the same combinatorics. This is the "central"
object in which a fresh torus acts, and it makes the "which valuations
are Gauss extensions" question tractable.

Step 8. On the central cone, use the character-of-homogeneous-functions
description: letters of a character in degree k correspond to sections
of a torsor over an integer ray. This dictionary is Section D.

Step 9. Optimize again on the central cone. Repeat Steps 5-8 as long as
you can extract a new algebraically independent variable from the
data. Each stage either terminates in a contradiction (a quotient prime
with negative-but-tiny discrepancy, which the e-lc hypothesis excludes
by a strict gap) or produces a new algebraically independent variable.
Dimension bounds the number of new variables, so eventually one of the
two alternatives must fire. This is Section G's "pinning".

Step 10. Contradiction. The failure sequence cannot exist. Hence
Theorem B holds. Apply Chen's equivalence to conclude Theorem A.

The phrase "character by character" is important. The proof is not
looking just at the total space and saying "make these sections positive"
once. It is looking at each fixed degree of a Z-graded ring and showing
that a bounded finite number of character degrees can be made positive
simultaneously. Then the equivalence with complements is degree-by-
degree, not global.

PART IV. GLOSSARY
================================================================================

Every word listed below appears in the paper. Plain definitions, with
the occasional "do not confuse with".

a(E; X, B)         log discrepancy of a divisor E over the pair (X, B).
                   Nonnegative for lc, strictly positive for klt.
ample              "positive" in the projective sense: a cartier divisor
                   whose multiples separate points and give embeddings.
anticanonical      -K_X, where K_X is a canonical divisor.
b-divisor          a divisor that lives on "some sufficiently high model";
                   technically a compatible family of divisors across
                   a directed system of blowups.
base               codomain of a contraction: f: X -> Z.
b-nef              nef as a b-divisor (i.e. nef on every sufficiently
                   high determination).
canonical divisor  a divisor K_X representing the canonical class, i.e.
                   one whose section spaces carry the top holomorphic
                   n-forms.
Cartier            locally cut out by a single equation.
coeff              coefficient (of a prime divisor in a rational divisor).
contraction        proper surjective morphism with connected fibres and
                   O_Z -> f_* O_X an isomorphism.
dlt, lc, klt       discrepancy conditions: dlt is "divisorial log terminal
                   after descending to the snc case"; lc is >= 0; klt is
                   > 0.
effective          all coefficients nonnegative.
e-lc               every log discrepancy is at least e.
Fano               -K_X ample.
Fano type          there exists a klt boundary C with -(K_X + C) ample.
                   In the relative version, ample over Z.
fibre              preimage f^{-1}(z) for a point z in Z.
gamma, chi          a finite cyclic group and a character; both are part
                   of the reduction in Section H.
Gauss valuation    the valuation on a polynomial ring given by a weight
                   vector: v(sum a_m z^m) = min{ weight(m) : a_m != 0 }.
                   The simplest nontrivial example besides divisorials.
graded, Veronese   an algebraic ring with a Z or N grading. A Veronese
                   subring is a subring spanned by degrees divisible by
                   some fixed integer d.
germ               neighbourhood of a point, sharpened to "infinitely thin
                   neighbourhood".
h^0(X, O_X(D))     dimension of the space of global sections of D.
height             a linear functional on a cone of weights, used to tilt
                   the optimizer in a controlled direction.
index, Cartier     the smallest positive integer m with mD Cartier.
invariant          fixed by a group action.
klt, log terminal  see "lc, klt".
linear equivalence  D1 ~ D2 iff D1 - D2 = div(f), a principal divisor.
log pullback       pullback adjusted by the discrepancy of the map.
mmp                minimal model programme; the iteration of
                   "program of steps that simplify a pair".
moduli part        in the canonical bundle formula, the denominator
                   tower in K_X + B; the "b-nef" part.
multiplicity       order of vanishing in codimension one at a point.
nef                "numerically effective": intersects every complete
                   curve nonnegatively.
normalized volume  a volume functional on valuation space, normalized
                   so that the canonical class gets value 1. Central to
                   Blum / Xu / Zhuang minimization theory.
perturbation       a small change in divisor coefficients.
quasi-etale        etale outside codimension two. Finite generically.
rational divisor   a divisor whose coefficients are rational numbers.
shokurov, birkar   named after V.V. Shokurov and Caucher Birkar.
                       Birkar proved bounded lc complements in 2019;
                       Shokurov conjectured klt complements in 2004.
snc                simple normal crossings: local model is a union of
                   coordinate hyperplanes.
torsor             a principal homogeneous space; here a line bund-
                   le with a nowhere-zero section removed.
valuational center  the point below a valuation; where it "sits".
volume             normalized multiplicity growth of a valuation ideal.

================================================================================
PART V. THE ABSTRACT, AND THE CONVENTIONS
================================================================================

  In characteristic zero, the Cartier-divisor conjecture of Birkar and
  Shokurov holds for rational boundaries. Over C, it holds for real
  boundaries. The constants depend only on the total dimension and
  the discrepancy gap of the log canonical pair.

  For the one-line reading of every lemma in the original, see
  PART VIII below. A short list of conventions used throughout:

  - A "pair" (X, B) always means X normal, B effective with rational
    coefficients, K_X + B rationally Cartier.
  - "Bounded dimension" means there is an n_0 such that all examples
    have dim <= n_0.
  - "Eventually", "for all i >> 0" all mean: for all sufficiently
    large indices in the sequence. In practice the proof passes to
    subsequences.

================================================================================
PART VI. WHAT A HIGH SCHOOL READER CAN ACTUALLY DO
================================================================================

The mathematics of the paper is genuinely beyond high school. But the
SHAPE of the proof is not. Below are a handful of concrete things a
curious reader can compute by hand or on a computer, that touch the
"nerve" of the argument. Each is small and finite.

Exercise 1 (The simplest lct calculation). Let X = C^2 with co-
ordinates (x, y), let z = (0, 0), and let D be defined by g(x, y) = x.
Verify that the pair (X, B = 0 + t D) is log canonical if and only if
t <= 1. Use the blowup of z and check the discrepancy of the
exceptional divisor. This is the single easiest case of Theorem A.

Exercise 2 (Threshold of the cube). Let g(x, y) = xy. Show that the
threshold on C^2 is 1. Let g = x^2 + y^2. Show it is 1. Let g = x^2.
Show it is 1/2. Let g = x^3 + y^3. Show it is 2/3. General pattern:
for g homogeneous of degree d with smooth projective zero locus, the
threshold is min(1, 2 / d). Contrast with the smooth case d = 1 where
it is 1.

Exercise 3 (Ratio q(Y) = q(s) - m q(h)). Take X = C, h = x, s = x^m.
Then A(x = 0) = 1 and h(x=0) = 1. Compute Y = s / h^m = 1; q(Y) = 0.
That is the base case of Step 4. Now take X = C, s = x^{m+1}. Then
Y = x, and every q with q(x) > 0 gives q(Y) > 0. This is the "saturation
hypothesis" in the simplest possible case.

Exercise 4 (A character computation). On C[x, y] with the group
mu_2 acting as (x, y) -> (-x, -y), which monomials are invariant? They
are the even-degree monomials. Which have character chi (the nontrivial
character)? The odd-degree monomials. This is the toy model for the
"character by degree" mechanism in Section D.

Exercise 5 (Sanity check of Lemma C.6). Compute the dimension of the
space of homogeneous degree-d polynomials in 3 variables: it is
(d+1)(d+2)/2. This is your typical "section count". The paper needs
bounds of the form "h^0 <= C v_i n^p" where p is the transcendence
degree of a field.

Exercise 6 (Blowup and discrepancy). Blow up the origin in C^2. The
exceptional divisor E is P^1. Since K_{C^2} is trivial, K_{Bl} =
pi^* K_{C^2} + E = E as a divisor. Using the paper's convention
a(E; X, B) = 1 + coeff_E(K_W - pi^*(K_X + B)), check that
a(E; C^2, 0) = 1 + 1 = 2, so (C^2, 0) is 2-lc. This is the basic
blowup calculation underlying Exercises 1 and 2.

Exercise 7 (Fano examples). The complex projective line P^1 is Fano:
-K_{P^1} is ample. The complex projective plane P^2 is Fano. Blowup
P^2 at k <= 8 points in general position; -K remains ample, giving a
del Pezzo surface with (-K)^2 = 9 - k. At k = 9 general points the
square (-K)^2 = 0 and the surface is Calabi-Yau, not Fano; beyond that
-K is not even nef. Compute (-K_{Bl_1 P^2})^2 and (-K_{Bl_1 P^2}) . (a
line) and check positivity. This is the model to keep in mind when the
paper says "Fano type contraction".

Exercise 8 (Can you prove the 2D case?). For X a smooth surface and
z in X with B = 0, pick D = a smooth curve through z. Then (X, D) is
log canonical because both X and D are smooth, so tau = 1 works for
the identity contraction X -> X. More generally the log canonical
threshold of the maximal ideal m_z on a smooth n-fold is n: blow up
z, check discrepancies, then induct. For a surface this is 2, and
Lemma H.7 reduces a bound on lct(m_z) to a Cartier-divisor bound of
min(that, 1) = 1, matching the construction above. Chen (2025) proves
the surface LOCAL assertion with explicit coefficient e^2/24, and
hence both conjectures for bases of dimension at most two (point and
curve bases). The paper here handles arbitrary base dimension. The
2D smooth case is essentially high school; the gap to the general
case is the substance of the paper.

================================================================================
PART VII. WHY THIS MATTERS
================================================================================

Three reasons a high schooler might care beyond "it's math":

(1) The theorem settles a conjecture that has been open for over
    twenty years. That is rare and worth noticing.

(2) The proof is a machine. It does not look at any individual Fano
    type contraction. It uses boundedness of Fano varieties, of
    rationally connected CY varieties, and of nef-and-big divisors
    (all themselves recent theorems). The general strategy -- "pass
    to the central cone, minimize, extract a character, iterate until
    dimension stops you" -- is a template. Variants of it are being
    used in ongoing work on stability and moduli.

(3) The result is non-effective. It produces a positive tau, but says
    nothing about HOW BIG tau is. Only that it depends on d and e.
    This is common in algebraic geometry: the constant exists by
    compactness, not by an explicit construction. If you ever want
    to run this on a computer, you need extra work.

================================================================================
PART VIII. FULL LEMMA INDEX OF THE ORIGINAL
================================================================================

Plain-English description of every named result in the original, in
the order they appear within each section. Numbering below (A.1, A.2,
...) is the reading order in the source, not the compiled subsection
numbers of the printed PDF. For the exact statement and proof of any
item, look it up by name (the names are quoted from the original
headings) in the corresponding section of the original file.

Section A (Discriminant comparison and uniform chambers).

  A.1. Comparison along long parameter intervals (Lemma). The
       discriminant discrepancies of a bounded generalized CY family
       obey the same klt and ratio bounds on the base as on the
       higher model, uniformly over the family.

  A.2. Phases under pure pullback (Lemma). A signed phase bound for a
       rational Cartier divisor on a high model descends uniformly to
       the base in the small-discrepancy limit.

  A.3. A uniform linear chamber (Lemma). For a linear family of
       generalized CY data with bounded-denominator initial data, the
       discriminants stay affine in the family parameter on a
       uniform positive interval.

  A.4. Tools at an lc place (Lemma). At a prescribed lc place, one
       can build a tailored ordinary lc complement, preserving the
       chosen place, with a bounded complement index.

  A.5. Uniform charts (Proposition). Any prescribed place in a Mori
       step sits on a toroidal chart with bounded matrices.
       Discrepancies of arbitrary generalized CY data are Lipschitz
       in the angular coordinates, uniformly in the contact.

  A.6. Bounded toroidal charts (Lemma). Toroidalisation produces
       these charts with bounded structure, after a bounded extension
       of the base field.

  A.7. Degrees control angular variation (Lemma). A rational function
       with bounded generic pole degree has bounded angular Lipschitz
       constant on the chart.

  A.8. Horizontal variants (Corollary). The same chart and metric
       results hold for horizontal places with analogous bounds.

  A.9. Integral approximation (Lemma). Real chart points can be
       approximated multiplicatively by integral chart points whose
       orders are legitimate prime valuations.

  A.10. Quantized chart cells (Claim). When the functions involved
        have bounded denominators, their cells of linearity are cut
        out by finitely many fixed normals and have bounded
        denominators at every vertex.

Section B (Nonvanishing from signed phases).

  B.1. Phase nonvanishing (Lemma). If a bounded family of Fano type
       varieties carries effective CY data whose polarization has
       controlled signed phases at small-discrepancy divisors, then a
       bounded multiple of the polarization has a nonzero section.

  B.2. Bundle construction and discrepancies (Lemma). A rational
       divisor H gives a Seifert-like bundle with two sections whose
       discrepancy formula reduces to a |h| term plus the base
       discrepancy. This is a dictionary from base sections to
       bundle sections.

  B.3. Clearing the small denominators (Lemma). After multiplying H
       by a bounded integer, the denominator boundary becomes klt log
       Fano, so the bundle is of Fano type.

  B.4. An unbounded-index obstruction (Lemma). If the anticanonical
       index of a Fano type variety with a polarization grows without
       bound, then a small-height divisor at infinity with small
       discrepancy cannot exist.

  B.5. Bounding a principalization order (Lemma). If a rational
       divisor P has bounded signed phases at small-discrepancy
       divisors and the polarization has effective Fano type data,
       then the principalization order of P is bounded.

Section C (Cutoff fields, phase bounds, and section counts).

  C.1. Uniform cutoff models and order functions (Lemma). The cutoff
       contraction D_W splits into an ample base polarization plus
       the small-coefficient floor primes, giving bounded field
       generators and a b-nef order function satisfying precise
       inequalities.

  C.2. Scaled phase bound (Lemma). At tiny multiples of the
       polarization, principal orders of any rational function have
       bounded phase by a fixed multiple.

  C.3. Saturation and section counts (Lemma). After a subsequence,
       there is a field plateau K_* in which every bounded-cost
       function lies, and its section counts match a common
       normalization v_i * n^p.

  C.4. Vanishing on a smooth orbifold (Lemma). Kodaira-style
       vanishing holds on a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack with klt
       boundary.

  C.5. Adjoint Taylor exponents (Lemma). For a birational nef and big
       integral divisor N, the jet body determines which Taylor
       exponents actually occur: any interior-lattice exponent of tK
       occurs.

  C.6. Uniform count from the jet body (Lemma). The upper and lower
       bounds for h^0(N) and for the product's dimension are both
       controlled by the volume of the jet body.

  C.7. Fixed generators and neutral algebraic extensions
       (Corollary). At one eventual index, the whole field generated
       by a bounded-cost set lands in K_*, and extensions of
       trivially valued subfields remain trivially valued.

Section D (Characters and regularization on affine cones).

  D.1. Cone dictionary (Proposition). Homogeneous functions on an
       affine cone give a dictionary between self-dual ray weights
       and Cartier divisors on the Proj, with discrepancies and
       phases expressed monomial-minimally.

  D.2. All divisorial lc tests are Gauss (Lemma). Every divisorial
       lc place of the cone is a Gauss valuation, so the divisorial
       testing set is essentially the monomial one.

  D.3. Sections as regular symbols (Lemma). Sections of the cone's
       polarization correspond to regular symbols of the form
       (rational function) * h^b times a monomial z^weight.

  D.4. Sandwich regularization (Proposition). Given bounded phase
       information on the small-discrepancy locus, a bounded
       multiple of J_m has a nonzero regular symbol.

  D.5. Bounded-multiple character regularization (Corollary). For
       a weight in a bounded-width strip, a bounded multiple of a
       character times h^b is regular.

  D.6. Count for a single character and weight (Corollary). The
       number of homogeneous invariant symbols in a bounded-width
       character-weight box is bounded by a constant times v_i *
       n^p.

  D.7. Characters for a tilted height (Corollary). A bounded number
       of negative-degree character eigenfunctions plus a small tilt
       of the polarization gives positive-degree regularization.

Section E (Optimization on common log canonical slices).

  E.1. Logarithmic jet estimate (Lemma). On a fixed resolution, the
       monomial retraction and the valuation differ on polynomials of
       degree at most D by O(D * discrepancy). Iterating gives
       uniform control on every regular function, not only polynomials.

  E.2. Compactness and uniform centring (Lemma). The common lc slice
       is a compact finite polyhedral complex, with uniform lower
       volume bounds on centred valuations.

  E.3. Simultaneous approximation (Corollary). For a single constant
       C, translation to a monomial retraction loses at most (1 + C e)
       in every regular function, uniformly over all such valuations.
       Thus volume of a centred valuation is controlled by the volume
       of its retraction.

  E.4. Convex domination (Lemma). Any convex combination of
       common-slice valuations is dominated (pointwise on every
       regular function) by a single common-slice valuation. The
       allowed heights behave well under this domination.

  E.5. Opposite ratios (Corollary). Opposite-order ratios of the
       form q'/q with q*q' regular interpolate exactly under the
       convex-domination step.

  E.6. Volume interpolation and a strict gap (Lemma). If two
       valuations differ on some regular function by more than a
       fixed factor, their midpoint interpolant has strictly smaller
       volume (relative to either). Near-minimizers are therefore
       multiplicatively close on every regular function.

  E.7. A boundary encoding the common slice (Lemma). An effective
       lc boundary whose zero-discrepancy rays are exactly the
       common slice can be built by adding small pencil members
       with positive bounded coefficients.

  E.8. Exact common-slice minimizer (Theorem). For a rational
       height, minimization over the common slice is realized
       exactly by the normalized-volume minimizer of an ordinary
       klt pair. One single rational perturbation parameter lambda,
       fixed for all competitors, suffices.

  E.9. Near-minimizers for a height (Corollary). Two valuations
       that are both within o(1) relative error of the minimum
       must be multiplicatively 1+o(1) equal on every regular
       function.

  E.10. Domination by the minimizer (Corollary). Any common-slice
        valuation is dominated by 2^n times the minimizer, up to
        height normalization.

  E.11. One-sided height tilt (Lemma). If a common-slice valuation
        has a positive value on a sign function, then a small
        one-sided tilt of the height yields a minimizer that also
        has positive sign and stays bounded on the slice.

Section F (Degeneration and rank refinement).

  F.1. Weighted presentations (Lemma). The associated graded
       algebra of a quasi-monomial valuation is a quotient of a
       power series ring by the initial ideal; nearby rational
       regradings give nearby quasi-monomial valuations.

  F.2. Canonical forms under degeneration (Proposition). The
       reflexive top form descends to the central fibre with the
       same grading weight and group character, and the group
       action on the cone remains quasi-etale.

  F.3. Lc equality and principal boundaries (Corollary). If the
       degenerating valuation is h-lc, the central pair is lc and
       the canonical generator has the same group character as the
       initial symbol of h.

  F.4. Sign transfer (Proposition). An inequality of the form
       q(g) <= 0 on the actual common slice is equivalent to the
       corresponding inequality on the central common slice, for
       any test ratio g, without restriction on its cost.

  F.5. Exact tests and pure weights (Corollary). Exact vanishing
       of a test throughout the actual slice is equivalent to
       exact vanishing of its initial form throughout the central
       slice; pure-weight valuations are always h-lc and common.

  F.6. Compression (Lemma). Two successive gradings (from two
       successive minimizations) can be compressed into a single
       actual valuation on the original germ, preserving all lc
       equalities and all exact tests and increasing the rational
       rank.

  F.7. Rank refinement (Proposition). If a sequence of balanced
       cones satisfies a fixed balance condition, the refinement
       step replaces the valuation by a near-equal one with the
       same main properties and satisfying the angular order
       condition (C-alpha) with error eta_i -> 0.

Section G (Pinning the gradings).

  G.1. Pinning obstruction (Theorem). An infinite sequence of
       quasi-etale character-graded klt germs with lc equality and
       the saturation hypothesis cannot exist. This is the
       contradiction that proves the birational complement theorem.

  G.2. Non-cancellation in every degree (Lemma). Among finitely
       many bounded-cost monomial products, the least-w initial
       term does not cancel at any later working valuation on the
       pre-pinning slice, in every polynomial degree.

  G.3. Uniform product growth (Lemma). For a finite-dimensional
       space V of dimension g with transcendence degree a, N-fold
       products of members of V have dimension at least
       c_n * g * N^a.

  G.4. High-scale pin (Proposition). At a high stage, finitely many
       proportion tests of cost O(L) can be added to force the
       selected values either to zero or to a rational line,
       retaining a centred point and all previous constraints.

  G.5. Field and lattice consequences of the pin (Lemma). The pin
       automatically puts every lattice direction of width O(L) on
       the anchor line, and forces the angular tests to act
       trivially on the cutoff field at the next scale.

  G.6. Additional row via tilted characters (Claim). If a
       projection of the current constrained set is thin, a tilted
       character block produces an integral tie at the comparator
       that is transverse to the previously imposed rows.

Section H (Reduction to the pinning input).

  H.1. Factorial saturation (Proposition). Failure of klt
       complements gives a factorial order w whose section orders
       dominate k A_X(w) on every prime, uniformly, and reduces
       to a geometric generic slice.

  H.2. The isomorphic case (Lemma). In the birational-but-Cartier
       case, a canonical cover produces the exact pinning input
       (torsion, character match, lc equality, saturation).

  H.3. The signed anti-canonical torsor (Lemma). In the general
       birational case, a signed anti-canonical Z-graded torsor
       serves as the model, with both positive and negative slopes
       to catch section vanishing.

  H.4. Padding and the actual quotient discrepancy (Lemma).
       Padding by b-1 extra coordinates makes the canonical
       character match b and preserves a fixed quotient
       discrepancy gap e.

  H.5. The pinning input (Proposition). The reduced data satisfy
       every hypothesis of Theorem G.1 (the pinning obstruction).

  H.6. Point ideals and change of field (Lemma). Reduction to C
       via descent, and the passage between the lc threshold of
       the point ideal and existence of a good Cartier divisor
       through the point.
