\begin{abstract}
Every square matrix $A$ comes with a compact convex subset $W(A)$ of
the complex plane, called its numerical range. The complete Crouzeix
conjecture says: if you plug $A$ into a matrix-valued polynomial $P$,
the result is never larger than twice the biggest value of $P$ on
$W(A)$, and this is uniform in all matrix dimensions. We give a
self-contained direct proof. It uses an exterior conformal map of an
analytic convex domain containing $W(A)$, a positive kernel on the
unit circle, and a two-by-two positive block comparison. Degenerate
cases, where $W(A)$ is a single point or a line segment, are included.
\end{abstract}

\subsection*{The numerical range}

Fix a positive integer $n$ and a matrix $A \in M_n(\C)$. A vector
$u \in \C^n$ with $u^*u = 1$ is a \emph{unit vector}. Its
\emph{Rayleigh quotient} is the complex number $u^* A u$. The
\emph{numerical range} of $A$ is the set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wr}
  W(A) \;=\; \{\,u^* A u \,:\, u \in \C^n,\ u^*u = 1\,\}.
\end{equation}
The numerical range of $A$ is always a compact convex subset of $\C$, and
it always contains the spectrum of $A$: a unit eigenvector $u$ of an
eigenvalue $\lambda$ satisfies $u^* A u = \lambda u^* u = \lambda$.

Two extreme examples are useful.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $A = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ is diagonal with real entries, $W(A)$ is the interval $[\min \lambda_i,\ \max \lambda_i]$ on the real axis.
\item If $A = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 2\\0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, then
      $|u^*Au| = 2|u_1 u_2| \le |u_1|^2 + |u_2|^2 = 1$ for every unit $u$, with equality whenever $|u_1| = |u_2| = 1/\sqrt2$.
      Convexity then forces $W(A)$ to be the closed unit disk $\overline{\D}$.
      Notice that the spectrum is just $\{0\}$, yet the numerical range is the whole disk. This is the simplest example where nonnormality shows up in $W(A)$ but not in the spectrum.
\end{itemize}

For a normal matrix (one with $A A^* = A^* A$), the spectral theorem
gives a unitary $U$ with $A = U\Lambda U^*$, and then $u^*Au$ is a
convex combination of the eigenvalues. So $W(A)$ is exactly the convex
hull of the eigenvalues. For a general matrix, this statement fails
badly: the numerical range is genuinely larger than the spectrum,
and that extra size is what makes the spectral-set constant $2$
possible.

\subsection*{The main theorem}

We allow \emph{matrix-valued} polynomials. If
$P(z) = \sum_{k=0}^d B_k z^k$ with coefficient matrices $B_k \in M_m(\C)$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PA}
  P[A] \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^d A^k \otimes B_k \;\in\; M_n(\C) \otimes M_m(\C) \;\cong\; M_{nm}(\C).
\end{equation}
Here $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product: the factor $A^k$ acts on
$\C^n$ and the factor $B_k$ acts on $\C^m$, and the two actions commute.
Equivalently, $P[A]$ is obtained by replacing the scalar variable $z$
with the matrix $A$, keeping the coefficients $B_k$ in the exact order
written. The two tensor factors are the \emph{base} $\C^n$ and the
\emph{coefficient} $\C^m$.

All norms are the operator norms from the Euclidean inner products and
their tensor products (equivalently, the largest singular value). The
Hilbert--Schmidt norm of a matrix $C$ is denoted $\|C\|_2 = (\tr C^* C)^{1/2}$.

\begin{theorem}[Complete Crouzeix inequality]\label{thm:main}
Let $n,m \ge 1$, let $A\in M_n(\C)$, let $d \ge 0$, and let
$P(z) = \sum_{k=0}^d B_k z^k$ with $B_k \in M_m(\C)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
  \|P[A]\| \;\le\; 2 \, \max_{z \in W(A)} \, \|P(z)\|.
\end{equation}
The constant $2$ is optimal: it cannot be lowered uniformly in $n, m, d$.
\end{theorem}

The word \emph{complete} refers to the uniformity in the coefficient
dimension $m$. A scalar proof (the case $m = 1$) does not automatically
extend, because scalar coefficients commute but matrix coefficients
need not: $F(z)G(z)$ and $G(z)F(z)$ are different $M_m(\C)$-valued
functions in general, and $F[A]G[A]$ is not $G[A]F[A]$. The proof below
keeps these two products separate all the way through, which is why it
works uniformly in $m$.

\subsection*{Why the constant cannot be less than $2$}

Take $n = 2$, $m = 1$, $A = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 2\\0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$,
and $P(z) = z$. The eigenvalue $0$ of $A$ has a two-dimensional
generalised eigenvector space, and $\|A\| = 2$. Meanwhile the previous
subsection showed that $W(A)$ is the closed unit disk, so
$\max_{W(A)} |P(z)| = \max_{W(A)} |z| = 1$. The ratio
$\|P[A]\|/\max_{W(A)}|P| = 2$, so no universal constant below $2$
works. The rest of the paper proves that $2$ is enough.

\subsection*{Roadmap of the proof}

The argument has five steps.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Enclose.} Place $W(A)$ strictly inside a bounded convex
      open set $\Omega$ whose boundary is a smooth real-analytic Jordan
      curve. This lets us use conformal mapping. The final section
      shows how to remove this hypothesis.

\item \textbf{Coordinate.} Build an exterior conformal map
      $h : \{\lambda : |\lambda| > 1\} \to \C \setminus \overline\Omega$.
      On the unit circle $|\lambda|=1$, $h$ parametrises $\partial\Omega$,
      so every boundary integral becomes a circle integral.

\item \textbf{Kernel.} Expand the Cauchy kernel $q(\lambda)/(h(\lambda)-z)$
      at infinity, where $q(\lambda) = \lambda h'(\lambda)$. This
      produces polynomials $b_k(z)$ (the \emph{Faber polynomials} of
      the exterior coordinate), and a positive kernel $K(\lambda,\mu)$
      on the unit circle. Positivity of $K$ is where convexity of
      $\Omega$ enters the proof.

\item \textbf{Coefficient bound.} From $K$, deduce that for every
      matrix-valued polynomial $G = \sum b_k C_k$,
      \begin{equation}\label{eq:road-coef}
        \sum_k \|C_k\|_2^2 \;\le\; \int_\T \|G(h(\mu))\|_2^2\, d\tau(\mu) \;\le\; \|C_0\|_2^2 + 2 \sum_{k\ge1} \|C_k\|_2^2.
      \end{equation}
      The factor of $2$ on the right is the sole source of the constant $2$
      in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. This step uses the positivity of $K$ in a
      decisive way.

\item \textbf{Functional analysis.} Let
      $R(\lambda) = q(\lambda)(h(\lambda)I - A)^{-1}$ be the exterior
      resolvent. Positivity of $R+R^*$ (a consequence of $W(A)\subset\Omega$)
      lets a two-by-two positive block comparison pass \eqref{eq:road-coef}
      to the single polynomial $F[A]$ and force $\|F[A]\|\le 2$
      whenever $\|F\|\le 1$ on $\partial\Omega$. Then shrink
      $\Omega$ down to $W(A)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Prerequisites and notation}

We use:
\begin{itemize}
\item Linear algebra: unitary matrices, tensor products, singular values,
      operator norm $\|\cdot\|$ and Hilbert--Schmidt norm $\|\cdot\|_2$.
\item Complex analysis: holomorphic functions, conformal maps, the
      Riemann mapping theorem, Schwarz reflection. A short self-contained
      proof of the exterior collar is in Appendix~\ref{sec:collar}.
\item Fourier analysis on the unit circle $\T = \{|z|=1\}$, equipped with
      the normalised measure $d\tau(e^{it}) = dt/(2\pi)$. Every constant
      function of modulus one has integral one; every function with only
      positive or only negative Fourier modes has integral zero.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Historical context}

Crouzeix asked the scalar question in 2004 and proved a universal
complete bound of $11.08$ in 2007
\cite{Crouzeix2004,Crouzeix2007}.
The exterior-kernel method traces back to Delyon and Delyon
\cite{DelyonDelyon1999}.
Crouzeix and Palencia obtained the complete bound $1+\sqrt2$
\cite{CrouzeixPalencia2017}, and Ransford and Schwenninger simplified
the final step \cite{RansfordSchwenninger2018}.
Badea, Crouzeix and Delyon proved the complete constant $2$ for all
base matrices of order $2$ \cite{BadeaCrouzeixDelyon2006}, and
\AA hag, Czy{\.z}, Virtanen proved the complete constant $2$ for base
matrices of order $3$ \cite{AhagCzyzVirtanen2026}.
The scalar case $m=1$ was settled recently by Jin \cite{Jin2026},
Lorist and Schwenninger \cite{LoristSchwenninger2026}, and Luo
\cite{Luo2026}.
A structural proof of the complete case is given in the companion
preprint \cite{OpenAI2026Complete}; the exterior kernel, marginal
identities and positive block comparison below have counterparts there.
The point of the present paper is to give a direct and self-contained
argument for the \emph{complete} case, with all matrix dimensions
independent.

\section{Exterior coordinates and Faber coefficients}\label{sec:coords}

Throughout this section, $\Omega \subset \C$ is a bounded open convex
set whose boundary is a \emph{regular real-analytic Jordan curve}:
a simple closed curve parametrised real-analytically with nowhere-zero
derivative. The unit circle is $\T = \{\lambda : |\lambda|=1\}$. The
exterior unit disk is $\C \setminus \overline{\D} = \{\lambda : |\lambda| > 1\}$.
Every circle integral uses $d\tau(e^{it}) = dt/(2\pi)$, so constant
functions of modulus one have integral one.

\subsection*{The exterior coordinate}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:exterior-collar} in the Appendix, there is a number
$0 < r_0 < 1$ and a map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h}
  h(\lambda) \;=\; c\lambda + c_0 + \sum_{j \ge 1} c_j \lambda^{-j}, \qquad c \ne 0,
\end{equation}
meromorphic on $\{\lambda : |\lambda| > r_0\} \cup \{\infty\}$, one-to-one
there as a map of the Riemann sphere, with a simple pole at infinity,
mapping $|\lambda|>1$ conformally onto $\C \setminus \overline\Omega$
and $|\lambda|=1$ onto $\partial\Omega$. This is the
\emph{exterior conformal map}, or \emph{exterior coordinate} of $\Omega$.

The Appendix gives a complete derivation from Riemann's mapping theorem.
The reader who wants to accept it can take \eqref{eq:h} and the
statements below as basic inputs.

\subsection*{The supporting line and the outward normal}

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q}
  q(\lambda) \;=\; \lambda h'(\lambda).
\end{equation}
The two partial derivatives of $h$ at a point $e^{i\theta} \in \T$ are
$\partial_\theta h = i q$ (tangent to the boundary) and
$\partial_r h = q$ where $r = |\lambda|$ (radial direction, pointing
outward from $\overline\Omega$). So $q(\lambda)$ is a nonzero outward
normal vector at $h(\lambda) \in \partial\Omega$, and $i q(\lambda)$ is
a positively oriented tangent vector.

Because $\Omega$ is convex, the entire set $\overline\Omega$ lies on the
inner side of the supporting line through $h(\lambda)$ with normal
direction $q(\lambda)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:support}
  \Re\!\left( \overline{q(\lambda)} \bigl( h(\lambda) - z \bigr) \right) \;\ge\; 0
  \qquad (\lambda \in \T,\ z \in \overline\Omega).
\end{equation}
This is the only geometric input from convexity, and it is what makes
the kernel below nonnegative. Figure~\ref{fig:normal} shows the picture.

\subsection*{Faber polynomials}

Define scalar polynomials $b_k(z)$, $k \ge 0$, by the expansion at infinity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:faber}
  \frac{q(\lambda)}{h(\lambda) - z}
  \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} b_k(z)\, \lambda^{-k}, \qquad |\lambda| \gg 1.
\end{equation}
The left side is holomorphic at $\lambda = \infty$ (since
$h(\lambda) \sim c \lambda$ with $c \ne 0$), so the expansion makes sense.
These $b_k$ are the \emph{Faber polynomials} of the exterior coordinate $h$;
they are the natural generalisation of the monomials $z^k$ to the domain
$\Omega$. In the simplest case $\Omega = \D$ with $h(\lambda) = \lambda$,
one has $b_k(z) = z^k$; for a general $\Omega$, the $b_k$ have
corrections coming from the shape of $\partial\Omega$.

We can be more precise: after factoring $c \lambda$ from the denominator,
we find $b_0 = 1$ and, for $k \ge 1$, $b_k$ is a polynomial of degree $k$
with leading coefficient $c^{-k}$. So $(b_k)_{k \ge 0}$ really is a basis
of the space of polynomials, and polynomial degrees match indices.
This is useful because it lets us rewrite any matrix polynomial
$G(z) = \sum_{k=0}^N b_k(z) C_k$ uniquely in the Faber basis, with
$C_k \in M_m(\C)$ in the natural way.

\subsection*{Boundary values and the mixed coefficient}

The boundary restrictions $b_k \circ h$ of the Faber polynomials have a
remarkably clean Fourier form. There are scalars $s_{kj}$, indexed by
$k,j \ge 1$, such that on the exterior collar one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-basis}
  b_k(h(\mu)) \;=\; \mu^k \;+\; \sum_{j\ge1} s_{kj}\, \mu^{-j}, \qquad k \ge 1.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the \emph{mixed correction kernel}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s}
  s(\lambda,\mu) \;=\; \frac{q(\lambda)}{h(\lambda) - h(\mu)} \;-\; \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - \mu}
  \;=\; \sum_{k,j \ge 1} s_{kj}\, \lambda^{-k} \mu^{-j}
\end{equation}
collects exactly the difference between the true boundary kernel and
the boundary kernel of the unit disk.

The function $s$ has a nice structure. It extends holomorphically
across the diagonal $\lambda = \mu$ and across both infinities, so it
is regular on a full product collar of the exterior. Its mixed Taylor
coefficients decay geometrically:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s-decay}
  |s_{kj}| \;\le\; C_\rho\, \rho^{k+j} \qquad (r_0 < \rho < 1,\ k,j \ge 1).
\end{equation}
Consequently the double series converges normally on the exterior
product collar, hence absolutely and uniformly on $\T^2$. So every
interchange of sum and integral below is legal.

The kernel we will need is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K}
  K(\lambda,\mu) \;=\; 1 + s(\lambda,\mu) + \overline{s(\lambda,\mu)}.
\end{equation}
Since $s$ has only strictly mixed Fourier modes
($\lambda^{-k}\mu^{-j}$ with $k,j \ge 1$), neither $s$ nor its conjugate
contributes to the integral in $\lambda$ alone or in $\mu$ alone. So
\[
  \int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, d\tau(\lambda) = 1 \quad (\text{for each fixed } \mu),
  \qquad
  \int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, d\tau(\mu) = 1 \quad (\text{for each fixed } \lambda).
\]
These are the \emph{marginal identities}. They say: $K$ is like the
kernel of the identity operator, but with a real, symmetric, nonnegative
correction.

\subsection*{The positive kernel theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Exterior Faber coefficients and the positive kernel]\label{lem:faber}
Let $\Omega$ be a bounded convex domain with regular real-analytic Jordan
boundary. Let $h$ be as in \eqref{eq:h}, and let $q$, $(b_k)_{k\ge0}$,
and $s_{kj}$ be defined by \eqref{eq:q}, \eqref{eq:faber}, and
\eqref{eq:s}. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $b_0 = 1$, and for each $k \ge 1$, $b_k$ is a polynomial of
      degree $k$ with leading coefficient $c^{-k}$ in \eqref{eq:h}.
\item The mixed coefficients $s_{kj}$ satisfy the geometric decay
      estimate \eqref{eq:s-decay}.
\item The kernel $K$ in \eqref{eq:K} is continuous on $\T^2$,
      nonnegative everywhere, and has integral one in each variable
      with the other fixed.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Part 1 (degree and leading coefficient).}
Write $h(\lambda) - z = c\lambda \cdot (1 + (c_0 - z)/(c\lambda) + O(\lambda^{-2}))$.
For large $|\lambda|$,
\[
  \frac{q(\lambda)}{h(\lambda) - z}
  = \frac{c\lambda + O(\lambda^{-1})}{c\lambda} \cdot
  \left(1 + \frac{c_0 - z}{c\lambda} + \cdots\right)^{-1}
  = 1 + O(\lambda^{-1}).
\]
Expanding the inverse as a geometric series in $(c_0-z)/(c\lambda)$
produces, for the coefficient of $\lambda^{-k}$, a polynomial in $z$
of degree $k$. Inspecting the largest power of $z$ shows it is $c^{-k} z^k$.
So $b_0 = 1$ and $b_k(z) = c^{-k} z^k + (\text{lower degree})$ for $k \ge 1$.
In particular the $b_k$ form a basis of the scalar polynomials.

\emph{Part 2 (geometric decay and joint extension).}
Consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
  H(\lambda,\mu) \;=\; \frac{h(\lambda) - h(\mu)}{\lambda - \mu}.
\end{equation}
Write $u = \lambda^{-1}$, $v = \mu^{-1}$, and $h(\lambda) = c\lambda + g(u)$
with $g$ holomorphic on $|u| < r_0^{-1}$. Then
\[
  H(\lambda,\mu) = c - uv \, \frac{g(u) - g(v)}{u - v}.
\]
The divided difference $(g(u)-g(v))/(u-v)$ is jointly holomorphic in
$(u,v)$, with diagonal value $g'(u)$, so this formula covers the diagonal
and infinities. Direct differentiation gives
\[
  s(\lambda,\mu) \;=\; \frac{\lambda \partial_\lambda H}{H} \;=\; -\frac{u \partial_u H}{H}.
\]
Since $H$ is nonzero on the reciprocal bidisk $|u|,|v| < r_0^{-1}$
(injectivity of $h$, and $H \to c$ at infinity), $s$ is holomorphic
there and vanishes on both axes $\{u=0\}$ and $\{v=0\}$. Its Taylor
expansion therefore contains exactly the strictly mixed powers
$\lambda^{-k}\mu^{-j}$ with $k,j \ge 1$.

For any $r_0 < \rho < 1$, apply Cauchy estimates on the closed disk
$|u|,|v| \le \rho^{-1}$: there is a constant $C_\rho$ with
$|s_{kj}| \le C_\rho \rho^{k+j}$. Summing the resulting geometric series
shows the Laurent expansion \eqref{eq:s} converges normally (absolutely
and uniformly on compacta, including those meeting infinity) throughout
the product collar $|\lambda|,|\mu| > r_0$. Restricting to $|\lambda| = |\mu| = 1$
gives absolute uniform convergence on $\T^2$.

Comparing \eqref{eq:faber} and \eqref{eq:s} coefficient-by-coefficient
in $\lambda^{-k}$, with $\mu$ fixed in the exterior collar, yields
\eqref{eq:boundary-basis}. Indeed, $\lambda/(\lambda-\mu) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \mu^k \lambda^{-k}$,
so the difference of the two sides matches $b_k(h(\mu)) - \mu^k$.

\emph{Part 3 (positivity of $K$ and marginal identities).}
For distinct $\lambda,\mu \in \T$, split $K$ as
\[
  K(\lambda,\mu) \;=\; 2\,\Re \frac{q(\lambda)}{h(\lambda) - h(\mu)} \;+\; \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\mu} - \frac{\bar\lambda}{\bar\lambda-\bar\mu}\right).
\]
On $|\lambda| = |\mu| = 1$, we have $\bar\lambda = 1/\lambda$, $\bar\mu = 1/\mu$, and
one verifies directly that $\lambda/(\lambda-\mu) + \bar\lambda/(\bar\lambda-\bar\mu) = 1$,
so the bracketed term vanishes. Hence
\[
  K(\lambda,\mu) \;=\; 2\,\Re \frac{q(\lambda)}{h(\lambda) - h(\mu)}.
\]
Now $\Re(q/(h(\lambda) - h(\mu))) = |q|^2 / \Re(\overline{q}(h(\lambda) - h(\mu)))$
where $|q|^2 > 0$, and the denominator is nonnegative by \eqref{eq:support}
with $z = h(\mu)$. So $K(\lambda,\mu) \ge 0$ for distinct $\lambda,\mu$,
and continuity gives the same on the diagonal.

For the marginal identities: every term in $s$ has Fourier mode
$\lambda^{-k}\mu^{-j}$ with $k,j \ge 1$, so integrating against
$d\tau(\lambda)$ picks out $k=0$, which never occurs; and similarly for
the conjugate. Hence
$\int_\T s(\lambda,\mu) d\tau(\lambda) = \int_\T \overline{s(\lambda,\mu)} d\tau(\lambda) = 0$,
so $\int_\T K(\lambda,\mu) d\tau(\lambda) = 1$. Symmetric argument in $\mu$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Identification of $b_k$ as the Faber polynomial}

Let $\Phi = h^{-1}$ denote the inverse of $h$ on the exterior collar
$\C \setminus \overline\Omega \to \{\lambda : |\lambda| > 1\}$. Then
\eqref{eq:boundary-basis}, which continues to hold throughout the
exterior collar, gives
\[
  b_k(z) - \Phi(z)^k = O(z^{-1}), \qquad z \to \infty.
\]
So $b_k$ is the polynomial part of $\Phi^k$ -- the classical
\emph{Faber polynomial} associated with the exterior coordinate $h$
(see \cite{Beckermann2005, BeckermannCrouzeix2014} for background).
This is why the notation $b_k$ is standard.

The mixed coefficients $s_{kj}$ are closely related to the
differentiated \emph{Grunsky coefficients} of the exterior map
$\Phi$; the classical normalization gives a weighted coefficient
inequality \cite[eq.~(1.1)--(1.3)]{BeckermannStylianopoulos2018}.
The positivity estimate we need below is a consequence of convexity
and the marginal identities already proved in Theorem~\ref{lem:faber};
no extra Grunsky estimate is required.

\section{The coefficient norm estimate}\label{sec:coef}

The positive kernel $K$ from Theorem~\ref{lem:faber} is the entire source
of the constant $2$ in the main theorem. This section extracts one clean
inequality from it. Fix any dimension $m$ and any finite-family of
coefficient matrices $C_0, C_1, \dots, C_N \in M_m(\C)$, and put
\[
  G(z) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^N b_k(z)\, C_k.
\]
That is, $G$ is an $M_m(\C)$-valued polynomial written in the Faber basis
of $\Omega$. We want to bound the Hilbert--Schmidt $L^2$-norm of
$G \circ h$ on the unit circle from above and below by the coefficient
Hilbert--Schmidt norms $\|C_k\|_2$.

\subsection*{The operator $T$ defined by $K$}

Consider the bounded operator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T}
  (Tu)(\mu) \;=\; \int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, u(\lambda)\, d\tau(\lambda),
  \qquad u \in L^2(\T;\mathcal H),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal H$ is any separable Hilbert space (in practice
$\mathcal H = M_m(\C)$ with the Hilbert--Schmidt inner product).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:T}
The operator $T$ extends to a contraction of $L^2(\T;\mathcal H)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Nonnegativity $K \ge 0$ and the marginal identity
$\int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, d\tau(\lambda) = 1$ together give, for each
fixed $\mu$, by Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
  \|(Tu)(\mu)\|^2
  \;\le\; \left(\int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, d\tau(\lambda)\right)
            \left(\int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, \|u(\lambda)\|^2\, d\tau(\lambda)\right)
  \;=\; \int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, \|u(\lambda)\|^2\, d\tau(\lambda).
\]
Integrate over $\mu$ and use the other marginal identity:
\[
  \|Tu\|_{L^2}^2
  \;\le\; \int_\T \left(\int_\T K(\lambda,\mu)\, d\tau(\mu)\right)\, \|u(\lambda)\|^2\, d\tau(\lambda)
  \;=\; \|u\|_{L^2}^2.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection*{What $T$ does on monomials}

For a vector-valued function of the form
$u(\lambda) = \sum_{k=1}^N C_k \lambda^k$, where each $C_k \in M_m(\C)$,
direct substitution in \eqref{eq:T} shows that only the $s$ summand
of $K$ contributes: the term $1$ in $K$ gives a zero integral
(nonzero frequencies $\lambda^1,\dots,\lambda^N$), and the term
$\overline{s}$ gives a zero integral because
$\overline{s(\lambda,\mu)} = \sum_{k,j\ge1} \overline{s_{kj}} \lambda^k \mu^j$, whose
product with $C_k \lambda^k$ has only nonnegative powers of $\lambda$.
So
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tu-monom}
  (Tu)(\mu) \;=\; \sum_{j\ge1} \Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^N s_{kj}\, C_k\Bigr)\, \mu^{-j}.
\end{equation}
The normal convergence in Theorem~\ref{lem:faber} makes all interchanges
legal. Because the $\mu^{-j}$ are orthonormal in $L^2(\T)$,
\[
  \|Tu\|_{L^2}^2 \;=\; \sum_{j\ge1} \Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^N s_{kj}\, C_k\Bigr\|_2^2,
\]
where $\|\cdot\|_2$ denotes the Hilbert--Schmidt norm on $M_m(\C)$.
Combined with Lemma~\ref{lem:T},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coef-contraction}
  \sum_{j\ge1} \Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^N s_{kj}\, C_k\Bigr\|_2^2 \;\le\; \sum_{k=1}^N \|C_k\|_2^2.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{The two-sided coefficient inequality}

Now observe that on the boundary circle, by \eqref{eq:boundary-basis},
\[
  G(h(\mu)) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^N b_k(h(\mu)) C_k
             \;=\; C_0 + \sum_{k=1}^N C_k \mu^k + \sum_{k=1}^N \left(\sum_{j\ge1} s_{kj}\, \mu^{-j}\right) C_k
             \;=\; C_0 + u(\mu) + (Tu)(\mu).
\]
The three summands $C_0$, $u$, and $Tu$ have disjoint Fourier supports
(constant, positive, negative). Parseval's identity applies:
\[
  \int_\T \|G(h(\mu))\|_2^2\, d\tau(\mu)
  \;=\; \|C_0\|_2^2 + \sum_{k=1}^N \|C_k\|_2^2 + \|Tu\|_{L^2}^2.
\]
Combine this identity with \eqref{eq:coef-contraction}. The case gives
\[
  \|C_0\|_2^2 + \sum_{k=1}^N \|C_k\|_2^2
  \;\le\; \int_\T \|G(h(\mu))\|_2^2\, d\tau(\mu)
  \;\le\; \|C_0\|_2^2 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^N \|C_k\|_2^2.
\]
This is the key estimate. In words: passing from a coefficient array
$(C_0, C_1, \dots)$ to the boundary values of $G$ can enlarge the
coefficient Hilbert--Schmidt norm by at most a universal factor of $2$,
and never shrinks it.

\begin{proposition}[The coefficient norm estimate]\label{prop:coefficients}
For every positive integer $m$, every integer $N \ge 0$, and every
$M_m(\C)$-valued polynomial $G(z) = \sum_{k=0}^N b_k(z)\, C_k$ in the
Faber basis of $\Omega$, one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-estimate}
  \sum_{k=0}^N \|C_k\|_2^2
  \;\le\; \int_\T \|G(h(\mu))\|_2^2\, d\tau(\mu)
  \;\le\; \|C_0\|_2^2 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^N \|C_k\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
This is exactly the combined identity above. The lower bound uses only
$\|Tu\|^2 \ge 0$. The upper bound uses Lemma~\ref{lem:T} to shoehorn
$\|Tu\|_{L^2} \le \sum_k \|C_k\|_2$ into the Parseval identity.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{A note on the literature}

For comparison with \cite[Proposition~3.1]{OpenAI2026Complete}, the
companion paper's exterior map $G$ and variables $t, w$ correspond to
$h, \lambda, \mu$, respectively. The mixed correction satisfies
$s(\lambda,\mu) = B(\mu,\lambda)$, so $s_{kj} = \beta_{jk}$ in that paper's
notation. Our formula \eqref{eq:Tu-monom} uses this orientation, and
the proof above does not require symmetry of the kernel.

\section{The resolvent and the double-layer positivity}\label{sec:resolvent}

From now on the matrix $A \in M_n(\C)$ is fixed, with $W(A) \subset \Omega$.
We will also fix the exterior coordinate $h$ of $\Omega$ from
section~\ref{sec:coords}. The purpose of this section is to define the
\emph{exterior resolvent} and show that it has a positive Hermitian part.

\subsection*{The resolvent function}

Define, on the exterior circle $\T$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R-def}
  R(\lambda) \;=\; q(\lambda) \left( h(\lambda) I - A \right)^{-1}
  \;\in\; M_n(\C).
\end{equation}
Here $h(\lambda) I - A$ is invertible whenever $h(\lambda) \notin \sigma(A)$.
Since $\sigma(A) \subset W(A) \subset \Omega$ (compact) and the circle
$\T$ parametrises $\partial\Omega$ (compact, disjoint from $\sigma(A)$),
the resolvent exists and is continuous on $\T$, and extends
holomorphically to a collar $\{|\lambda| > r_A\}$ for some $r_0 < r_A < 1$
with $r_A$ closer to one than $r_0$.

\begin{theorem}[Resolvent expansion and double-layer positivity]\label{lem:resolvent}
Let $\Omega, h, q, (b_k)$ be as above. For every $n \ge 1$ and every
$A \in M_n(\C)$ with $W(A) \subset \Omega$, the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Expansion.} $R$ has the Laurent series
      \begin{equation}\label{eq:R-expansion}
        R(\lambda) \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} b_k(A)\, \lambda^{-k}, \qquad |\lambda| \ge 1,
      \end{equation}
      converging absolutely and uniformly on $\T$. The coefficient
      matrices $b_k(A)$ decay geometrically: there are constants
      $C < \infty$ and $0 < \theta < 1$ (depending on $A$ and $h$) with
      $\|b_k(A)\| \le C \theta^k$ for all $k \ge 0$.
\item \emph{Means.} $\int_\T R(\lambda)\, d\tau(\lambda) = I$ and
      $\int_\T (R(\lambda) + R(\lambda)^*)\, d\tau(\lambda) = 2I$.
\item \emph{Positivity.} On the exterior circle,
      $R(\lambda) + R(\lambda)^* \succeq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Part 1 (expansion and decay).}
Every eigenvalue of $A$ belongs to $W(A) \subset \Omega$, by testing
the corresponding unit eigenvector. Hence the resolvent
$(h(\lambda) I - A)^{-1}$ is holomorphic on and outside $\T$, including
a small exterior collar. Expanding at $\infty$ in $\lambda$ gives a
Laurent series in $\lambda^{-1}$, and comparing with \eqref{eq:faber}
with $z$ replaced by $A$ shows that the coefficient of $\lambda^{-k}$
is exactly $b_k(A)$. The series converges absolutely and uniformly on
$\T$ by compactness of the exterior collar. To get the geometric
bound, pick $r_A < \theta < 1$ with $R$ holomorphic on
$|\lambda| > r_A$, apply Cauchy estimates on the circle
$|\lambda| = \theta^{-1}$.

\emph{Part 2 (means).}
The constant coefficient $b_0(A) = b_0(z)\big|_{z=A} = I$
(Theorem~\ref{lem:faber}, part 1). So by \eqref{eq:R-expansion} and
orthogonality of the modes $\lambda^{-k}$,
$\int_\T R\, d\tau = b_0(A) = I$. Taking adjoints and adding gives
$\int_\T (R+R^*)\, d\tau = 2I$.

\emph{Part 3 (positivity).}
Let $B(\lambda) = h(\lambda) I - A$ so that $R = q B^{-1}$. Then
$R^* = \overline{q}\, (B^*)^{-1}$ and
\[
  R + R^* \;=\; q\, B^{-1} + \overline{q}\, (B^*)^{-1}
  \;=\; (B^*)^{-1} \left( \overline{q}\, B + q\, B^* \right) B^{-1}.
\]
Since $(B^*)^{-1}$ and $B^{-1}$ are adjoints of each other, they give a
congruence that preserves positivity. So it suffices to show that
$\overline{q(\lambda)}\, B(\lambda) + q(\lambda)\, B(\lambda)^* \succeq 0$.

For any unit vector $v \in \C^n$, the quadratic form on the left at $v$
is
\[
  v^* \left( \overline{q}\, B + q\, B^* \right) v
  \;=\; \overline{q}\, v^* B v + q\, v^* B^* v
  \;=\; 2 \Re\!\left( \overline{q(\lambda)}\, v^* B(\lambda) v \right).
\]
Substituting $B = h(\lambda) I - A$:
\[
  v^* B v \;=\; h(\lambda) - v^* A v.
\]
So the quadratic form is
$\Re\!\left( \overline{q(\lambda)} (h(\lambda) - v^* A v) \right)$.
By the support inequality \eqref{eq:support} with $z = v^* A v \in W(A) \subset \overline\Omega$,
this is $\ge 0$. The congruence argument finishes the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{The main estimate on an analytic convex domain}\label{sec:matrix}

Now the payoff. In this section $\Omega, h, q, (b_k)$ are as in
Section~\ref{sec:coords}, $A \in M_n(\C)$ satisfies $W(A) \subset \Omega$,
and $R(\lambda) = q(\lambda)(h(\lambda) I - A)^{-1}$ is the exterior
resolvent from Theorem~\ref{lem:resolvent}.

\subsection*{Statement and strategy}

\begin{theorem}[Bound on an analytic convex domain]\label{thm:domain}
Let $n, m \ge 1$. Let $\Omega \subset \C$ be bounded, open, convex,
with regular real-analytic Jordan boundary, and let $A \in M_n(\C)$ with
$W(A) \subset \Omega$. Let $F$ be an $M_m(\C)$-valued polynomial with
$\|F(z)\| \le 1$ on $\partial\Omega$ (equivalently, on $\overline\Omega$,
since $F$ is a polynomial and the boundary controls the closure). Then
\[
  \|F[A]\| \;\le\; 2.
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is a bookkeeping exercise with three moving pieces:
\begin{itemize}
\item Top singular vectors $x, y$ of $F[A]$ that turn the norm into a
      \emph{scalar} $y^* F[A] x = \|F[A]\|$.
\item The two products $FG$ and $GF$ that turn $x^* G[A] x$ and
      $y^* G[A] y$ into the same scalar $y^* (\cdot)[A] x$ for two
      different choices of $G$.
\item The two-by-two block $R+R^*\succeq 0$ that lets positivity compare
      the three fields $P+P^*$, $Q+Q^*$, and $M+M^*$ to each other.
\end{itemize}
Each piece contributes one inequality, and together they force $\|F[A]\|\le 2$.

\subsection*{Setup: singular vectors and block notation}

Put $\gamma = \|F[A]\|$. If $\gamma = 0$, there is nothing to prove, so
assume $\gamma > 0$. Choose unit vectors $x, y \in \C^n \otimes \C^m$
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singular}
  F[A] x \;=\; \gamma y, \qquad F[A]^* y \;=\; \gamma x.
\end{equation}
Such a pair exists by the finite-dimensional singular value decomposition.

Identify $\C^n \otimes \C^m \cong (\C^n)^m = \C^{nm}$ via the standard
basis $e_1, \dots, e_m$ of $\C^m$. Any vector $x \in \C^n \otimes \C^m$
can be written uniquely as $x = \sum_{i=1}^m X e_i \otimes e_i$ for an
$n \times m$ matrix $X$. Similarly, write $y = \sum_{i=1}^m Y e_i \otimes e_i$
with $Y$ an $n \times m$ matrix. The unit-length conditions become
\[
  \|X\|_2^2 = \|x\|^2 = 1, \qquad \|Y\|_2^2 = \|y\|^2 = 1
\]
(here and below $\|X\|_2^2 = \tr(X^* X)$).

Define the $m \times m$ matrix functions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PQML}
  P = X^* R X, \quad Q = Y^* R Y, \quad M = Y^* R X, \quad L = X^* R Y.
\end{equation}
Their Laurent coefficients in the variable $\lambda^{-1}$ will be
denoted with subscripts: $M_k = Y^* b_k(A) X$, $P_k = X^* b_k(A) X$,
$Q_k = Y^* b_k(A) Y$, $L_k = X^* b_k(A) Y$. Since $\|b_k(A)\| \le C \theta^k$
(Theorem~\ref{lem:resolvent}), all four series converge absolutely and
uniformly on $\T$, and all four are square-summable in the Hilbert-Schmidt
norm.

Important bookkeeping at $k = 0$. Since $b_0(A) = I$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PQML-0}
  P_0 = X^* X, \quad Q_0 = Y^* Y, \quad L_0^* = M_0.
\end{equation}
In particular $P_0$ and $Q_0$ are Hermitian and positive semidefinite.

Finally, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a}
  a^2 \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} \|M_k\|_2^2, \qquad\text{finite and } a > 0.
\end{equation}
The finiteness is from the geometric decay of $b_k(A)$. The positivity
$a > 0$ follows from applying the cross-functional bound below to $G = F$:
the value $y^* F[A] x = \gamma > 0$ cannot be achieved by a Cauchy-Schwarz
bound with a zero right-hand side.

\subsection*{Step A. The off-diagonal functional}

For any $M_m(\C)$-valued polynomial $G(z) = \sum_{k=0}^N b_k(z) C_k$
written in the Faber basis, direct expansion in the tensor product gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairing}
  y^* G[A] x \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^N \sum_{i,j=1}^m (M_k)_{ij}\, (C_k)_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Let us verify \eqref{eq:pairing} once (it is used three more times).
Since $x = \sum_j X e_j \otimes e_j$ and
$G[A] = \sum_k b_k(A) \otimes C_k$,
\[
  G[A] x \;=\; \sum_{k,j} \bigl(b_k(A) X e_j\bigr) \otimes \bigl(C_k e_j\bigr).
\]
Pairing with $y = \sum_i Y e_i \otimes e_i$:
\[
  y^* G[A] x \;=\; \sum_{k,i,j} (Y e_i)^* b_k(A) (X e_j) \cdot e_i^* C_k e_j
  \;=\; \sum_{k,i,j} (M_k)_{ij}\, (C_k)_{ij},
\]
as claimed. Equation \eqref{eq:pairing} is a scalar bilinear pairing of
the coefficient arrays $(M_k)_{ij}$ and $(C_k)_{ij}$.

Cauchy-Schwarz on \eqref{eq:pairing} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossfunctional}
  |y^* G[A] x|
  \;\le\; \left( \sum_{k\ge0} \|M_k\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2}
         \left( \sum_{k=0}^N \|C_k\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2}
  \;=\; a \left( \sum_{k=0}^N \|C_k\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Plugging in $G = F$ and using $y^* F[A] x = \gamma$ from \eqref{eq:singular}
shows that $a > 0$.

\subsection*{Step B. Two ordered tests}

Polynomial evaluation with matrix coefficients respects multiplication
in the given order. If $F(z) = \sum_i D_i z^i$ and $G(z) = \sum_j E_j z^j$,
then
\[
  (FG)[A] \;=\; \sum_{i,j} A^{i+j} \otimes D_i E_j \;=\; F[A] G[A].
\]
Similarly $(GF)[A] = G[A] F[A]$ when factors are in the reversed order.
Multiplying \eqref{eq:singular} on the left by suitable pieces,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordered-products}
  y^* (FG)[A] x \;=\; y^* F[A] G[A] x \;=\; \gamma\, x^* G[A] x,
  \qquad
  y^* (GF)[A] x \;=\; \gamma\, y^* G[A] y.
\end{equation}
So the diagonal functionals $x^* G[A] x$ and $y^* G[A] y$ can be turned
into the off-diagonal functional by ordering the two factors differently.

Write the two products in the Faber basis:
\[
  FG \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} b_k U_k, \qquad GF \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} b_k V_k,
\]
with finitely many nonzero coefficient matrices $U_k, V_k \in M_m(\C)$.
Define the boundary norm
\[
  \|H\|_\partial^2 \;=\; \int_\T \|H(h(\mu))\|_2^2\, d\tau(\mu),
  \qquad H \in M_m(\C)[z].
\]
Because $\|F(z)\| \le 1$ on the boundary, submultiplicativity of the
pointwise operator norm gives
\[
  \|FG\|_\partial \;\le\; \|F\|_\partial\, \|G\|_\partial \;\le\; \|G\|_\partial,
  \qquad
  \|GF\|_\partial \;\le\; \|G\|_\partial.
\]
(The two inequalities $\|F(z)G(z)\|_2 \le \|F(z)\| \|G(z)\|_2$ and
$\|G(z)F(z)\|_2 \le \|G(z)\|_2 \|F(z)\|$ are used in their respective
orders.)

Apply \eqref{eq:crossfunctional} to $FG$ and $GF$, and use
\eqref{eq:ordered-products}, the lower coefficient estimate from
Proposition~\ref{prop:coefficients}, and the boundary multiplier bounds:
\begin{align*}
  \gamma\, |x^* G[A] x|
    &= |y^* (FG)[A] x|
    \le a \left( \sum_k \|U_k\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2}
    \le a \|FG\|_\partial
    \le a \|G\|_\partial, \\
  \gamma\, |y^* G[A] y|
    &= |y^* (GF)[A] x|
    \le a \left( \sum_k \|V_k\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2}
    \le a \|GF\|_\partial
    \le a \|G\|_\partial.
\end{align*}
The upper coefficient estimate for $G$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:coefficients}
turns $\|G\|_\partial$ into a norm of the coefficient array:
$\|G\|_\partial \le (\|C_0\|_2^2 + 2 \sum_{k\ge1} \|C_k\|_2^2)^{1/2}$.
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diagonal-functional}
  \max\bigl\{ |x^* G[A] x|, |y^* G[A] y| \bigr\}
  \;\le\; \frac{a}{\gamma}\,
        \left( \|C_0\|_2^2 \;+\; 2 \sum_{k\ge1} \|C_k\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
This is the crucial estimate that produces the weight of two.

\subsection*{Step C. Weighted dual norms}

The functional $x^* G[A] x$ pairs $C_k$ against $P_k$
(repeat the calculation of \eqref{eq:pairing}, but with $y$ replaced by
$x$; the coefficient array is $P_k = X^* b_k(A) X$). Similarly $y^* G[A] y$
pairs $C_k$ against $Q_k$. So \eqref{eq:diagonal-functional} constrains
the size of the sequences $(P_k)$ and $(Q_k)$, with weights that reverse
the ones in \eqref{eq:diagonal-functional}.

Concretely, for each $N \ge 0$ set
\[
  d_N^{(P)} \;=\; \|P_0\|_2^2 \;+\; \tfrac12 \sum_{k=1}^N \|P_k\|_2^2.
\]
Test \eqref{eq:diagonal-functional} with the coefficient sequence
\[
  C_0 = \overline{P_0}, \qquad C_k = \overline{P_k}/2 \;(1 \le k \le N), \qquad C_k = 0 \;(k > N),
\]
where the bar denotes entrywise complex conjugation. The scalar paired
value is exactly $d_N^{(P)}$. The weighted norm on the right is
$\|C_0\|_2^2 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^N \|C_k\|_2^2 = \|P_0\|_2^2 + \tfrac12 \sum_{k=1}^N \|P_k\|_2^2 = d_N^{(P)}$.
So
\[
  d_N^{(P)} \;\le\; \frac{a}{\gamma} \,(d_N^{(P)})^{1/2}.
\]
If $d_N^{(P)} = 0$ this is trivial. Otherwise divide and square:
$d_N^{(P)} \le a^2/\gamma^2$. Letting $N \to \infty$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-dual}
  \|P_0\|_2^2 + \tfrac12 \sum_{k\ge1} \|P_k\|_2^2 \;\le\; \frac{a^2}{\gamma^2},
  \qquad
  \|Q_0\|_2^2 + \tfrac12 \sum_{k\ge1} \|Q_k\|_2^2 \;\le\; \frac{a^2}{\gamma^2}.
\end{equation}
The same finite test with $Q_k$ in place of $P_k$ gives the second bound.

\subsection*{Step D. Boundary $L^2$ norms of the blocks}

Now compute the square-integrated Hilbert-Schmidt norms of the three
extreme matrix fields $P+P^*$, $Q+Q^*$, and $M+L^*$ on the circle.

\emph{Diagonal blocks.} The function $P+P^*$ has Laurent expansion
\[
  (P+P^*)(\lambda) \;=\; 2 P_0 \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} P_k\, \lambda^{-k} \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} P_k^*\, \lambda^{k}
\]
where we used $P_0^* = P_0$ and $\overline{\lambda}^k = \lambda^{-k}$ on
$|\lambda| = 1$. The three groups (constant, negative powers, positive
powers) have disjoint Fourier support, so Parseval gives
\[
  \int_\T \|P+P^*\|_2^2\, d\tau(\lambda)
  \;=\; \|2 P_0\|_2^2 \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} \|P_k\|_2^2 \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} \|P_k^*\|_2^2
  \;=\; 4 \|P_0\|_2^2 \;+\; 2 \sum_{k\ge1} \|P_k\|_2^2
  \;=\; 4 d_\infty^{(P)},
\]
where $d_\infty^{(P)} = \|P_0\|_2^2 + \tfrac12 \sum_{k\ge1} \|P_k\|_2^2$
is the limit of the sequence $d_N^{(P)}$ from Step C.
Combined with \eqref{eq:weighted-dual},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diag-L2}
  \int_\T \|P+P^*\|_2^2\, d\tau \;\le\; 4 a^2 / \gamma^2,
  \qquad
  \int_\T \|Q+Q^*\|_2^2\, d\tau \;\le\; 4 a^2 / \gamma^2.
\end{equation}
The second inequality follows from the second half of \eqref{eq:weighted-dual}
by the same calculation with $Q$ in place of $P$.

\emph{Cross block.} The function $M+L^*$ has Laurent expansion
\[
  (M+L^*)(\lambda) \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} M_k\, \lambda^{-k} \;+\; \sum_{k\ge0} L_k^*\, \lambda^{k}.
\]
The constant coefficient is $M_0 + L_0^* = 2 M_0$, using $L_0^* = M_0$
from \eqref{eq:PQML-0}. Grouping by Fourier mode,
\[
  \int_\T \|M+L^*\|_2^2\, d\tau
  \;=\; \|2 M_0\|_2^2 \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} \|M_k\|_2^2 \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} \|L_k^*\|_2^2.
\]
Since $\|L_k^*\|_2 = \|L_k\|_2$ (the Hilbert-Schmidt norm is invariant
under adjoint) and every term is nonnegative,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross-L2}
  \int_\T \|M+L^*\|_2^2\, d\tau
  \;\ge\; 4 \|M_0\|_2^2 \;+\; \sum_{k\ge1} \|M_k\|_2^2
  \;\ge\; \sum_{k\ge0} \|M_k\|_2^2
  \;=\; a^2.
\end{equation}
The final inequality holds because the coefficient of $4\|M_0\|_2^2$
exceeds the coefficient of $\|M_0\|_2^2$; dropping the $\|L_k\|_2^2$ terms
only makes the left smaller.

\subsection*{Step E. Positive block comparison}

The proof now closes with linear algebra.

From Theorem~\ref{lem:resolvent},
$R(\lambda) + R(\lambda)^* \succeq 0$ for every $\lambda \in \T$.
Compress by the $n \times 2m$ matrix $[X\ Y]$. The $2m \times 2m$ matrix
of blocks
\[
  \Delta(\lambda) \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} P+P^* & L+M^* \\ M+L^* & Q+Q^* \end{pmatrix}(\lambda)
  \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix}^* (R+R^*)(\lambda)\, \begin{pmatrix} X & Y \end{pmatrix}
  \;\succeq\; 0
\]
is positive semidefinite for every $\lambda$. A compression of a positive
semidefinite matrix is positive semidefinite, so this is immediate.

Let $J = \diag(I_m, -I_m)$. If $\Delta \succeq 0$, then $J \Delta J \succeq 0$
too (the conjugation by $J$ keeps positivity). If two matrices $S$
and $T$ are positive semidefinite, then $\tr(S T) \ge 0$: writing
$S = A A^*$ and $T = B B^*$, $\tr(S T) = \tr(A A^* B B^*) = \tr((A^* B)^* (A^* B)) = \|A^* B\|_2^2 \ge 0$.
So
\[
  0 \;\le\; \tr(\Delta (J \Delta J)) \;=\; \tr((\Delta J)^2).
\]
Compute $(\Delta J)^2$ directly. $\Delta J = \begin{pmatrix} P+P^* & -(L+M^*) \\ M+L^* & -(Q+Q^*) \end{pmatrix}$.
Then
\[
  \tr\bigl((\Delta J)^2\bigr)
  \;=\; \tr\bigl((P+P^*)^2\bigr) \;-\; \tr\bigl((L+M^*)(M+L^*)\bigr) \;-\; \tr\bigl((M+L^*)(L+M^*)\bigr) \;+\; \tr\bigl((Q+Q^*)^2\bigr).
\]
Since $\tr((A B)^*) = \overline{\tr(A B)}$ but also $(L+M^*)(M+L^*)$
and its adjoint $(M+L^*)(L+M^*)$ have the same trace (they are adjoints,
and their traces are complex conjugates; both equal $\tr(A A^*)$ with
$A = L+M^*$, which is real nonnegative), the two middle terms sum to
\[
  -\tr\bigl((L+M^*)(M+L^*) + (M+L^*)(L+M^*)\bigr) \;=\; -2 \tr(L+M^*)^*(L+M^*) \;=\; -2 \|M+L^*\|_2^2.
\]
Putting it all together:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-trace}
  0 \;\le\; \|P+P^*\|_2^2 \;+\; \|Q+Q^*\|_2^2 \;-\; 2 \|M+L^*\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
This holds for every $\lambda \in \T$.

Integrate \eqref{eq:block-trace} over $\mu \in \T$, use \eqref{eq:diag-L2}
for the two diagonal terms and \eqref{eq:cross-L2} for the cross term:
\[
  0 \;\le\; 4 a^2/\gamma^2 + 4 a^2/\gamma^2 \;-\; 2 a^2
  \;=\; 8 a^2/\gamma^2 \;-\; 2 a^2.
\]
So
\[
  2 a^2 \;\le\; 8 a^2 / \gamma^2.
\]
Since $a > 0$, divide by $a^2$: $\gamma^2 \le 4$, hence $\gamma \le 2$.

This finishes the proof that $\|F[A]\| \le 2$.
$\square$

\subsection*{Remarks on the argument}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Where the $2$ comes from.} The factor of two in the upper
      coefficient estimate \eqref{eq:coefficient-estimate} is multiplied
      through Step E until it becomes $\gamma^2 = 4$. Removing the factor
      of two would give $\gamma^2 \le 2$, and that is false in general
      (the sharpness example in Section~\ref{sec:limit} has $\gamma = 2$).

\item \emph{Where convexity enters.} The only place convexity is used is
      in the support inequality \eqref{eq:support}, which in turn gives
      the positivity of $K$ (Theorem~\ref{lem:faber}) and the positivity
      of $R+R^*$ (Theorem~\ref{lem:resolvent}). If $\Omega$ were not
      convex, both positivity statements could fail and the argument
      would not go through.

\item \emph{Why scalar proofs do not suffice.} In a scalar argument
      ($m = 1$), one would use $FG = GF$ freely. Here, the two products
      $FG$ and $GF$ are genuinely different $M_m(\C)$-valued polynomials.
      The whole point of Step B is to use these two different products
      separately, obtaining bounds on $x^*G[A]x$ and $y^*G[A]y$ that
      share the same cross-functional $a$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Enclosing domains and the general case}\label{sec:limit}

We have proved $\|F[A]\| \le 2$ under the hypothesis
$W(A) \subset \Omega$ with $\Omega$ an analytic convex domain. We now
remove that hypothesis: for an arbitrary compact convex $W(A)$ we
construct analytic convex neighborhoods $\Omega_j$ shrinking to $W(A)$,
apply the previous section, and pass to the limit.

\subsection*{Analytic convex outer approximation}

\begin{lemma}[Analytic convex outer approximation]\label{lem:approx}
Let $K \subset \C$ be nonempty, compact, and convex. There exist bounded
open convex domains $\Omega_j \subset \C$ with regular real-analytic
Jordan boundaries such that $K \subset \Omega_j$ for every $j$ and
\[
  \sup_{z \in \overline\Omega_j} \dist(z, K) \;\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{}\; 0.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The construction is a smooth convex interpolation between $K$ and a
small thickening. Fix $0 < \delta < 1$. Choose a finite $\delta$-net
$D_\delta$ of unit directions that includes $1, -1, i, -i$. Let
$s_K(\nu) = \max_{z \in K} \Re(\overline\nu z)$ be the support function
of $K$. Choose $t_\delta > 0$ small enough that $|D_\delta| e^{-t_\delta \delta} < 1$.

Define the smooth, strictly convex function
\[
  \Phi_\delta(z) \;=\; \sum_{\nu \in D_\delta} \exp\bigl(t_\delta\, (\Re(\overline\nu z) - s_K(\nu) - \delta)\bigr)
\]
and set $\Omega_\delta = \{z \in \C : \Phi_\delta(z) < 1\}$.

\emph{(i) $K \subset \Omega_\delta$.} For $z \in K$ and any $\nu$,
$\Re(\overline\nu z) \le s_K(\nu)$, so each exponential is at most
$e^{-t_\delta \delta}$, and the sum is at most $|D_\delta| e^{-t_\delta \delta} < 1$.

\emph{(ii) $\overline\Omega_\delta$ lies in a fixed rectangle.} If
$\Phi_\delta(z) \le 1$, then every summand is $\le 1$ (since each is
nonnegative), hence $\Re(\overline\nu z) \le s_K(\nu) + \delta$ for every
$\nu \in D_\delta$. In particular, for $\nu = 1$ and $\nu = -1$, we get
$\Re z$ in $[\min_{w \in K}\Re w - \delta, \max_{w \in K}\Re w + \delta]$;
and similarly the imaginary part. So all $\overline\Omega_\delta$ for
$0 < \delta < 1$ sit inside one fixed bounded rectangle $R \subset \C$.

\emph{(iii) $\Phi_\delta$ is strictly convex with positive definite Hessian.}
The Hessian is a sum of positive multiples of $\nu \nu^{\mathsf T}$ (outer
products), where $\nu$ ranges over $D_\delta$. Each $\nu \nu^{\mathsf T}$ is
positive semidefinite; the four directions $1, -1, i, -i$ together span
$\mathbb R^2$, so the sum is strictly positive definite. Hence $\Phi_\delta$
is strictly convex and real analytic.

\emph{(iv) Level set is regular and a Jordan curve.} If $z_0$ were a
critical point of $\Phi_\delta$ with $\Phi_\delta(z_0) = 1$, then by
convexity $\Phi_\delta$ would attain a global minimum at $z_0$, contradicting
$\Phi_\delta(\text{any } z \in K) < 1$. So $\nabla\Phi_\delta \ne 0$ on
$\Phi_\delta^{-1}(1)$. The implicit function theorem makes that level a
regular real-analytic curve. Pick an interior point $w_0 \in \Omega_\delta$
(any point of $K$ will do). Each ray from $w_0$ crosses $\partial\Omega_\delta$
exactly once, because $\Phi_\delta$ is strictly convex and bounded. Radial
projection gives a continuous bijection $\partial\Omega_\delta \to \mathbf S^1$,
so $\partial\Omega_\delta$ is a Jordan curve and $\overline\Omega_\delta = \{\Phi_\delta \le 1\}$.

\emph{(v) Distance to $K$.} For $z \in \overline\Omega_\delta$, let $w \in K$
be the unique nearest point of $K$ and put $d = |z - w|$. If $z \in K$,
then $d = 0$. Otherwise $d > 0$; set $\nu = (z - w)/d$, a unit vector.
Choose $\mu \in D_\delta$ with $|\mu - \nu| \le \delta$, and choose
$v \in K$ attaining $s_K(\mu) = \Re(\overline\mu v)$.

Using minimality of $w$, for every $w' \in K$ we have
$\Re(\overline\nu(w' - w)) \le 0$ (the real part of the derivative of
$|z - w|^2$ along $w + t(w' - w)$ at $t = 0$). In particular
$\Re(\overline\nu(v - w)) \le 0$ and $\Re(\overline\mu(w - v)) \le 0$.

Now compute:
\begin{itemize}
\item Since $|\mu - \nu| \le \delta$, $\Re(\overline\mu \nu) \ge 1 - \delta$
      and hence $\Re(\overline\mu(z - w)) = d \Re(\overline\mu \nu) \ge (1 - \delta)\, d$.
\item Since $\Re(\overline\mu(v - w)) \ge 0$ (support inequality, since
      $v$ maximizes $\Re(\overline\mu \cdot)$ on $K$) and since
      $\Phi_\delta(z) \le 1$ forces $\Re(\overline\mu z) \le \Re(\overline\mu v) + \delta$,
      we get $\Re(\overline\mu(z - v)) \le \delta$.
\item From $\Re(\overline\nu(v - w)) \le 0$, together with $|\mu - \nu| \le \delta$,
      we get $\Re(\overline\mu(v - w)) \le \delta |v - w|$.
\end{itemize}
Hence
\[
  (1 - \delta)\, d
  \;\le\; \Re(\overline\mu(z - w))
  \;=\; \Re(\overline\mu(z - v)) + \Re(\overline\mu(v - w))
  \;\le\; \delta + \delta\, |v - w|
  \;\le\; \delta(1 + \operatorname{diam}K).
\]
Points of $K$ have distance zero, so
\[
  \sup_{z \in \overline\Omega_\delta} \dist(z, K)
  \;\le\; \frac{\delta(1 + \operatorname{diam}K)}{1 - \delta}.
\]
Taking $\delta = \delta_j = 1/(j+1)$ and $\Omega_j = \Omega_{\delta_j}$ completes
the construction.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of the main theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
First recall the Toeplitz--Hausdorff convexity theorem
\cite{Toeplitz1918,Hausdorff1919}: $W(A)$ is convex.

\emph{Compactly.} $W(A) = \{u^* A u : u^*u = 1\}$ is the image of the unit
sphere (compact) under a continuous map, hence is compact.

\emph{Convexly.} Take any $u, v \in \C^n$ with $|u| = |v| = 1$. We want
to show that the segment $[u^*Au, v^*Av]$ lies in $W(A)$. Let
$V = \mathrm{span}\{u, v\}$, of complex dimension 1 or 2.

If $\dim V = 1$, then $v = e^{i\theta} u$ and $v^*Av = u^*Au$, so the segment is trivial.

If $\dim V = 2$, work in an orthonormal basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$ of $V$, and
let $B$ be the $2\times 2$ matrix of $P_V A|_{V}$ in this basis. For a
unit vector $x = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2$, writing
$X = 2\Re(\overline{x_1} x_2)$, $Y = 2\Im(\overline{x_1} x_2)$, $Z = |x_1|^2 - |x_2|^2$,
one has $X^2 + Y^2 + Z^2 = 1$ (because $4 |x_1 x_2|^2 + (|x_1|^2 - |x_2|^2)^2 = (|x_1|^2 + |x_2|^2)^2 = 1$),
so $(X, Y, Z)$ ranges over the unit sphere $\mathbf S^2 \subset \mathbb R^3$,
and $x^* B x$ is a real-linear function $f(X, Y, Z)$ into $\mathbf C \cong \mathbb R^2$.
The linear part of $f$ has a one-dimensional kernel. Every point of the
unit ball can be pushed along the kernel direction onto the sphere without
changing $f$, so $f(\mathbf S^2) = f(\overline{\mathbb B^3})$. The image of
a ball under a linear map is convex, so $f(\mathbf S^2)$ is convex. In
particular, the segment $[u^*Au, v^*Av] \subset f(\mathbf S^2) \subset W(A)$.

So $W(A)$ is compact and convex.

\emph{Enclosing.} Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:approx} with $K = W(A)$. Get
analytic convex domains $\Omega_j$ with $W(A) \subset \Omega_j$ and
$\sup_{\overline\Omega_j}\dist(\cdot, W(A)) \to 0$. In particular every
$\overline\Omega_j$ lies inside the fixed bounded rectangle from Lemma~\ref{lem:approx}$(ii)$.

\emph{Uniform bound.} Fix one $\Omega_j$. Let
$M_j = \sup_{z \in \overline\Omega_j} \|P(z)\|$. Two cases.

If $M_j = 0$, then $P$ vanishes on $\Omega_j$ (open), so $P$ is the zero
polynomial and $P[A] = 0$.

If $M_j > 0$, multiply $P$ by $1/M_j$. The normalized polynomial
satisfies $\|P/M_j\| \le 1$ on $\partial\Omega_j$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:domain}
applies and gives $\|(P/M_j)[A]\| \le 2$, i.e., $\|P[A]\| \le 2 M_j$.

In either case, $\|P[A]\| \le 2 M_j$.

\emph{Shrinking.} Since $\overline\Omega_j$ converges to $W(A)$ in
Hausdorff distance and all $\overline\Omega_j$ lie in a fixed compact set,
uniform continuity of the continuous function $z \mapsto \|P(z)\|$ implies
$M_j \to \sup_{z \in W(A)} \|P(z)\| = \max_{W(A)} \|P\|$. Passing to
the limit in $\|P[A]\| \le 2 M_j$,
\[
  \|P[A]\| \;\le\; 2 \max_{z \in W(A)} \|P(z)\|.
\]
This is exactly \eqref{eq:main}.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Sharpness of the constant $2$}

\begin{proof}[Sharpness in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Take $n = 2$, $m = 1$, $d = 1$, $P(z) = z$, and
$A = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 2\\0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. We already saw in
the Introduction that $W(A)$ is the closed unit disk, and that
$\|A\| = 2$; so $\|P[A]\| = 2$ and $\max_{W(A)}|P(z)| = 1$. The ratio is
\[
  \frac{\|P[A]\|}{\max_{W(A)} \|P(z)\|} \;=\; \frac{2}{1} \;=\; 2.
\]
Hence no smaller constant works uniformly in $n, m, d$.
\end{proof}

The constant $2$ is achieved by a very asymmetric matrix: $A$ is
$2$-step nilpotent, $W(A)$ is a disk (not the spectrum), and the ratio
$\|A\|/\max_{W(A)}|z|$ is exactly $2$. This is why the numerical range,
not the spectrum, is the right set: the spectrum of $A$ is $\{0\}$, and
no bound $\|P[A]\| \le C \max_{\sigma(A)} |P|$ would hold for any finite $C$.

\section{The exterior conformal collar}\label{sec:collar}

This appendix supplies the classical conformal input used in
Section~\ref{sec:coords}. Everything here is covered by
Ahlfors~\cite{Ahlfors1979}; we spell out the two ingredients that are
rarely stated explicitly: real-analytic boundary continuation and global
injectivity on a small exterior collar.

\begin{lemma}[Exterior coordinate and a univalent collar]\label{lem:exterior-collar}
Let $\Omega \subset \C$ be a bounded convex domain with regular
real-analytic Jordan boundary. Then there are $0 < r_0 < 1$ and a map
$h$ which is meromorphic and one-to-one on
$\{\lambda : |\lambda| > r_0\} \cup \{\infty\}$, has a simple pole at
$\infty$, and maps $|\lambda| > 1$ conformally onto
$\C \setminus \overline\Omega$, and $|\lambda| = 1$ onto
$\partial\Omega$. The expansion at infinity is
\[
  h(\lambda) \;=\; c\lambda + c_0 + \sum_{j \ge 1} c_j \lambda^{-j}, \qquad c \ne 0.
\]
For $\lambda$ on the unit circle, $q(\lambda) = \lambda h'(\lambda)$ is a
nonzero outward normal vector at $h(\lambda)$, and the support inequality
\eqref{eq:support} holds.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: A bounded simply connected model.}
Choose a point $z_* \in \Omega$ and apply $z \mapsto 1/(z - z_*)$ (with
$\infty$ mapped to $0$). The spherical exterior
$(\C \setminus \overline\Omega) \cup \{\infty\}$ maps onto a bounded
simply connected domain $U$ containing $0$. Because $\Omega$ is convex,
each ray from $z_*$ meets $\partial\Omega$ in a unique point; after
inversion, the exterior portions of those rays become a domain that is
star-shaped about~$0$. Its boundary is a regular real-analytic Jordan
curve: the inversion of a regular real-analytic curve is again one.

\emph{Step 2: Riemann mapping.}
By the Riemann mapping theorem
\cite[Ch.~6, \S1.1, Thm.~1]{Ahlfors1979}, there is a conformal bijection
$g : \D \to U$ with $g(0) = 0$. By Carath\'eodory's Jordan-domain theorem
\cite[Ch.~6, \S1.2]{Ahlfors1979}, $g$ extends to a homeomorphism
$\overline\D \to \overline U$.

\emph{Step 3: Local real-analytic continuation across the boundary.}
A regular real-analytic boundary arc has a real-analytic
parametrisation with nonzero derivative. Its holomorphic continuation
and the holomorphic inverse function theorem give local coordinates
in which the arc is a real interval and the domain is one side of that
interval. Fix such coordinates at a boundary point $\zeta \in \partial\D$
and its image $p = g(\zeta) \in \partial U$. Continuity of the boundary
extension allows shrinking the source neighborhood so that $g$ maps
its intersection with $\overline\D$ into the target chart. In these
coordinates, $g$ is holomorphic on a half-disk, continuous on the real
interval, and real-valued there. The Schwarz reflection principle
\cite[Ch.~4, \S6.5; Ch.~6, \S\S1.3--1.4]{Ahlfors1979} extends $g$
holomorphically across the interval. Apply the same procedure to
$g^{-1}$. Shrinking neighborhoods allows composing the reflected maps.
Their compositions equal the identity on the original domain side, and
hence throughout a smaller neighborhood by the identity theorem. So the
continued $g$ has nonzero derivative at every boundary point.

\emph{Step 4: Patching local continuations.}
For each $\zeta \in \partial\D$, choose a disk $B(\zeta, R_\zeta)$ on
which such a continuation $\tilde g_\zeta$ exists, agreeing with $g$ on
$B(\zeta, R_\zeta) \cap \D$. Cover the compact circle by finitely many
smaller disks $V_i = B(\zeta_i, R_i/3)$; write $U_i = B(\zeta_i, R_i)$.
If $V_i \cap V_j \ne \varnothing$ and $R_i \ge R_j$, then
$|\zeta_i - \zeta_j| < (R_i + R_j)/3 \le 2 R_i/3 < R_i$, so the two
larger disks overlap in a connected lens $U_i \cap U_j$ that contains
$\zeta_j$ and meets $\D$. On $\D \cap U_i \cap U_j$, both $\tilde g_i$
and $\tilde g_j$ agree with $g$, hence with each other. By the identity
theorem they agree on the whole lens $U_i \cap U_j$. So the local
extensions patch with $g$ to a single holomorphic function on
$\D \cup \bigcup_i V_i$, which is a neighborhood of the closed disk.

\emph{Step 5: Global injectivity on a small neighborhood.}
The extension is injective on a (possibly smaller) neighborhood of the
closed disk. Otherwise there would be sequences $(w_n), (w_n')$ with
$w_n \ne w_n'$, $w_n, w_n' \to$ (a neighborhood of closed disk), and
$\tilde g(w_n) = \tilde g(w_n')$. Passing to subsequences inside the
compact closed disk, both go to a common limit $w^*$ in the closed disk.
Since $\tilde g$ is continuous and injective on the closed disk
($g$ is a homeomorphism on $\overline\D$), and $\tilde g(w_n) = \tilde g(w_n')$,
we must eventually have $w_n, w_n'$ in a common small disk about $w^*$
on which $\tilde g$ is injective, contradiction. By compactness, the
(injective) neighborhood is open and contains a disk $\{w : |w| < R_0\}$
for some $R_0 > 1$.

\emph{Step 6: Assembly.}
The function $g$ is univalent on $\{w : |w| < R_0\} \supset \overline\D$.
Its only zero there is $0$, and that zero is simple ($g'(0) \ne 0$ by
conformality). Set
\[
  h(\lambda) \;=\; z_* + \frac{1}{g(1/\lambda)}, \qquad r_0 \;=\; R_0^{-1}.
\]
Then $h$ is meromorphic on $\{|\lambda| > r_0\} \cup \{\infty\}$,
has a simple pole at $\infty$ (from the simple zero of $g$ at $0$), maps
$|\lambda| > 1$ conformally onto $\C \setminus \overline\Omega$ (from
$g$ mapping $\D$ to $U$ and $z \mapsto z_* + 1/z$ undoing the inversion),
and maps $|\lambda| = 1$ onto $\partial\Omega$. The expansion
$h(\lambda) = c\lambda + c_0 + O(1/\lambda)$ has $c = 1/g'(0) \ne 0$.

\emph{Step 7: The support inequality.}
At $\lambda = e^{i\theta} \in \T$, the radial and angular derivatives of
$h$ are $q(\lambda) = \lambda h'(\lambda)$ and $i q(\lambda)$, respectively.
They are perpendicular and nonzero. Increasing the radial parameter
$|\lambda|$ traces out the exterior of $\overline\Omega$, so $q(\lambda)$
points outward. Since $\Omega$ is convex with a smooth boundary, the
supporting-line property holds at $h(\lambda)$: for every $z \in \overline\Omega$,
\[
  \Re\!\left( \overline{q(\lambda)} \bigl( h(\lambda) - z \bigr) \right) \;\ge\; 0.
\]
This is exactly \eqref{eq:support}.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Sanity check for the disk}

For $\Omega = \D$ (the unit disk), the exterior coordinate is
$h(\lambda) = \lambda$. Then $q(\lambda) = \lambda$, $b_k(z) = z^k$,
and $s(\lambda,\mu) = 0$. The kernel $K$ is $1$, and the marginal
identities are trivial. The support inequality reads
$\Re(\overline\lambda(\lambda - z)) = 1 - \Re(\overline\lambda z) \ge 0$
for $|z| \le 1$, $|\lambda| = 1$, which is just Cauchy--Schwarz.
