1. THE PUZZLE
================================================================

Imagine n fair coins.  Each coin is +1 (heads) or -1 (tails), equally
likely.  The n coins together form a random point

    X = (X_1, X_2, ..., X_n)  in  {-1, +1}^n,

called the discrete cube (also hypercube, or Ising cube).  All 2^n
points are equally likely.

You want to compress all n coins to one bit of summary.  Call your
summary U = +1 or -1.  You are allowed to choose U by any rule you
like based on X, even a random rule.  Formally, you choose any soft
function

    u : {-1, +1}^n  -->  [-1, +1],

and let U have conditional mean E[U | X = x] = u(x).  The value u(x)=0
means "totally undecided".  A Boolean summary is the special case
where u(x) is always +1 or -1.

Now every coin passes through its own noisy channel.  Coin i is
flipped with some probability, independently across coins.  Write the
received vector as Y = (Y_1, ..., Y_n).  The correlation parameter
rho in [-1, +1] says how faithful the channel is:

    rho =  1:  Y = X               (perfect)
    rho =  0:  Y independent of X  (pure noise)
    rho = -1:  Y = -X              (always flipped)

For 0 < rho < 1 you can think of the channel as "each coin is silently
re-tossed with probability eps = (1 - rho)/2".

Question.  For a given noise level rho, which U retains the most
information about Y?

The Courtade-Kumar conjecture (2013-2014) says: a single coin is
best.  U = X_i.  No clever combination of coins beats it.  The
intuition is "democracies do not help": mixing many coins dilutes.

This paper proves a much stronger statement.  Allow U to be soft
(any u : {-1, +1}^n -> [-1, +1]), fix the information I(U; X) that
U carries about X, and ask: what is the maximum possible I(U; Y)?
The answer is exactly the curve that a soft coordinate u(x) = a x_i
achieves.  So single coins are optimal not just among Boolean
summaries, but among all soft summaries at every information level.

We call the theorem "sharp" because the bound I(U; Y) <= (that curve)
is attained exactly by a coordinate.  There is no loss.

    -----------------------------  schematic picture

    X ---- (noisy channel) ----> Y
    |
    v
    U   (your one-bit summary)

We want to upper-bound I(U; Y) in terms of I(U; X) and rho.


2. PROBABILITY AND INFORMATION
================================================================

2.1  Entropy: how much uncertainty?

Entropy measures the uncertainty of a random variable.  It is the
average number of yes/no questions needed to pin the variable down.
Units in this paper: nats (natural log).  One bit of uncertainty
equals log 2 = 0.693 nats.  We write

    L = log 2.

For a single sign S with probability distribution

    P(S = +1) = (1+r)/2,    P(S = -1) = (1-r)/2,

with mean r = E S in [-1, +1], the entropy is

    H(r) = -(1+r)/2 log((1+r)/2) - (1-r)/2 log((1-r)/2).

Useful facts (all use L = log 2):

    H(1) = 0,    H(0) = L,    H(-1) = 0,    H is even.

As |r| shrinks toward 0, H(r) grows toward L.  At r=0 the sign is
maximally uncertain (L nats of uncertainty).  At |r|=1 it is fully
determined (0 nats of uncertainty).  H is concave in r.

For independent signs, entropy adds:

    H(S_1, S_2, ..., S_n) = H(S_1) + H(S_2) + ... + H(S_n).

2.2  The companion psi: information above zero

Define

    psi(r) = L - H(r).

Then psi(0) = 0 (no information) and psi(1) = L (full information
about a sign).  psi measures "how much you know" rather than "how
uncertain you are".  Its Taylor series is

    psi(r) = r^2/2 + r^4/12 + r^6/30 + r^8/56 + ...

so psi(r) is roughly r^2/2 for small r.  Two bounds we will use:

    (1/2) r^2  <=  psi(r)  <=  L r^2,     for -1 <= r <= 1.

The derivatives come out neatly:

    psi'(r)  = V(r)  := atanh(r) = (1/2) log((1+r)/(1-r)),

    psi''(r) = 1/(1 - r^2).

The function V(r) is the inverse hyperbolic tangent, also called the
log-odds or Fisher z-transform.  It blows up as r -> 1.

2.3  Mutual information: how much U knows about X

For a random pair (U, X), the mutual information is

    I(U; X) = H(U) - H(U | X).

Roughly: how many nats of uncertainty about U are resolved by seeing
X.  It is symmetric in U and X, lies in [0, min(H(U), H(X))], and is
zero exactly when U and X are independent.

In this paper U is a sign with conditional means.  If U has
conditional mean E[U | X] = u(X) for a soft function
u: {-1, +1}^n -> [-1, +1], then

    I(U; X) = H(E u(X)) - E H(u(X))
           = E psi(u(X)) - psi(E u(X)).

Define

    I(u) := E psi(u(X)) - psi(E u(X)).

So I(u) = I(U;X).  Useful checks:

    If u is constant,              I(u) = 0.
    If u(x) = r x_i (coordinate),  I(u) = psi(r).
    If u is Boolean,               I(u) = H(E u).

2.4  The noise correlation parameter rho

For each coin i, the received B_i = X_i Z_i where Z_i is a fresh sign
independent of X and of the other Z_j, with mean E Z_i = rho.
Equivalently:

    B_i = X_i     with probability (1+rho)/2,
    B_i = -X_i    with probability (1-rho)/2.

So rho measures how faithfully the channel transmits.  The paper
works directly with rho in [-1, +1], including negative rho (the
always-flip-more-than-half-wrong channel).  Crossover probability
eps in [0, 1/2] corresponds to rho = 1 - 2 eps.


3. THE DISCRETE CUBE AND THE NOISE OPERATOR
================================================================

3.1  The Walsh characters

For each subset A of {1, ..., n}, define a function

    chi_A(x) = product over i in A of x_i.

These chi_A are called Walsh characters.  Facts:

    chi_A(x) is +1 or -1.
    chi_empty(x) = 1  (constant).
    chi_{i}(x)   = x_i  (single coordinate).
    chi_A(x) * chi_B(x) = chi_{A xor B}(x).
    chi_A(x) * chi_A(x) = 1.

They are orthonormal under the uniform measure:

    E[chi_A(X) chi_B(X)] = 1 if A = B, else 0.

So they form an orthonormal basis of functions on the cube.  Every
f: {-1, +1}^n -> R has a unique expansion

    f(x) = sum over A of f_hat(A) chi_A(x),
    f_hat(A) = E[f(X) chi_A(X)].

This is the Walsh expansion.  It is the tool that turns "noise" into
"multiply each component by rho^{|A|}".

3.2  The noise operator T_rho

The noise operator maps a soft function u to another soft function
according to

    T_rho u(y) = E[u(X) | Y = y],

where Y is the noisy version of X described in Section 2.4.  It is
the best estimate of u(X) from Y alone.

Because noise acts independently on each coordinate, we can compute
what T_rho does to a Walsh character:

    T_rho chi_A = rho^{|A|} chi_A,    |A| = size of A.

In particular:

    T_rho 1     = 1
    T_rho chi_i = rho chi_i
    T_rho (chi_i chi_j) = rho^2 chi_i chi_j    for i != j
    ...

So T_rho "shrinks" each Walsh coefficient by rho^{|A|}: large subsets
get killed quickly, small subsets survive.

3.3  Information carried by a soft function

The important identity is

    I(T_rho u) = I(U; Y).

Why?  Because T_rho u(Y) = E[U | Y] is the conditional mean of U
given Y, so I(U; Y) = E psi(T_rho u) - psi(E T_rho u) by the same
information formula, with Y as the conditioning variable.  This is
the quantity we are trying to bound.

3.4  The continuous semigroup P_t

Instead of thinking of correlations rho one at a time, the proof uses
a continuous time parameter.  Define

    P_t = T_{e^{-t}}     for t >= 0.

Since e^{-t} decreases continuously from 1 (t=0) to 0 (t=infinity),
P_t smoothly interpolates between no noise (t=0) and pure noise
(t=infinity, operator becomes constant).

It is a semigroup: P_{s+t} = P_s P_t.  Its generator is

    N = (1/2) sum_{i=1}^n (Id - flip_i),

where flip_i(x) flips coordinate i.  On a Walsh character:

    flip_i chi_A = chi_A         if i not in A,
    flip_i chi_A = -chi_A        if i in A.

So (Id - flip_i) chi_A = 0 if i not in A, and = 2 chi_A if i in A.
Averaging over i gives

    N chi_A = |A| chi_A,

and hence P_t chi_A = e^{-t|A|} chi_A, matching T_rho with
rho = e^{-t}.  The operator N is nonnegative (each |A| >= 0), so
P_t is a contraction of information over time: the noise eats it
away.


4. WHAT "CONTRACTION" MEANS
================================================================

The word "contraction" has a precise meaning: an information quantity
shrinks over time.

Fix a soft function u.  Evolve it under noise:

    u_t(x) = P_t u(x) = T_{e^{-t}} u(x).

Consider the information I(u_t).  As t increases, u_t loses
information about X; the signal fades.  The paper shows that I(u_t)
shrinks at least as fast as a single noisy coordinate with the same
initial information.

To get a flow, define the instantaneous information loss:

    D(u) = - d/dt [ I(P_t u) ]   evaluated at t=0.

Direct differentiation of I(u_t) = E psi(P_t u) - psi(E P_t u),
using the identities psi'(r) = V(r) and P_t = e^{-tN} together with
differentiation of P_t at t=0 (which equals -N), yields:

    D(u) = E[ V(u(X)) N u(X) ].

This is the "Dirichlet form" of u.  The flow identity is

    d/dt I(P_t u) = - D(P_t u)     for every t >= 0.

Now: suppose we can prove

    D(u) >= F(I(u))     for all soft u,

for some increasing scalar function F: [0, L) -> [0, +infinity).
Then the flow gives the differential inequality

    d/dt I(P_t u) <= - F( I(P_t u) ).

Integrating this 1D ODE gives an explicit upper bound on I(P_t u).
The bound is exactly the "sharp contraction" of the title.

The ideal "comparison rate" comes from a single coordinate.  Take
u(x) = r x_i with 0 <= r <= 1.  Then

    I(u)   = psi(r),
    N u(x) = r  (since N x_i = x_i),
    D(u)   = E[ V(r x_i) * r x_i ] = r V(r),

using V(-rx_i)(-rx_i) = V(rx_i)(rx_i).  So the coordinate achieves

    F(psi(r)) = r V(r),   for 0 <= r < 1.

The monotone function F on [0, L) is thus pinned by

    F(psi(r)) = r V(r)         for 0 <= r < 1,

with F extended continuously to F(0) = 0.

The core question: can we show D(u) >= F(I(u)) for EVERY soft u,
not just coordinates?  If yes, the theorem follows.  And can we do
better when the mean E u(X) is not zero?  Yes (Sections 13-15):
the "reserve" profile B_m dominates F for nonzero mean.

    u_t = P_t u           <--- evolve under noise
      |
      v
    I(u_t) decreases:  dI/dt = -D
      |
      v
    want D >= F(I)        (compare against a single coordinate)
      |
      v
    integrate ODE  --->  bound I(P_t u) <= psi(e^{-t} psi^{-1}(I(u)))


5. THE MAIN THEOREM
================================================================

Theorem 5.1  (Sharp soft-channel contraction).
------------------------------------------------
For every integer n >= 0, every soft function
u : {-1, +1}^n -> [-1, +1], and every correlation rho in [-1, +1],

    I(T_rho u)   <=   psi( |rho| * psi^{-1}( I(u) ) ).       (*)

Here psi^{-1}: [0, L] -> [0, 1] is the inverse of psi on [0, 1].
Equality is attained by every soft coordinate u(x) = a x_i with
|a| <= 1, on any coordinate i.
------------------------------------------------

Remarks.

(a)  Two channels, one curve.  The left side is the information the
     noisy observation Y carries about the summary U.  The right
     side is what a single soft coordinate with the same starting
     information I(u) would carry through the same noise level |rho|.
     No soft summary retains more.

(b)  The parameter r = psi^{-1}(I(u)) is the "effective amplitude".
     It is the amplitude of a soft coordinate carrying the same
     information as u.  The theorem says the effective amplitude
     contracts at least as fast as a coordinate.

(c)  |rho| vs rho.  The identity T_{-rho} u(x) = T_rho u(-x) and the
     sign-flip symmetry of the cube show that information depends on
     rho only through |rho|.  So we can assume |rho| >= 0.

(d)  Boundary cases.
        rho =  1: T_1 u = u; the inequality is an identity.
        rho =  0: T_0 u = E u (constant); I(T_0 u) = 0; the right
                  side is psi(0) = 0; trivially true.
        rho = -1: same as rho=1 with the sign-flip trick.
        u constant: I(u) = 0; both sides vanish.

(e)  Sharpness.  Every soft coordinate u(x) = a x_i attains equality.
     So the right side is not only an upper bound; it is the exact
     optimum at every information level.

Proof plan for Theorem 5.1.

    step 1  (production): D(u) >= F(I(u)) for all soft u.
    step 2  (integrate): along P_t, dI/dt <= -F(I); integrate.
    step 3  (substitute): rho = e^{-t}.

Steps 2 and 3 are mechanical.  Step 1 (Sections 9-16) is the deep
work.  A stronger version (Theorem 15.1) also produces the
mean-dependent profile B_m(I) and shows D(u) >= B_m(I(u)) >= F(I(u)).


6. THE COURTADE-KUMAR COROLLARY
================================================================

Specialize to Boolean f : {-1, +1}^n -> {-1, +1}.  With no hedging:

    I(f) = H(E f) =: H(m),   m = E f in [-1, +1].

The maximum is L (fair case m=0); the minimum is 0 (constant f).

Corollary 6.1  (Boolean bound, with output bias).
------------------------------------------------
For every n >= 0, rho in [-1, +1], and Boolean
f : {-1, +1}^n -> {-1, +1} with m = E f, define

    r_m := psi^{-1}( H(m) ).        ("effective amplitude of f")

Then

    I(f(X); Y)  <=  psi( |rho| r_m )  <=  psi( |rho| ).       (**)

Both inequalities are exact equalities (i.e. equality in both) for
signed coordinate functions f(x) = +- x_i with m=0, at every rho.
If 0 < |m| < 1 and 0 < |rho| < 1, the refined curve psi(|rho| r_m)
is strictly below the universal curve psi(|rho|).
------------------------------------------------

Reading (**).  The right inequality

    I(f(X); Y)  <=  psi( |rho| )

is the universal Courtade-Kumar bound.  psi(1) = L, so at |rho|=1
the bound is "one full bit".  In words:

    A Boolean summary of a uniform input, under independent
    bit-flip noise with correlation rho, carries at most psi(|rho|)
    nats of information about the input.

Convert to bits.  Since psi(|rho|)/L = 1 - H(|rho|)/L and the binary
entropy h_2(p) = -p log_2(p) - (1-p) log_2(1-p) satisfies
h_2(eps) = H(|rho|)/L with eps = (1-|rho|)/2, we get

    I_2( f(X); Y )  <=  1 - h_2(eps).

This is exactly the Courtade-Kumar conjecture, in its original form.

The left inequality in (**) refines the universal bound using the
output bias m.  For a biased f (0 < |m| < 1), the effective amplitude
r_m is strictly less than 1, so psi(|rho| r_m) < psi(|rho|): the
information of a noisy biased Boolean function is strictly below the
universal curve.

Why r_m = psi^{-1}(H(m))?  A soft coordinate r x_i with r = r_m
carries exactly H(m) nats, matching f.  Theorem 5.1 then bounds
I(f(X);Y) by psi(|rho| r_m), the exact coordinate-curve value at that
information level.  So f is bounded by the best possible coordinate
of matching information, and by nothing less.

The corollary holds at every rho and every n.  No asymptotics.

    rho    psi(rho)   [nats]   psi(rho)/L [bits]   eps  1-h_2(eps)
    ----   ----------------   ----------------   ----  ----------
    1.00   0.693147 (L)       1.000000           0.000 1.000000
    0.90   0.494632           0.713521           0.050 0.713603

The last two columns agree (as they must) because the substitution

7. SHARPNESS
================================================================

The theorem says coordinates achieve equality.  This section collects
concrete checks.

7.1  A single soft coordinate.

Let u(x) = r x_1 on a cube of any dimension n >= 1, with 0 <= r <= 1.
Then:

    I(u)             = psi(r)
    I(T_rho u)       = psi(|rho| r)
    D(u)             = r V(r)
    F(I(u)) = F(psi(r)) = r V(r)

So D = F is tight for every coordinate.  The main theorem 5.1
becomes psi(|rho| r) <= psi(|rho| psi^{-1}(psi(r))) = psi(|rho| r),
an identity.

7.2  Non-coordinate summaries lose.

Take n=3 and f(x) = sign(x_1 + x_2 + x_3), the majority of 3 votes.
It is Boolean, m = E f = 0 (symmetric), so H(m) = L.

A direct computation gives I(f(X); Y) strictly less than psi(|rho|)
for every 0 < rho < 1.  We do not need the exact number here.  At
rho = 0.90, for example, the universal CK bound is 0.4946 nats, and
majority's value sits strictly below it.  The gap widens at moderate
noise.  The coordinate summary x_1 attains exactly the bound.

7.3  Reading the name "Courtade-Kumar".

The conjecture was originally posed as: which Boolean f maximizes
I(f(X); Y)?  Courtade and Kumar's answer: a signed coordinate
f(x) = +- x_i, m = 0.  Earlier work proved this in high-noise and
low-noise ranges, piecewise.  This paper proves the full conjecture,
at every rho and n, in fact in the stronger soft form where the
input summary U is permitted to be soft.


8. WHAT MAKES THE PROOF HARD
================================================================

We have reduced everything to a single inequality:

    D(u)  >=  F(I(u))     for every soft u : {-1,+1}^n -> [-1,+1].

If we can prove this at every dimension, Theorem 5.1 follows.  This
is where all the difficulty is.

8.1  It is dimension-free.

The rate F depends on the scalar I(u) only.  It must control D(u) for
fields of arbitrarily high dimension.  This is strong.

The linear "D(u) >= 2 I(u)" form is closed in the classical cube
logarithmic Sobolev inequality.  The hard part is the surplus:
F(I) - 2I > 0 for I > 0.  This nonlinear surplus is what needs a new
argument.

8.2  Split the cube along one coordinate.

Fix a soft u.  Split into two halves:

    u_+ = u restricted to the half where x_n = +1,
    u_- = u restricted to the half where x_n = -1.

Both u_+, u_- are soft functions on {-1, +1}^{n-1}.  Their averages are

    m_+ := E u_+,    m_- := E u_-.

The parent average is m := E u = (m_+ + m_-)/2.  In general m_+ != m_-.
Also record the average conditional entropies e_+ := E H(u_+) and
e_- := E H(u_-), and the child information values
I_+ := H(m_+) - e_+ and I_- := H(m_-) - e_-.

The generator N splits into "acts on the other n-1 coordinates" plus
"flips x_n".  The flip part produces a joining cost:

    c(a, b) = (a-b)/4 * ( V(a) - V(b) ),

for scalars a, b in [-1, +1].  Properties:

    c(a, a) = 0 (no cost when they agree),
    c(a, b) > 0 for a != b,
    c(1, 1) = c(-1, -1) = 0  (extend c by zero at these corners),
    every other boundary pair  ->  +infinity
    (the minimizer never puts mass there).

The exact energy split is

    D(u) = [D(u_+) + D(u_-)] / 2  +  E[ c(u_+(X'), u_-(X')) ]

where X' = (X_1, ..., X_{n-1}) is the remaining cube.

Interpretation.  D of the parent equals the average D of the two
children PLUS the cost of joining them along the last coordinate.
The children live on a lower-dimensional cube; the joining cost is
where the surplus F - 2I comes from.

8.3  The induction strategy.

Induction on n.

    Base case n=0: u is a constant on the single point; trivial.

    Inductive step: assume child inequalities
        D(u_+) >= B_hat_{m_+}(I_+)   and   D(u_-) >= B_hat_{m_-}(I_-).
    Then
        D(u) >= [B_hat_{m_+}(I_+) + B_hat_{m_-}(I_-)] / 2
              + E[ c(u_+, u_-) ].

    Set j = (I_+ + I_-)/2.  By concavity of H, the average of H(m_+)
    and H(m_-) is at most H(m), so j <= I(u).  Both lie in [0, L], and
    F is increasing on [0, L), so F(j) <= F(I(u)).  The joining cost
    must cover the gap

        B_hat_m(I(u)) - [B_hat_{m_+}(I_+) + B_hat_{m_-}(I_-)] / 2.

So the problem reduces to a "local joining" question: over all coupled
pairs (u_+, u_-) with fixed means m_+ = m + delta, m_- = m - delta,
and fixed average entropy e = (e_+ + e_-)/2, what is the minimum
joining cost E c(u_+, u_-)?  This is candidate for a three-point
minimizer (Section 11).

8.4  Why the obvious approach fails.

The linear rate "D >= 2I" is classical.  Hope: prove that linear
rate, then upgrade the constant "2" to the function F(I) via a local
comparison.  This fails for a concrete reason.  Chen, Gohari and
Nair (2025) noted that the local joining inequality for the profile
F alone can FAIL: there are coupled pairs (u_+, u_-) with the same
child profiles where E c(u_+, u_-) is LESS than the required
F(I(u)) - average[F(I_+), F(I_-)] gap.  So the naive upgrade cannot
work at every state.

The fix is to strengthen the comparison.  Introduce a mean-dependent
envelope

    B_m(I) = 2I + (1 - m^2) S( L - (H(m) - I)/(1 - m^2) ),    m = E u,

with S(I) := F(I) - 2I.  The factor (1 - m^2) is a variance weight:
the average of the two child variance factors at m +/- delta is
1 - m^2 - delta^2, so pooling their surplus terms uses precisely this
factor.  Remarkably, B_m satisfies the local joining inequality.
Then take B_hat_m := max{B_m, F}, the "hybrid" profile.  Section 15
proves B_hat does what F alone could not.

8.5  What the surplus is.

    F(I) = 2I + S(I),    S(I) = F(I) - 2I,    S(0) = 0.

Facts about S:

    S(I) > 0 for I > 0,
    S(0) = S'(0) = 0   (S(I) ~ (2/3) I^2 near 0, from F''(0) = 4/3),
    S is convex on (0, L),   S' >= 0,   S'' > 0.

Numeric: using S(I) = (2/3) I^2 + O(I^3):

    I = 0.00    S(0.00) = 0
    I = 0.10    S(0.10) around 0.0069
    I = 0.20    S(0.20) around 0.0285
    I = L/2     S(L/2)  around 0.086

As I tends to L from below, psi^{-1}(I) -> 1, so F(I) = r V(r) blows
up and S(I) blows up.  S is finite on [0, L) and diverges as I -> L.

The mean-dependent profile B_m sidesteps this divergence.  Its
surplus term is S( L - (H(m) - I)/(1 - m^2) ), evaluated at an
argument that stays strictly below L even at I = H(m).  The "1 - m^2"
factor is a variance weight that sets where the effective entropy
ceiling sits.


9. SCALAR FACTS ABOUT THE RATE FUNCTION F
================================================================

The production rate F is the smallest object worth studying.  Here
is everything the paper needs to know about it, and about S and V.

9.1  Notation reminder.

    L = log 2                     (~0.693147)
    H(r) = binary entropy in nats, of a sign of mean r
    psi(r) = L - H(r)             (information of a sign of mean r)
    V(r) = atanh(r) = psi'(r)     (log-odds, grows as r -> 1)
    F: [0, L) -> [0, +infinity)   (the production rate)
    S(I) = F(I) - 2I              (surplus over the linear rate)

with

    F(psi(r)) = r V(r)            for 0 <= r < 1.

9.2  Convexity and derivatives of F.

    F(0) = 0,
    F'(0) = 2,
    F''(0) = 4/3,
    F''(I) >= 4/3     for all I in [0, L),
    F'''(I) >= 0      for all I in [0, L).

So F is C^1 on [0, L), smooth on (0, L), strictly convex, and grows
faster than linear but slower than any power near L (it diverges
logarithmically).

To remember the derivative values, work in r = psi^{-1}(I): with
r = tanh(v), the formulas come out as double integrals of cosh
factors.  The specific bounds: from

    F''(psi(r)) = cosh^2(v) * A(v),
    A(v) = 2 integral_0^1 (1 - s^2) cosh(2 v s) ds,

we get A(v) >= A(0) = 4/3 and A'(v) > 0 for v > 0, which gives
F'' >= 4/3 and (with a bit of care) F''' > 0 for 0 < I < L.

9.3  Curvature growth.

For every I in (0, L),

    F'''(I) / F''(I)  >  2.

Equivalently, the function I |-> e^{-2I} F''(I) is strictly
increasing on [0, L).  This "rate of change of curvature" is a key
input to the proof of the reserve lemma and the high-information
threshold.  It follows from the same cosh-integral representation.

9.4  Scaled entropy curvature.

Define, for 0 < h <= L,

    P(h) := h^2 F''(L - h).

Then

    1/2 <= P(h) <= M := 4 L^2 / 3,

with the upper bound attained at h = L (i.e. at r=0).  The constant M
satisfies 1/2 < M < 2/3 < L.

This "curvature near the entropy ceiling" controls the thinning
argument (Section 14), where the surplus must be paid out of a
shrinking entropy window.

9.5  Constants.

    L = log 2                 in (2/3, 25/36)
    M = 4 L^2 / 3             in (1/2, 2/3)
    2 > 3 M                   (a strict margin, needed for the
                               thinning argument)

These follow from the elementary identities L = 2 atanh(1/3) and
some binomial-style estimates.  The margin 2 > 3M is a numerical
compat condition that makes the whole proof work; a happy accident
of the constants.

--------------------------------------------------------------
11. THINNING: PAYING FOR INFORMATION WITH MASS
--------------------------------------------------------------

11.1 WHY ANOTHER ESTIMATE IS NEEDED. At fixed bias m, the profile B_m can be
smaller than F. Section 9.2 says exactly when: the cheap capacity c_m = H(m)
- alpha L is paid at rate 2, while F charges a rate that rises above 2 as the
information grows. So for small information the reserve is the honest bound,
and for large information the coin rate is. Section 12 takes the maximum.
That maximum, however, has to satisfy the local joining inequality itself,
and the difficulty is concentrated in the branch where F is the binding
profile. This section supplies the estimate that branch needs, and it is the
only place in the paper where the actual shape of the canonical optimizer is
used, not just its cost.

11.2 THE THINNING PATH. Take an interior ordered core a_c > b_c with
nonnegative center, and put

    x_c = (a_c+b_c)/2 >= 0,   d_c = (a_c-b_c)/2 > 0,
    h_c = [ H(a_c) + H(b_c) ] / 2,   J_c = H(x_c) - h_c > 0.

The strict inequality holds by strict concavity of H, since a_c is not b_c.
For 0 < lambda <= 1 put mass lambda on the core and mass 1 - lambda on the
common output (1,1). The mixture has

    parent mean  x = 1 - lambda(1 - x_c),   radius  d = lambda d_c,
    entropy      e = lambda h_c,

so its two faces are the deterministic values x + d and x - d. Define

    hbar = [ H(x+d) + H(x-d) ] / 2,   nu = hbar - e,
    J = H(x) - hbar,   I = nu + J = H(x) - e.

Entropy concavity gives nu >= 0, strict concavity gives J > 0, and e > 0
gives I < L. Also x >= 0 and 0 < d < 1-x, so every quantity below is in the
range where the scalar facts of section 5 apply.

11.3 THE CHANNEL READING. The path is a mixture of two channels, and saying
so makes the identities of 11.4 obvious. Let X be a fair sign and let B be an
independent flag with P(B = 1) = lambda. Given X and B, publish the sign U
with conditional mean

    E[U | X, B] = (1-B) + B ( x_c + d_c X ).

Then, by the chain rule and the definitions,

    H(U) = H(x),    H(U|X) = hbar,    H(U|X,B) = e,
    I(U;X,B) = I = nu + J,    I(U;B|X) = nu,    I(U;X) = J.

So nu is the information about the FLAG that survives once the row is known,
and J is the information about the ROW. The state is a weighted family of
channels in which the flag and the row compete for the same bit.

11.4 THE PAYMENT THEOREM. The estimate this section proves is a statement
about the increment of F along the path.

THEOREM (common-output payment). For the core and mass of 11.2,

    F(nu + J) - F(nu) <= lambda F(J_c).

Read it as follows. The left side is the extra rate that the coin profile
demands for the information I = nu + J over what it demands for nu alone:
that is, the part of the demand that is created by mixing the core with the
common output. The right side is the rate of the core, discounted by the
mass actually carried. So a common output lets you buy the increment at a
discount proportional to the retained mass.

The proof shows that [F(nu+J) - F(nu)]/lambda INCREASES with lambda. At
lambda = 1 the flag is deterministic and the increment is exactly F(J_c),
which is the endpoint to compare against; so the ratio is at most F(J_c)
throughout, which is the theorem. Everything below is the derivative
computation for that monotonicity, and it is the last genuinely delicate
step in the paper.

11.5 THE DERIVATIVES ALONG THE PATH. Put

    p = 1 + x,   sigma = 1 - x,   b = ln(1+x),   A = ln( 2/(1+x) ) = L - b,
    q = e + psi(x) = L - I,   s = e + L - hbar = L - nu,
    kappa = -(1/2) ln( 1 - d^2/p^2 ) > 0.

Then 0 < q < s <= L, and s - q = J. Note that A is not the same letter as the
alpha of section 9; it is a logarithm here, and it is used only inside this
section.

Two identities are needed. Both come from the one-variable identity

    t h_bin'(t) - h_bin(t) = ln(1 - t),
    h_bin(t) = -t ln t - (1-t) ln(1-t) = H(1-2t),

which is a two-line check, applied to the coins (1-x)/2, (1-x-d)/2 and
(1-x+d)/2.

  (a)  lambda I' = I - A.

       Here x' = -(1 - x_c) and e' = h_c, so
       lambda I' = lambda(1-x_c)V(x) - lambda h_c = (1-x)V(x) - e by
       1 - x = lambda(1-x_c). The claim is therefore
       (1-x)V(x) - e = H(x) - e - ln(2/(1+x)), that is
       H(x) - ln(2/(1+x)) = (1-x)V(x), which follows from
       H(x) = L - (1/2)ln(1-x^2) - xV(x) and L - ln(1+x) = ln(2/(1+x)).

  (b)  lambda nu' = nu - A - kappa.

       Writing hbar' in terms of V(x+d) and V(x-d) and using
       1 - x_c = sigma/lambda and d_c = d/lambda gives
       lambda hbar' = [(sigma-d)V(x+d) + (sigma+d)V(x-d)]/2. Applying the
       entropy identity to the two child coins and averaging, the two
       logarithms have average

           (1/2) ln( (1-(x+d)^2) (1-(x-d)^2) / 4 ) = (1/2) ln( ((1+x)^2-d^2)/4 )
                                                   = (1/2) ln( p^2(1 - d^2/p^2)/4 )
                                                   = -A - kappa,

       which is (b).

Now put Delta(lambda) = F(I) - F(nu). Then

    lambda Delta' - Delta
      = F'(I)(lambda I') - F'(nu)(lambda nu') - F(I) + F(nu)
      = [F'(I)(I-A) - F(I)] - [F'(nu)(nu-A-kappa) - F(nu)]
      = int_nu^I (u - A) F''(u) du + F'(nu) kappa,

the last step by the fundamental theorem of calculus, since the derivative in
u of (u-A)F'(u) - F(u) is (u-A)F''(u). This is the exact Euler derivative of
the normalized increment, and it is where the shape of F enters.


10. THE LOCAL JOINING PROBLEM
================================================================

Everything now depends on one piece of geometry: minimizing the
joining cost

    c(a, b) = (a-b)/4 * ( V(a) - V(b) )

over pairs (A, B) with specified "means" and "entropy budget".

10.1  Formal statement.

Let a, b in (-1, +1), with -1 < b < a < 1.  Write

    m  := (a + b) / 2      (parent mean),
    delta := (a - b) / 2   (child spread; > 0).

So the two child means are m + delta = a and m - delta = b.

For a coupling of two random variables (A, B) on {-1, +1} values with
joint distribution, we say "the coupling has means (a, b)" if
E A = a and E B = b.  Its "average entropy" is

    e(A, B) := [E H(A) + E H(B)] / 2.

Jensen gives e <= (H(a) + H(b))/2 =: e_max.  The coupling achieving
e_max is the deterministic one (A = a constant? no, A and B
independent, each deterministic at its mean is degenerate; the
COUPLING attaining e_max is the "product" coupling with A and B
independent and each having the unique distribution with the given
mean — actually the max-entropy coupling at fixed margins is the
INDEPENDENT one).  Regardless, e_max is the ceiling.

Define the budget problem:

    K(m, delta, e) :=
        inf { E[c(A, B)] :
              E A = m+delta, E B = m-delta,
              0 < e(A,B) <= e <= e_max }.

The infimum is over all joint laws on [-1, +1]^2 with those means.
We extend c by zero at (1, 1) and (-1, -1), and by +infinity at every
other boundary pair.

10.2  Why this is the object we need.

Section 8.3 reduced the whole induction to bounding
E[c(u_+(X'), u_-(X'))] from below by the "gap" between
B_hat_m(I(u)) and the average children B values.  But E[c(u_+, u_-)]
is bounded below by the minimum over all couplings with the same
means and average entropy: E c >= K(m, delta, e).  So if we can show

    K(m, delta, e)  >=  (gap from Section 8.3)

for every (m, delta, e), we get the induction.  Sections 11-14 do
exactly this.  The answer has a clean structure:

    * The minimum K(m, delta, e) is attained by a "three-point"
      distribution: one ordered interior pair (x, y) with x > y in
      (-1, +1), plus zero, one, or two equal-output atoms at (1,1)
      and (-1,-1).

    * Which of the two geometries (both corners vs one corner) is
      optimal depends on a threshold e_0(m, delta) that comes out of
      the entropy of the reflected core.

    * There is an exact comparison between the "gap" and this
      minimum, proved in two moves.

10.3  The cost function c itself.

The joining cost has a probabilistic reading.  It is symmetric,
c(a, b) = c(b, a), and vanishes on the diagonal, c(a, a) = 0.  Its
sign: since (a - b) has the same sign as V(a) - V(b) (V is strictly
increasing), c >= 0 pointwise.  It blows up near the equal-corner
boundary (1, 1) and (-1, -1) is where we extend to zero, and every
other boundary pair to +infinity.

A useful reformulation: use the identity

    c(a, b) = 1/8 [ J_bar(1+a, 1+b) + J_bar(1-a, 1-b) ],
    J_bar(s, t) = (s - t) * log(s/t).

Here J_bar is the sum of two relative-entropy perspectives (a Bregman
divergence on (0, +infinity)).  From this, one gets joint convexity of
c on (-1, 1)^2.  The extension to the closed square by lower
semicontinuity agrees with the "zero at equal corners, infinity at
other boundary" extension above.


11. THE CANONICAL THREE-POINT OPTIMIZER
================================================================

We now compute K(m, delta, e) exactly.

11.1  The pricing trick.

Fix m, delta.  Introduce a "price" p >= 0 for entropy.  Consider the
priced objective

    inf { E c(A, B) + p [E H(A) + E H(B)] :
          E A = a, E B = b }.

This is a Lagrange multiplier setup: by varying p, we trace out the
minimal-cost solutions at each entropy level (standard convex
duality; Section 11.7 fills in the details of coverage).

Write the objective for a single candidate law:

    law = lambda * delta_{(x,y)} + pi_+ * delta_{(1,1)}
                                    + pi_- * delta_{(-1,-1)}.

This places:
    - mass lambda on an interior ordered pair (x, y) with x > y;
    - mass pi_+ on the equal corner (1, 1);
    - mass pi_- on the equal corner (-1, -1).

Mass conservation: lambda + pi_+ + pi_- = 1.  The equal corners
contribute neither cost (c = 0 there) nor entropy (H(+-1) = 0), so
the priced value of this law is

    lambda * [ c(x,y) + p (H(x) + H(y)) ].

The mean-difference constraint fixes lambda (x - y):

    lambda (x - y) = (a - b) = 2 delta.

Putting these together, the priced value equals

    2 delta * F_p(z, w),

where z = log((1+x)/(1+y)) and w = log((1-y)/(1-x)) and

    F_p(z, w) = (z + w)/8  +  p  H(z, w),

    H(z, w) = (H(x) + H(y)) / (x - y).

So the pruned optimization lives in the (z, w) coordinates.

11.2  The (z, w) coordinate system.

Define

    z := log( (1+x) / (1+y) )       (> 0 when x > y),
    w := log( (1-y) / (1-x) )       (> 0),

    u := 1 / (e^z - 1)              (>= 0),
    v := 1 / (e^w - 1)              (>= 0),
    n := 1 + u + v                  (>= 1).

In terms of these:

    x = (1 + u - v) / n,
    y = (u - v - 1) / n,
    x - y = 2 / n.

The mean-constraint equations determine the masses:

    lambda = delta * n,
    pi_+ = (1 + b - 2 delta u) / 2,
    pi_- = (1 - a - 2 delta v) / 2.

The nonnegativity conditions pi_+ >= 0, pi_- >= 0 translate into
"floors" on (z, w):

    z >= z_0 := log( (1+a) / (1+b) ),
    w >= w_0 := log( (1-b) / (1-a) ).

These ceilings on u, v restrict (z, w) to a quadrant-with-corner.

11.3  The entropy in (z, w).

Define the "normalized entropy"

    H(z, w) = (H(x) + H(y)) / (x - y).

It has a useful positive series:

    H(z, w) = (q(z) + q(w))/2
              + sum_{i, j >= 1}  e^{-(i z + j w)} / max(i, j),

    q(t) = t/(e^t - 1) - log(1 - e^{-t}).

In particular, H is symmetric under z <-> w (because x, y
symmetric under swap), strictly convex on (0, +infinity)^2, and
differentiable.

The convexity of H is essential: it makes F_p strictly convex for
p > 0, so the minimum is unique.

11.4  The strict-convexity minimum.

Suppose p > 0.  F_p is strictly convex on the (z, w) region, and the
constraint is a floor at (z_0, w_0).  So the minimum of F_p subject to
the floor is uniquely attained at some (z*, w*).

There are two shapes it can take:

    (a) Reflected pair geometry: z = w.  Then x = -y: the core is
        (r, -r).  The masses become lambda = delta / r, pi_+ and
        pi_- adjusted so the mean is m.  This is valid when pi_+ and
        pi_- are nonnegative, i.e. r > delta.

    (b) One-corner geometry: w = w_0 (equivalently pi_- = 0).
        Only the (1, 1) corner is present.  Then y = 1 - (1-b)/k and
        x = 1 - (1-a)/k for the core mass k = lambda.

Which shape occurs depends on p.  As p increases (rewarding low
entropy), we move from the deterministic p=0 solution (which is the
floor z_0, w_0, with mass lambda = delta/(...)) toward lower entropy
cores.  Eventually we hit z = w (reflected geometry) or w = w_0
(one-corner).  Which one is hit first depends on the numbers.

Reflected hypothesis: as p -> infinity, is the floor still the
active constraint?  If z >= z_0 and w >= w_0 both bind at p=infinity
then it would be the deterministic case.  Otherwise the minimizer
approaches a corner.

Let me collapse the algebra.  In the strict-convexity regime p > 0:

    * At the floor (z_0, w_0): both corner masses positive, lambda at
      the deterministic pair (a, b).  This is p=0.

    * As p grows: if the minimizer stays in the interior box, it
      solves grad F_p = 0.  Strict convexity makes this unique.  The
      solution has z < w unless m = 0.  It can be either above the
      floor or hit the floor w = w_0.

    * The transition between "both corners" and "only positive corner"
      happens on the wall w = w_0 (as the "transposed" wall z = z_0
      corresponds to the symmetric case with only negative corner).

So as p varies, we get one of the two shapes: full "reflected" pair
(core aligned with sign of m) or one-corner.  The transition is a
specific entropy level

    e_0(m, delta) = (1 - m) H( delta / (1 - m) ).

For e <= e_0: reflected geometry.  For e >= e_0: one-corner geometry.
At e = e_0: boundary, one corner (positive if m >= 0).

11.5  The reflected geometry, made concrete.

Core (r, -r) with r > delta, lambda = delta / r, and

    e_reflected(r, delta) = (delta/r) H(r),
    K_reflected = delta V(r).

Note: e_reflected does NOT depend on m.  This is important.  The
cost K_reflected is a function of delta and r only (r determined by
delta and e).  So at fixed (delta, e), reflected cores are
m-independent, and their cost doesn't move as the parent mean moves.

11.6  The one-corner geometry, made concrete.

Only the (1, 1) corner, with core mass k in [1 - m, 1].  Then

    x = 1 - (1-a)/k,
    y = 1 - (1-b)/k,
    e_onecorner = (k/2)(H(x) + H(y)),
    K_onecorner = k c(x, y).

The cost and entropy increase with k.  The threshold k = 1 - m gives
e = e_0(m, delta), which connects to the reflected geometry.

11.7  The affine support argument.

To prove K is exactly the "best lower bound" (i.e., no other coupled
law can beat the three-point optimizer), we use an affine lower bound
in the pair values (A, B).

At the priced optimizer (z*, w*), define multipliers gamma_+ >= 0 and
gamma_- >= 0 by

    gamma_+ = (F_p)_z / (2 u(u+1)),
    gamma_- = (F_p)_w / (2 v(v+1)).

A derivative vanishes unless its floor is active.  The function

    Q(z, w) := F_p(z, w) + gamma_+ (1 + 2u) + gamma_- (1 + 2v)

has zero gradient at (z*, w*).  It is strictly convex on the whole
positive quadrant.  Its minimum kappa > 0 (positivity: the linear
term in (z+w)/8 is strictly positive).  Multiplying Q >= kappa by
(x - y) > 0 gives a global affine support inequality:

    c(x, y) + p[H(x) + H(y)] - kappa(x - y)
             + gamma_+ (2 + x + y)  + gamma_- (2 - x - y)  >=  0.

This is valid for every (x, y) in the closed square (with c = 0 at
equal corners and c = +infinity elsewhere).  The optimizer law is
supported at equality points (equality at (x*, y*) by construction;
equality at the corners exactly when gamma = 0, i.e., corner mass
zero).  Averaging the support inequality proves optimality.

This argument also proves uniqueness of the optimizer, because the
only possible interior contact point is (x*, y*) and the equality
set is otherwise the corner atoms (which are supported only when
their gamma vanishes).

11.8  Exact entropy coverage.

For every 0 < e <= e_max, the priced problem attains exactly the
budgeted optimum K(m, delta, e):

    K(m, delta, e) = min_{p >= 0} [ 2 delta F_p(z*, w*) + 2 p e ],

and there is a price p = p(e) with entropy exactly e.

Sketch of coverage: the entropy of the priced optimizer is a
continuous nonincreasing function of p, equal to e_max at p = 0, and
tending to 0 as p -> infinity.  So by the intermediate value
theorem, every intermediate entropy is attained.  (The p -> infinity
limit uses a reflected-core comparison law whose entropy -> 0.)

Consequently, for any coupled law with the same means and average
entropy at most e, the priced optimizer gives a lower bound on cost:

    E c(A, B) >= K(m, delta, e).

This is the exact local joining inequality for the three-point cost.

11.9  Comparing the two geometries.

Assume m >= 0 (reflect for m < 0).  Then z_0 <= w_0.  Strict
convexity + symmetry gives z <= w at the optimizer (swapping
coordinates would be an equally valid law with the same value).
Consider p > 0.  If w > w_0 AND z < w, then (F_p)_w = 0 by
complementarity, but strict convexity + symmetry give
(F_p)_z < (F_p)_w = 0, contradicting optimality.  So either z = w
(reflected) or w = w_0 (one corner).

At w = w_0 we have y = 1 - (1-b)/k with k = lambda = delta * n.
Differentiating the entropy of the one-corner geometry in k gives

    d/dk [ (k/2)(H(x) + H(y)) ]
        = 1/2 [ log(2/(1+x)) + log(2/(1+y)) ]   > 0.

And the reflected-core entropy (delta/r) H(r) also increases in r
(equivalently in k).  Both monotonicities are strict.

Consequently the transition happens at a unique k = 1 - m, i.e.
entropy e_0(m, delta) = (1 - m) H( delta / (1 - m) ).  Reflected
cores have e <= e_0 and one-corner cores have e >= e_0.

This is a support-side threshold: it depends only on (m, delta), not
on the entropy level e.  It organises the analysis: at low entropy
(most of the mass at the corners) you're in the reflected world; at
high entropy you're in the one-corner world.


12. THE VARIANCE-REFINED ONE-BIT INEQUALITY
================================================================

The core of the reserve argument is a one-dimensional inequality for
a soft channel of a single fair sign.  This section proves it.

12.1  Statement.

Let X be a fair sign, and let g(X) = m + delta X with m real and
delta >= 0, |m| + delta < 1.  So g takes two values m + delta and
m - delta, each with probability 1/2.

Define

    alpha := 1 - m^2                     (> 0),
    beta  := alpha - delta^2 = 1 - m^2 - delta^2  (> 0),

    J  := ( psi(m+delta) + psi(m-delta) ) / 2 - psi(m),
    D  := ( delta / 2 ) * ( V(m+delta) - V(m-delta) ).

Interpretation:
    J  is the "extra information" that the correlated (m, delta)
       arrangement gives over the pure average m.
    D  is the "cost" — the Dirichlet form of g.

Lemma 12.1 (Variance-refined one-bit).
------------------------------------------------
For every m, delta >= 0 with |m| + delta < 1,

    J < alpha L,              (finite)
    D >= alpha F( J / alpha ).                  (12.1)
------------------------------------------------

Two consequences:

    c(m+delta, m-delta) >= alpha F(J/alpha)     (via Section 8.2)
    c(m+delta, m-delta) >= F(J)                 (convexity, F(0) = 0)

Both are useful; the alpha-weighted version is what pays the surplus.

12.2  Proof of Lemma 12.1 (sketch).

Reflection lets us assume m >= 0, delta >= 0.  The delta = 0 case is
trivial.  Assume delta > 0.

Introduce w_+, w_- and r_+, r_- by

    w_+ := (1 + m)/2,    w_- := (1 - m)/2,
    r_+ := delta / (1 + m),    r_- := delta / (1 - m).

These are the posterior probabilities (w_+, w_-) of a sign U (+1
with probability w_+, -1 with probability w_-) and the posterior
conditional means of X given U (r_+, -r_-).

The mutual information I(U; X) can be evaluated two ways.  In the
U-first order:

    J = w_+ psi(r_+) + w_- psi(r_-)
      = w_+ * I(r_+) + w_- * I(r_-),

where I(r) := psi(r) is the information of a sign of mean r.

In the X-first order:

    D = w_+ F(I(r_+)) + w_- F(I(r_-)),

where F(psi(r)) = r V(r) is the coordinate rate.  Here I use

    V(m+delta) - V(m-delta) = V(r_+) + V(r_-),
    w_+ r_+ = w_- r_- = delta / 2.

Both identities matter.

The secant slope phi(s) := F(s)/s, extended to phi(0) = 2, is
increasing and convex on [0, L).  (Convexity of F with F(0) = 0
gives monotonicity of the secant slope; convexity of phi follows
from F''' >= 0 via an integral representation.)

The "size-biased" weights w_+ i_+ / J and w_- i_- / J (with
i_+ := I(r_+), i_- := I(r_-)) are positive and sum to one.  The
mean of the two i-values under these weights is

    s_* := (w_+ i_+^2 + w_- i_-^2) / J,

with 0 < s_* <= max{i_+, i_-} < L.

Now apply Jensen to the size-biased average of phi = F/s:

    D/J = (w_+ i_+ / J) phi(i_+) + (w_- i_- / J) phi(i_-)
       >= phi(s_*).

If we can show s_* >= J/alpha, then combining with monotonicity of
phi gives

    D/J >= phi(J/alpha),

which is (12.1).  The J < alpha L side follows from
r_- < 1 and the pi/4 bound; see below.

12.3  The size-bias gain s_* >= J/alpha.

The ratio psi(r)/r^2 is strictly increasing on (0, 1) (Taylor series
with positive coefficients).  Hence

    q := i_+ / i_- <= q_0 := ( (1-m) / (1+m) )^2.

For 0 <= q <= 1, the ratio

    R(q) := (w_+ q^2 + w_-) / (w_+ q + w_-)^2

is strictly decreasing in q (derivative: -2 w_+ w_- (1-q) over
(w_+ q + w_-)^3 <= 0).

Direct substitution q = q_0 gives

    (w_+ i_+^2 + w_- i_-^2) / J^2  >=  (1 + 3 m^2) / (1 - m^2)
                                   >=  1 / alpha.

So s_* = (w_+ i_+^2 + w_- i_-^2) / J >= J / alpha.  Done.

12.5  Interpretation.

The lemma says: at a single biased one-bit channel, the "surplus
cost" (D) is at least alpha times what a coordinate of matching
information would cost (F).  The factor alpha = 1 - m^2 is the
variance weight of the parent sign, exactly what we need for the
B_m envelope.  This is the "variance-refined" step; without it, we
would get D >= F(J), which is weaker.

The classical one-bit bound (D >= F(J)) follows immediately from
convexity of F at F(0) = 0; the variance-refined version is what
pools correctly in Section 13.


13. THE RESERVE AND THE PAYMENT
================================================================

The mean-dependent profile B_m encodes a "reserve" that a zero-mean
profile F does not see.  Section 13 defines the reserve, builds the
profile, and computes exactly what a "join" must pay to make the
induction close.

13.1  The reserve function.

Define, for v > 0 and e > 0,

    R(v, e) := v * S( L - e/v ),

with S(I) = F(I) - 2I extended by zero for I < 0.  The argument
L - e/v can be negative; the zero extension handles that case.

Interpretation: R(v, e) is the surplus S "rescaled" by v and pinned
to the entropy interval [L - e/v, L].  It is a perspective function
of S in the pair (v, e), hence jointly convex (because S is convex).

13.2  The mean-dependent profile B_m.

The main profile is

    B_m(I) := 2I + R(1 - m^2, H(m) - I),

for 0 <= I < H(m).  This profile is defined for every m in (-1, 1).

Equivalently, letting alpha = 1 - m^2,

    B_m(I) = 2I + alpha S( L - (H(m) - I)/alpha ).

At I = H(m) the surplus argument hits L exactly; R(alpha, 0) = 0, so
B_m(H(m)) = 2 H(m).  At I = 0 the argument is L - H(m)/alpha; here
S may already be 0 if H(m)/alpha >= L.

A comparison with F: B_m(I) >= F(I)?  Not always.  At m = 0,
alpha = 1, B_0 = 2I + S(L - H(0) + I) = 2I + S(L - L + I) = 2I + S(I)
= F(I).  So B_0 = F identically.  For m != 0, B_m can be either above
or below F.  Section 15 shows B_m >= F above half information (the
"high-information reserve").  Below half, B_m may be less than F, so
we take the hybrid B_hat := max(B_m, F).

13.3  The two-capacity allocation.

The profile B_m has a clean interpretation as an allocation of the
total information budget between two "capacities":

    Set alpha = 1 - m^2 and c_m := H(m) - alpha L = L m^2 - psi(m).

    For 0 <= I < H(m),

        B_m(I) = inf { 2u + alpha F(v / alpha) :
                       u + v = I,  0 <= u <= c_m,
                       0 <= v < alpha L }.

    Interpretation: u is paid at the LINEAR rate 2 (a "baseline"),
    v is paid at the COORDINATE rate F (a "surplus").  The optimal
    split is u = min(I, c_m), v = I - u.

    So the total information I is split into a "baseline" part of
    capacity c_m = L m^2 - psi(m) (paid at rate 2), and a "surplus"
    part of capacity alpha L (paid at the coordinate rate).  The
    capacity c_m is a mean-dependent "bonus" that a nonzero mean
    provides; it comes from the fact that biased signs hold a small
    amount of information even at "baseline" (2I) rate.

Section 13.5 verifies the allocation formula.

13.4  Why a join must pay.

Consider the induction step.  Parent has mean m, child split at
m + delta (child A) and m - delta (child B), with average entropy
e = (e_a + e_b)/2.  Let's set

    alpha := 1 - m^2,
    beta := alpha - delta^2 = 1 - m^2 - delta^2,
    h_bar := (H(m+delta) + H(m-delta)) / 2,
    J := H(m) - h_bar  (>= 0 by concavity of H),

    j := h_bar - e = (I_A + I_B) / 2 (average child information).

The parent has information I = H(m) - e = J + j.  The child average
information is j (by assumption), and the "burst" from averaging is
J.

The inductive hypotheses give child productions:
    D(A) >= 2 j_A + R(1 - (m+delta)^2, H(m+delta) - e_A),
    D(B) >= 2 j_B + R(1 - (m-delta)^2, H(m-delta) - e_B),
where j_A = H(m+delta) - e_A, etc.

By Jensen (perspective of S is jointly convex) we can pool the
two child profiles to get

    [B_{m+delta}(I_A) + B_{m-delta}(I_B)] / 2
        >= 2 j + R(beta, e),

where I used v_A = 1 - (m+delta)^2, v_B = 1 - (m-delta)^2, whose
average is (2 - 2m^2 - 2 delta^2)/2 = 1 - m^2 - delta^2 = beta.

Now the parent profile is

    B_m(I) = 2(J + j) + R(alpha, e).

So the "payment" required from the joining cost is

    W(m, delta, e) := B_m(I) - 2j - R(beta, e)
                    = 2J + R(alpha, e) - R(beta, e).

So if E c(u_+, u_-) >= W(m, delta, e) for every coupled law with
the given means and average entropy e, the induction closes.  The
local joining inequality of Section 10 is exactly
E c(u_+, u_-) >= K(m, delta, e) >= W(m, delta, e).

So: reduce to proving K(m, delta, e) >= W(m, delta, e), i.e. that
the three-point optimizer's cost dominates the payment.
