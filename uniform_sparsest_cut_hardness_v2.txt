# Constant-factor hardness of uniform sparsest cut

*An expository proof, written to be readable with high-school mathematics.*

---

## 0. The paper in one sentence

Take the graph-partitioning problem where every pair of vertices carries one
unit of demand, and you want to cut as little capacity as possible per unit
of separated demand. This is **Uniform Sparsest Cut**. The paper proves:
for every fixed constant C>1, approximating it within a factor C is
**NP-hard**. There is no polynomial-time factor-C algorithm unless
P = NP.

The method is a *reduction*: a polynomial-time machine that turns any
Boolean formula into a graph, built so that

- a **satisfiable** formula yields a graph with a cut of ratio a;
- an **unsatisfiable** formula yields a graph where *every* cut has ratio
  bigger than Ca.

A gap by a factor >C means a factor-C approximation, which must always
output a cut within C of the true optimum, would have to output a cut with
ratio le Ca on satisfiable instances and would be forced above Ca on
unsatisfiable ones. Just compare the returned ratio with the rational
number Ca: this decides satisfiability. So a factor-C approximator
solves an NP-hard problem, which would collapse P and NP.

The rest of the paper is the construction.

---

## 1. The problem, precisely

### 1.1 Cuts and their ratio

Let G be a finite undirected graph on the vertex set
[N]={1,2,...,N}. Give each unordered pair of distinct vertices a
nonnegative rational **capacity** c_{ij}=c_{ji}ge 0, with c_{ii}=0.
A **cut** is a partition of the vertices into two nonempty parts S and
V∖ S. Its **capacity** is the total capacity of the edges
crossing it:

cap(S)=sum_{iin S, jnotin S} c_{ij}.

The **denominator** we divide by is the number of unordered pairs of
distinct vertices that the cut *separates*:

|S|,(N-|S|).

This is exactly the number of pairs with one vertex in S and one
outside. The **sparsest-cut ratio** of G under uniform demand is

Phi(G)=min_{∅ne S⊊ [N]}
(cap(S))/(|S|,(N-|S|)). 

### 1.2 Why this exact denominator

The denominator |S|(N-|S|) encodes **unit demand between every pair of
distinct vertices**: the cut "serves" |S|(N-|S|) demands and pays
cap(S) in capacity. Dividing, we get the average capacity
per separated pair. This normalization is what makes the problem sensitive
to bottlenecks at every scale, including cuts where one side is tiny. A
lone vertex cut has denominator 1·(N-1)=N-1; a balanced cut has
denominator about N^2/4. A cut with a huge numerator but an even huger
denominator can still be the sparsest. The uniform objective refuses to
let the big side win by being big.

### 1.3 What "factor-C approximation" means

A **factor-C approximation algorithm** must, on every input G with
Nge 2, output a nonempty proper cut S with

(cap(S))/(|S|,(N-|S|)) le C,Phi(G).

It may be worse than optimal by at most the factor C, but not more.
Capacities are given in binary; the algorithm is allowed to run in time
polynomial in the input length, with the exponent permitted to depend on
C.

### 1.4 The main theorem

**Theorem (main).** *For every fixed real constant C>1 there is a
polynomial-time reduction from 3CNF satisfiability to finite undirected
graphs with nonnegative rational capacities and exactly unit demands,
together with a positive rational threshold a, such that*

formula satisfiable ⇒ Phi(G)le a,
formula unsatisfiable ⇒ Phi(G)>Ca.

*The graph size and the binary lengths of all output numbers are polynomial
in the formula size, with constants and exponents allowed to depend on C.
In particular, approximating Uniform Sparsest Cut within any fixed factor
C>1 is NP-hard.*

The source decision problem is NP-complete (Karp's original list, Problem
11). The reduction uses **no unproved complexity hypothesis** — no Unique
Games Conjecture, no Small-Set Expansion hypothesis. It produces an
explicit multiplicative gap for the objective (1), valid for *every*
nonempty proper cut, however lopsided.

## 3. Conventions

**Functions, not presentations.** A predicate is a Boolean function of the
entire proof-bit assignment; its sampling data may be retained when
conditioning, but a response to the predicate depends only on that
function. Two presentations of the same function cannot receive different
cut colors. The same convention applies to scores.

**Variance.** For a probability law mu on a space and a Boolean function
h,

Var_mu(h)=mu(h=1),mu(h=0)=tfrac12,E_{B,B'simmu},|h(B)-h(B')|, 

the last with B,B' independent draws from mu. This identity is the
bridge between "the cut disagrees on a pair" and "the cut has positive
variance." Every error in Sections 7–9 is bounded by a multiple of this
variance, not by an additive tolerance. That is what lets the argument
survive a cut of arbitrarily small positive mass.

**Measure and integral conventions.** A raw comparison form in Section 8 is
a finite positive measure on pairs of scores; its evaluation on a lookup
h is D(h)= |h(B)-h(B')|,dmathfrak m(B,B'),frakm(B,B'), and its truth
cost at assignment x is int |B(x)-B'(x)|,dmathfrak m(B,B').frakm(B,B'). The total
raw comparison weight is a constant independent of input size.

## 4. The pencil: a list on which quadratics vanish rarely

Everything starts with one list of vectors, surprisingly short, on which
any quadratic polynomial that is *not everywhere zero* is zero at only a
small fraction of points. This is the only place the proof touches deep
algebra, and the algebra is entirely explicit.

### 4.1 The statement

**Lemma (Quadratic evaluation pencil).** *For every 0<lambda_0<1 and
every sufficiently large power of two r, let q=r^2. There is a
deterministic algorithm which, given an integer dge 1, outputs a list*

u_1,...,u_minF_q^d

*with:*

**(i)** m=O(d), and the list contains every standard basis vector of
F_q^d;

**(ii)** *if a homogeneous quadratic polynomial Q in d variables does
not vanish at every point of the list, then*
#{j:Q(u_j)=0}lelambda_0,m.

*The running time is 2^{O(d)} bit operations; the constants in both
bounds may depend on r, which is fixed independently of d.*

Think of it as a **pencil of measurements**: a small, structured set of
points such that a quadratic cannot hide by being zero at most of them. In
Section 5 the list supplies rank-one steps of a random walk on a matrix
space; the eigenvalue estimate on that walk needs precisely "a quadratic
nonzero somewhere on the list vanishes rarely". The application takes
d=O(log(input size+1)), so 2^{O(d)} time is polynomial in the
formula size.

The list is built from an explicit tower of function fields (the
Garcia–Stichtenoth tower). The proof has four stages: (1) the two local
shapes of the tower at a place; (2) all branches above every base place;
(3) an integral-lattice discriminant count; (4) a big space of global
functions, a norm-degree bound, and an explicit finite matrix.

### 4.2 The tower

Fix a power of two r so large that, with D=r+1,
(2D)/(r^2-r)lelambda_0. 
All finite fields below have characteristic two. Put F=F_q,
q=r^2, and let k be an algebraic closure of F. Define

S(Y)=Y^r+Y,

coefficient (order of zero is +∞); an element of k((z)) is

### 4.3 The two local extension shapes

Suppose an equation S(X)=b(z) becomes integral after subtracting S(g),
for a fixed gin k((z)). Put b_0=b-S(g)in k[[z]]. The polynomial
beta^r+beta=b_0(0) has derivative one, so it has exactly r roots,
differing by elements of F_r; call one beta. For each choice
there is a unique positive-order u with S(u)=b_0-S(beta). If
b_0-S(beta)=sum_{n>0}b_n z^n, then

determines coefficients successively. So the equation **splits into r
distinct roots** g+beta+u in k((z)). The local algebra is r copies
of k((z)), with integral lattice k[[z]]^r.

The other shape is a simple pole, and it accounts for ramification:

**Lemma (simple-pole extension).** *Let bin k((z)) have order -1. Then
X^r+X-b is irreducible over k((z)), its extension is a Laurent series
field k((w)) with w=1/X, z has order r in w, and the integral
lattice has k[[z]]-basis 1,w,...,w^{r-1} with discriminant of
z-order 2(r-1).*

*Proof.* q_0(z)=1/b(z) has order one and a compositional inverse psi.
In a new series field k((w)), set

z=psi!(frac{w^r}{1+w^{r-1}}). 

Substitution is injective k((z))to k((w)), and z=cw^r+... with
cne0, while X=1/w satisfies X^r+X=b(z). The monomials w^jz^n,
0le j<r, nge0, have distinct leading orders j+rn exhausting all
nonnegative integers, so successively subtracting the lowest remaining
coefficient converges formally and gives

k[[w]]=⊕_{j=0}^{r-1}w^j,k[[z]].

The sum is direct (leading orders fall in distinct residue classes mod
r), so [k((w)):k((z))]=r and k((w))=k((z))(X), proving
irreducibility; separability follows from S'(X)=1. The displayed free
module is closed under multiplication, so its elements are integral over
k[[z]]; conversely a negative-w-order element cannot satisfy a monic
polynomial over k[[z]] (its highest power would be the unique
smallest-order term). Hence k[[w]] is the integral closure. The
embeddings send X|-> X+c, cinF_r, hence
w|-> w_c=1/(X+c); for cne c',
w_c-w_{c'}=(c'-c)/((X+c)(X+c')) has w-order two, i.e. extended
z-order 2/r. The evaluation matrix of 1,w,...,w^{r-1} is
Vandermonde; its determinant squared is the trace-pairing determinant,
of order 2binom r2(2/r)=2(r-1). QED

**Trace and norm convention.** The trace of an element of a finite algebra
is the trace of its multiplication map, the norm its determinant. Over a
separable field or product of them, diagonalizing multiplication shows
trace is the sum of embedded values and the trace matrix of a basis is the
evaluation matrix times its transpose. This also gives the discriminant
identity used above.

### 4.5 Integral lattices and the discriminant count

Fix a base place with parameter t, R=k[[t]]. The product of branch
integral rings is a free R-module of rank N. In coefficient coordinates

for a basis matrix C set the **determinant order**

determinant reduces, by invertible row and column operations, to a diagonal

column); the dim_k of the cokernel equals the determinant order. Second,
for a branch k((z)) over k((t)), multiplication by a unit has unit
determinant on k[[z]], while multiplication by z has cokernel k and

(ainZ, uin k[[z]]^ast). 

The equality for negative a is by multiplicativity; each branch residue

over F avoids enumerating an algebraic closure.

evaluation tuples and selecting independent columns adds polynomial time.
Since N<rd, the whole construction takes 2^{O_r(d)} time, completing the
proof of the pencil lemma.

## 5. The bit-test system

We now build the actual tests. A **proof** is a bit assignment on an
explicitly constructed finite set of positions. A **test law** is a
distribution over question lists, and for each sampled list together with
its public sampling data, an acceptance predicate on the answers. Repeated
questions are allowed and count toward the arity.

Standard preprocessing puts every 3CNF formula into a convenient shape:
clauses have three literal positions with repetitions permitted; repeat
literals to pad a shorter clause; replace an empty clause by two opposing
unit clauses on a fresh variable and pad; replace a formula with no clauses
by a single tautological clause. These preserve satisfiability and cost
linear time.

### 5.1 The statement

**Theorem (bit-test system).** *For every fixed lambda>0 there are
constants eta>0, B<∞, and a fixed finite list of bounded-arity
test laws with the following properties. From a Boolean 3CNF formula one
constructs, in polynomial time, a finite uniform space S of bit
positions and these test laws.*

**(i)** *Every individual question marginal is at most B times uniform
measure on S. All samplers are polynomially enumerable finite
weighted lists with rational probabilities; every positive seed probability
is at least inverse-polynomial in the input size.*

**(ii)** *If the formula is satisfiable, some assignment y:S
F_2 is accepted by every test in every support.*

**(iii)** *If the formula is unsatisfiable, every assignment fails one of
the test laws with probability greater than eta.*

The finiteness of the test list and the constant soundness eta are the

for a random walk on a matrix space; a masked four-point parity law; a
principal-minor encoding of Boolean tables; and a self-correction argument
that turns almost-quadratic lines into exactly quadratic lines on
almost every line.

## 6. Primitive events and the valuation obstruction

The bit-test system speaks of assignments. We now package its output so a
*cut* can interact with it. The bridge is a **valuation**: a single bit for
each *predicate* (Boolean function of the whole proof assignment), with the
rule that two sampled descriptions of the same function must get the same
bit. We show that a good valuation decodes a satisfying assignment, and
that on unsatisfiable formulas no good valuation can exist.

Throughout, Ω={0,1}^{S} is the cube of bit assignments
of the bit-test system. A **predicate** is a Boolean function
R:Ωto{0,1}. We identify predicates that are equal as functions,
regardless of their sampled descriptions, and write R⊆ R' when
R(y)le R'(y) for every yinΩ.

A **primitive type** is a probability law P_s on predicates together with
a number p_sin[0,1]. An **ordered comparison** is a coupling of two
specified primitive laws supported on pairs R⊆ R'. The type index
is sampling data; it is not part of the predicate the valuation sees.

**Definition.** A Boolean valuation H on the actual predicates is
*zeta-calibrated and ordered* if, for every primitive type and every
specified comparison,

big|E_{Rsim P_s}H(R)-p_sbig|lezeta,
Prbig[H(R)>H(R')big]lezeta. 

### 6.1 The statement

**Lemma (event obstruction).** *There is a fixed finite family of primitive
types (P_s,p_s) and ordered comparisons, and a fixed zeta>0,
constructible in polynomial time from the input formula, such that:*

**(i)** *each predicate depends on boundedly many bit positions, and all
samplers are polynomially enumerable finite rational laws with
inverse-polynomial positive seed probabilities;*

**(ii)** *constant-one and constant-zero types are included, with
calibrations one and zero; every comparison marginal is a primitive type;
and every type has a comparison to the constant-one type;*

**(iii)** *in a satisfiable instance, evaluation at the perfect assignment
y^ast satisfies E_{P_s}R(y^ast)=p_s for every type;*

**(iv)** *in an unsatisfiable instance, no Boolean valuation is
zeta-calibrated and ordered.*

*The numbers p_s, the number of types and comparisons, and zeta are
independent of the input size.*

### 6.2 The primitive types

types:

## 7. From a rare conjunction to one valuation

This section is the technical heart of "arbitrarily small cut mass". We
have a Boolean response W to a conjunction r=R_1wedge...wedge R_t
of many independently sampled predicates, whose success probability may be
astronomically small. We prove that a good response, with errors measured
*relative to* its own success scale v, still forces a single valuation
satisfying (36). The key advantage is uniformity: the bounds do not involve
the number of predicates or any atom's probability.

The method is an entropy argument. Conditioning on success gives a
probability law with a controlled amount of relative entropy; a chain-rule
and convexity analysis exposes a *common* conditional density; counting with
the type-pattern bounds concentrates the law near a single type mixture;
two clipping steps pinch the density near 0 and a fixed value A; one
threshold then produces the valuation. (The parallel-repetition
entropy-distribution technique is due to Raz and Holenstein; the clipping and
thresholding here are adapted to produce one valuation of *actual
predicates*.)

## 8. Scores, comparison measures, and the amplitude signal

A **score** is a real function of the proof-bit assignment, depending on a
bounded number of positions. Identical functions are identified even if
their sampling presentations differ. A **lookup** h is a measurable
function from scores to {0,1} (Borel on each finite table of score
values). A **raw comparison form** is a finite positive measure
mathfrak mfrakm on pairs of scores, with

D(h)=|h(B)-h(B')|,dmathfrak m(B,B'),frakm(B,B'),
truth cost at x=int|B(x)-B'(x)|,dmathfrak m(B,B').frakm(B,B'). 

The task of this section: build, from the primitive system of Section 6, a
demand law and a sum D of comparison forms, so that at a
satisfying assignment the total truth cost is small, while in a NO instance
*every* positive-variance lookup incurs a large comparison cost relative to
its variance. The small-but-positive variance is exactly the arbitrarily
small cut mass; every error is measured in units of that variance.

### 8.1 The analytic score gap

**Theorem (score gap).** *For each fixed T_0ge1, the primitive system of
Section 6 admits a demand law mu of scores and a finite sum D

## 9. Conditional kernels and ordered paths

Fix the good stage of (101); abbreviate s=s_j, alpha=alpha_j,
beta=beta_j, I=I_j, S=S_j, J=J_{j-1}. All backgrounds run through
stage j only. The goal is to turn the amplitude signal g_i into a single
common Boolean response W on actual conjunction predicates, whose successes
and failures are bounded by multiples of nu — so that Lemma 7.1 applies.

The obstacle is that a slot's presentation can leak into the partition, so
the amplitude response g_i might depend on how a predicate is described
instead of what it is. The fix is a common **deleted-slot background**,

### 9.5 The interface for finite compilation

We record the effective structure of the construction. For fixed T_0, all
choices in (83), the conjunction length, the path menu, Delta, M, and
the sequential stage parameters are constants; the upper bounds on the
required integers and numerical precision can be huge functions of T_0 but
never depend on instance size. The primitive test samplers use fixed
mixtures of uniform choices from polynomial-size finite sets.

**Proposition (finite form structure).** *The demand law and raw comparison
measures of Theorem 8.1 have polynomially enumerable discrete seeds, whose
positive probabilities are at least an inverse polynomial in input size. For
each seed, after finitely many fixed subdivisions of numerical boxes, the
conditional densities are nonnegative rational polynomials and the score
table entries are rational polynomials of bounded degree in a fixed number of
numerical variables. There are only finitely many such numerical polynomial
patterns, independent of input size. The total raw comparison mass is finite
and bounded by a constant. For any fixed finite grid and deterministic
entrywise rounding rule, every positive rounded demand mass or comparison-pair
mass has an inverse-polynomial lower bound.*

*Proof.* Each sampled predicate has bounded arity and a polynomially
enumerable presentation law; a conjunction, a comparison path, and each slot
use a fixed number of samples, and the number of slots, stage choices, freeze
subsets, formal descriptors, and resampling indicators is fixed. Enumerating
their products takes a polynomial number of steps, and all discrete
probabilities are rational with polynomial bit length (products of a fixed
number of primitive probabilities and fixed rational weights), preserving an
inverse-polynomial lower bound. Descriptor interpretation and list refinement
need only fixed-size truth tables.

Separate the amplitude atom at zero from its uniform part. The remaining
variables are amplitudes, interpolation times, and noises in rational
intervals, with the integration variables of the derivative forms; their
joint densities are products of rational polynomial densities (absolute
derivative weights are polynomial on the two half intervals; -f'_xi is
already nonnegative on its interval). No lookup-dependent descriptor occurs
in any measure. All forms are positive measures on these fixed rectangular
pieces.

Fix a seed and an assignment to its bounded list of queried positions. Each
predicate value is 0 or 1, each descriptor a fixed sign, and each mark
determined by the finite trial counts; the scores are sums of rational
multiples of amplitudes, times, products of time and fresh amplitude, and
noises, multiplied by these fixed Booleans. So every score entry is a bounded
degree polynomial with rational coefficients; the possible local Boolean
tables, label-collision patterns, descriptor vectors, and mark sets range over
fixed finite sets (the numerical coefficients s_j,xi_j and external
weights are already fixed). Thus the polynomial patterns form a fixed finite
family; actual bit names only select where a table is evaluated, giving a
uniform bound on arity and range. Each derivative integral has mass at most
15/(8xi_j) per differentiated coordinate with finitely many formal cells;
ordinary comparisons have mass one before their finite external weights, so
total raw mass is bounded.

Fix a finite grid and entrywise rounding. For one numerical pattern, each
possible rounded local table, or pair of tables, has mass equal to the
integral of its fixed polynomial density over a fixed region of its numerical
box; these regions depend only on the pattern and the rounding rule. Finitely
many patterns and rounded tables give a positive minimum of their positive
masses. Renaming queried positions or identifying collisions adds no
numerical patterns. Any positive global rounded mass has at least one
contributing discrete seed; multiplying its inverse-polynomial probability by
the fixed positive minimum proves the last assertion (identifying identical
scores only adds masses). This completes the proposition and the remaining
effective assertions of Theorem 8.1. QED

fix e,B_ast,gamma,b_0,K_0,B_0; Lemma 7.1 fixes t,ε; then
Delta fixes M; finally each stage fixes alpha_j,m_j,n_j,s_j,xi_j in
that order. The path constants above do not grow when a later scale is made
smaller or a stage size larger.

## 10. Finite graphs with exactly uniform demands

We now convert Theorem 8.1 into an actual graph in which the ratio
Phi(G)=cap(∂ S)/(|S|(N-|S|)) is the objective. Two
obstacles must be handled.

- **Exactness.** The demand of Theorem 8.1 is a real measure, not uniform
  all-pairs counting. We retain a slightly smaller *rational* demand l
  in a way that preserves the denominator |S|(N-|S|) exactly.
- **Arbitrarily small masses.** Replacing the demand by an additive
  approximation would not suffice, since the separated mass can be as
  small as one prefers.

The fix is to compile every score to a finite rational table, replicate
copies so that demand pairs are uniform, add heavy "cluster" edges whose
capacity exceeds any target cut, and add "tether" edges from leftover keys
to every retained vertex so any extra separated demand is expensive.

### 10.7 Complexity and conclusion (Theorem 2.1)

To finish the proof of Theorem 2.1, fix C>1 and choose C'>C. Sections
10.1–10.6 turn every 3CNF formula F of size n into an N-vertex
graph G(F) with N polynomial in n, such that with the target rational
a=A_0/N^2 we have

* (completeness, Section 10.5) if F is satisfiable then Phi(G(F))le a;
* (soundness, Section 10.6) if F is unsatisfiable then every nonempty
  proper cut has ratio >C'a=kappa/N^2>C· A_0/N^2ge Ca.

Neither direction inspects a satisfying assignment.

**The complexity of the whole construction.** Enumerate the primitives:
formula -> bit-test space S (size 2^{O(d)} with d=O(log
n)); score experiments (poly-enumerable rational seeds, fixed finite
numerical boxes); canonical keys (poly many, since k is constant and g
is constant); rational compilation (poly many boxes over a fixed-mesh grid);
replication (m_i poly many, since l_i inverse-poly); graph (O(N^2)
vertices). Each step is polynomial in n; the exponent may depend on
C, never on N.

**Decision procedure.** Given a 3CNF formula of size n:
- compute G (poly time);
- approximate Phi(G) within factor C (poly time by assumption);
- if ratio le Ca, accept; else reject.

In a YES instance, Theorem 10.7's completeness gives a cut of ratio
le a, and the approximator returns ratio le Ca<C'a.
In a NO instance, every cut has ratio >C'a>Ca, so any returned ratio
>Ca. So C-approximating Uniform Sparsest Cut to within C'a/Ca=C'/C
ratio decides 3CNF, contradicting mathbf PbfPneNP.

Fix C>1 as in Section 2.2 (say C=1+10^{-12}): then C'>C exists and
the construction gives, unconditionally, a factor-C' approximation gap
for Uniform Sparsest Cut with exactly uniform all-pairs demands. Theorem
2.1 follows. QED
