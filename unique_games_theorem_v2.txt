Unique Games Theorem (edge-value form)

For every fixed pair of desired errors epsilon, delta in (0, 1/2),
there is a finite binary vector-space alphabet K and a deterministic
polynomial-time reduction from 3SAT to Unique Games with alphabet K such
that:

   YES case:  if the 3SAT formula is satisfiable,
              then value of the game is at least 1 - epsilon.

   NO case:   if the 3SAT formula is unsatisfiable,
              then value of the game is at most delta.

The game is simple and bipartite. Every constraint is a translation:

             label(right) = label(left) + constant.

The alphabet K, the polynomial running time, and its constants depend
only on epsilon and delta. A required fixed lower bound on alphabet size
can also be met without changing value: append label bits constrained to
agree on every edge. Zero appended bits extend old labelings, and
projection to the old label gives the reverse value bound.

Reading the statement. A reduction is one algorithm for each fixed pair
(epsilon, delta), not one fixed alphabet that works for every pair. Its
input is a 3SAT formula; its output explicitly lists vertices, edges, and
translation offsets. The YES and NO promises concern the best labeling
of that output, not a labeling supplied by the reduction. Later K denotes
the intermediate alphabet F_2^ell; the final product alphabet is K^t.

PART 1. THE GAME, THE CONJECTURE, AND WHY THE ORDER OF QUANTIFIERS MATTERS
================================================================================

1.1. What is a Unique Game?
------------------------------------------------------------

The Unique Games formulation is due to Subhash Khot. Its defining condition
is not merely that a constraint has a satisfying pair, but that each label
on either side has exactly one compatible label on the other. [1]

[1] Subhash Khot. "On the Power of Unique 2-Prover 1-Round Games." 2002;
    linked abstract: CCC 2002.
    https://static.aminer.org/pdf/PDF/000/104/637/on_the_power_of_unique_prover_round_games.pdf

A verifier chooses an edge (u,v), sends u to the left player and v to the
right player, and checks their answers against that edge's rule. The
players know the instance in advance but cannot communicate after receiving
their questions. Each answers with one label from a fixed palette:

               colors = {0, 1, 2, ..., q-1}.

The possible question pairs are the edges of a graph. Each edge comes
with a PERMUTATION of the q colors. A permutation is just a rule that
takes the left player's color and tells us the only acceptable color
for the right player.

For example, with q=5, one edge might say:

        right answer = left answer + 1 (mod 5).

So if the left player says 3, the right player must say 4. If the
left player says 4, the right player must say 0. There is exactly one
correct right answer for each left answer. That is the "unique" part.

A translation is one very regular kind of permutation. The final game
we construct uses only translations, which keeps the bookkeeping simple
while retaining the stated completeness and soundness guarantees.

The players may agree on their response rules before the questions are
sampled. Each rule must use only that player's own question. Strategies here
are classical. Private or shared randomness
independent of the questions cannot improve the optimum: fix both complete
random tapes before the questions are drawn and average the resulting
deterministic labeling values.

The VALUE of the game is the largest possible fraction of edges that
they can satisfy, over all strategies.

A labeling is the simplest kind of strategy: assign one permanent
color to every left vertex and one permanent color to every right
vertex. It is a harmless mathematical fact that in these games both
players can be assumed to use a fixed labeling. The game value is:

        value(game) = max over all labelings of
                      (satisfied edges) / (all edges).

For a weighted occurrence list, replace the edge fraction by the sum of
satisfied occurrence weights. Two occurrences can have the same endpoints
but remain different random outcomes. A constraint must be a permutation:
knowing either endpoint's label determines the unique satisfying label at
the other endpoint. A general projection constraint determines the right
label from the left, but need not be invertible. Every Unique Game is a
projection game; the reverse implication is false.

The more general two-prover, one-round framework separates the question
pair distribution from the acceptance predicate. Feige and Lovász study
that framework; the restriction to permutation predicates is the additional
condition defining Unique Games here. [2]

[2] Uriel Feige; László Lovász. "Two-Prover One-Round Proof Systems: Their
    Power and Their Problems." STOC 1992.
    https://crypto.cs.mcgill.ca/~crepeau/COMP647/2007/TOPIC08/FL92.pdf

The maximum is over a single labeling used on every edge. It is not the
average of the best answer chosen separately for each edge. The latter
would always be one, because each individual permutation constraint is
satisfiable. The difficulty is making all local choices consistent.

1.2. The Unique Games Conjecture
------------------------------------------------------------

The Unique Games Conjecture, usually abbreviated UGC, says roughly:

   It is NP-hard to distinguish nearly satisfiable Unique Games
   from very unsatisfiable Unique Games.

More precisely: for every epsilon, delta in (0,1/2), there is a fixed
alphabet size q for which it is NP-hard to distinguish instances of
value at least 1-epsilon from instances of value at most delta. A
polynomial-time algorithm for this promise problem would imply P = NP. [3]

[3] Subhash Khot. "On the Unique Games Conjecture." Invited survey, CCC 2010,
    pp. 99-121; author manuscript.
    https://cs.nyu.edu/~khot/papers/UGCSurvey.pdf

Parts 3-9 give the reduction establishing this hardness statement.

The theorem gives the explicit deterministic reduction form:
a single algorithm that converts a 3SAT formula into an explicit game.
The game is unweighted, simple, bipartite, and has translation
constraints on a binary alphabet K = F_2^s.

1.3. Why the order of quantifiers is everything
------------------------------------------------------------

Three different claims must not be conflated. A hardness reduction maps
arbitrary 3SAT inputs to a gap problem. An integrality gap exhibits instances
on which a specified relaxation overestimates the true optimum. An
algorithm can exploit structure absent from the worst-case reduction.
Khot and Vishnoi and Raghavendra and Steurer construct important integrality
gaps for Unique Games and cut relaxations; those gaps alone are not the
3SAT reduction claimed here. [4] [5]

[4] Subhash Khot; Nisheeth K. Vishnoi. "The Unique Games Conjecture,
    Integrality Gap for Cut Problems and Embeddability of Negative Type
    Metrics into l_1." Journal of the ACM 62, 2015; arXiv:1305.4581.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.4581

[5] Prasad Raghavendra; David Steurer. "Integrality Gaps for Strong SDP
    Relaxations of Unique Games." FOCS 2009.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/cspgaps/

Conversely, sum-of-squares certificates can establish expansion and support
rounding on particular families. Barak, Brandão, Harrow, Kelner, Steurer,
and Zhou develop this connection with hypercontractivity. The existence of
such certificates on a family does not assert their existence on every
game. [6]

[6] Boaz Barak; Fernando G. S. L. Brandão; Aram W. Harrow; Jonathan A. Kelner;
    David Steurer; Yuan Zhou. "Hypercontractivity, Sum-of-Squares Proofs, and
    Their Applications." STOC 2012.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/hypercontract/

The conjecture would be false if epsilon, delta, and the alphabet size
were ordered carelessly. Many algorithms solve Unique Games really
well when epsilon is extremely tiny relative to the instance size.

For example:

Trevisan obtains useful approximation guarantees when the completeness
error is sufficiently small relative to logarithmic instance-size
parameters. The shrinking-error regime is different from fixing a positive
error before the input size grows. [7]

[7] Luca Trevisan. "Approximation Algorithms for Unique Games." Theory of
    Computing 4, 2008, pp. 111-128.
    https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v004a005/

Charikar, Konstantin Makarychev, and Yury Makarychev give a rounding
algorithm with value 1-O(sqrt(epsilon log q)). Its guarantee depends on
both the completeness error epsilon and the alphabet size q. [8]

[8] Moses Charikar; Konstantin Makarychev; Yury Makarychev. "Near-Optimal
    Algorithms for Unique Games." STOC 2006, pp. 205-214.
    https://home.ttic.edu/~yury/papers/unique.pdf

Kolla's spectral enumeration is efficient when the relevant high-eigenvalue
subspace has small dimension. This is an additional structural condition,
not a polynomial-time algorithm for every constraint graph. [9]

[9] Alexandra Kolla. "Spectral Algorithms for Unique Games." Computational
    Complexity 20, 2011, pp. 177-206.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.2300

Arora, Barak, and Steurer give a subexponential-time algorithm for Unique
Games with small completeness error. Subexponential running time is not
polynomial running time, so the distinction matters here. [10]

[10] Sanjeev Arora; Boaz Barak; David Steurer. "Subexponential Algorithms for
    Unique Games and Related Problems." FOCS 2010.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/subexpug/

None of these contradict the theorem, because the theorem lets us
choose epsilon and delta FIRST, then fixes an alphabet K, and only then
is the input allowed to grow. In particular, the alphabet does not
grow with the input size.

The reduction uses exactly this order of quantifiers.

A second distinction is the objective being approximated. Gupta and Talwar
study approximation of the minimum number of violated constraints;
Chlamtac, Konstantin Makarychev, and Yury Makarychev use embeddings to round
nearly satisfiable games. A guarantee for the loss 1-value must be converted
before it can be compared with a guarantee for value. [11] [12]

[11] Anupam Gupta; Kunal Talwar. "Approximating Unique Games." SODA 2006.
    https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/approximating-unique-games/

[12] Eden Chlamtac; Konstantin Makarychev; Yury Makarychev. "How to Play
    Unique Games Using Embeddings." FOCS 2006.
    https://konstantin.makarychev.net/pdf/howtoplay.pdf

Graph assumptions must also be kept explicit. Arora, Khot, Kolla, Steurer,
Tulsiani, and Vishnoi, and subsequently Konstantin and Yury Makarychev,
give algorithms exploiting expansion of the constraint graph. Those results
do not assert the same guarantees on arbitrary graphs. [13] [14]

[13] Sanjeev Arora; Subhash Khot; Alexandra Kolla; David Steurer; Madhur
    Tulsiani; Nisheeth K. Vishnoi. "Unique Games on Expanding Constraint
    Graphs Are Easy." STOC 2008.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/ugexpansion/

[14] Konstantin Makarychev; Yury Makarychev. "How to Play Unique Games on
    Expanders." WAOA 2010; arXiv:0903.0367.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.0367

Later approaches exploit certified small-set expansion or global
hypercontractivity, as in Bafna, Barak, Kothari, Schramm, and Steurer, and
Bafna and Minzer. The proofs below do not assume that the output graphs
come with those algorithmic certificates. [15] [16]

[15] Mitali Bafna; Boaz Barak; Pravesh K. Kothari; Tselil Schramm; David
    Steurer. "Playing Unique Games on Certified Small-Set Expanders." STOC
    2021.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/ugcertsse/

[16] Mitali Bafna; Dor Minzer. "Solving Unique Games over Globally
    Hypercontractive Graphs." CCC 2024.
    https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10554548

The divide-and-conquer approach of Arora, Impagliazzo, Matthews, and Steurer
and the hierarchy-rounding approach of Barak, Raghavendra, and Steurer give
further algorithmic context. Their running-time and structural hypotheses
must be retained when comparing them with a fixed-parameter hardness
statement. [17] [18]

[17] Sanjeev Arora; Russell Impagliazzo; William Matthews; David Steurer.
    "Improved Algorithms for Unique Games via Divide and Conquer." ECCC
    TR10-041, 2010.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2010/041/

[18] Boaz Barak; Prasad Raghavendra; David Steurer. "Rounding Semidefinite
    Programming Hierarchies via Global Correlation." FOCS 2011.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/sdphierarchies/

Here is the quantifier order without shorthand:

  Choose the two numerical tolerances.
  Fix all dimensions, gadget tables, repetition counts, and the alphabet.
  Only then accept a formula of arbitrary size as input.

For example, a term N^k is polynomial in the input size N because k has
already become a constant. It would not give the claimed reduction if k
were subsequently allowed to increase with N. Likewise, a huge finite
precomputation is allowed here only because it depends on the tolerances,
not on the input formula. Section 9.1 checks each dependency explicitly.

Perfect satisfiability is a different regime from near satisfiability.
For a connected graph, try each label at one vertex and propagate it along
a spanning tree. Check the remaining edges for consistency. This decides
whether every constraint can be satisfied in polynomial time for a fixed
alphabet. The theorem therefore uses a positive completeness error, chosen
arbitrarily small but fixed, rather than asserting hardness at value one.

PART 2. THE PROOF IN ONE PAGE
================================================================================

The proof has two halves. The forward half builds an object. The
backward half proves that no cheap strategy can defeat it.

The starting gap is Håstad's three-variable parity theorem. It supplies the
hard input; the distinct-position cleanup in Part 3 is proved here. [19]

[19] Johan Håstad. "Some Optimal Inapproximability Results." Journal of the
    ACM 48, 2001, pp. 798-859, Theorem 5.4.
    https://johanhastad.se/optimalinap.pdf

The structural input is the Grassmann expansion theorem of Khot, Minzer,
and Safra. Part 6 derives the precise matrix-slice statement needed from
that theorem rather than assuming a stronger decoder. [20]

[20] Subhash Khot; Dor Minzer; Muli Safra. "Pseudorandom Sets in Grassmann
    Graph Have Near-Perfect Expansion." ECCC TR18-006, revision 2, 19 May
    2018, Theorem 1.12; journal version: Annals of Mathematics 198, 2023, pp.
    1-92.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2018/006/revision/2/download

The amplification input is Dinur and Steurer's alphabet-independent
projection-game repetition bound. It is used twice, with different base
games, in Parts 8 and 9. [21]

[21] Irit Dinur; David Steurer. "Analytical Approach to Parallel Repetition."
    STOC 2014, Corollary 1.2 and Sections 2.1-2.2.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/productgames.pdf

FORWARD HALF (the reduction)
------------------------------------------------------------

  3SAT
   |
   |  1. Håstad's parity gap
   v
  Weighted 3-variable parity equations over F2:
       YES -> almost all satisfiable
       NO  -> at most 1/2 + xi satisfiable
   |
   |  2. Make the three positions distinct
   v
  Parity equations with distinct variables:
       YES -> almost all satisfiable
       NO  -> at most 4/5 satisfiable
   |
   |  3. Build tables on tuples of k equations
   |     and apply a special LATENT ALPHABET GADGET
   v
  A weighted permutation-constraint game:
       YES -> value very close to 1
       NO  -> value at most 0.99
   |
   |  4. Round weights, subdivide edges
   v
  An unweighted simple bipartite game:
       YES -> value close to 1
       NO  -> value at most 1 - 1/800
   |
   |  5. Take the t-fold parallel product
   v
  Final Unique Games instance:
       YES -> value at least 1 - epsilon
       NO  -> value at most delta

BACKWARD HALF (soundness)
------------------------------------------------------------

We assume, for contradiction, that some labeling makes the matrix test
pass with probability at least 0.99.

  Step A. Fourier comparison.
         High acceptance under the latent noise implies positive
         acceptance under the ordinary uniform rank-one test.

  Step B. Matrix decoding.
         The inverse shortcode theorem plus an erasure argument gives
         two local advice strategies that agree with constant
         probability gamma > 0.

  Step C. Clean coordinates.
         On a NO parity instance, every pair of advice strategies
         agrees with probability at most exp(-c k^(1/3)).

For large enough k:

         exp(-c k^(1/3))  <  gamma,

which contradicts Steps B and C. Therefore no labeling passes the
matrix test with probability 0.99. Hence the game value is at most
0.99 on the NO side.

The two halves meet in Section 9. Parts 3-9 give the detailed reduction;
Parts 10-11 give the consequences and their finite formulation arguments.

What each half must deliver. The construction only needs a constant NO
loss before repetition. Its YES loss must be adjustable independently of
that constant. The latent gadget supplies this asymmetry: reducing p
improves completeness, while its detection probability stays at least 1/8.
Its dimensions may increase, but they are fixed before the formula arrives.

The soundness argument does not decode a globally satisfying assignment
directly from one table. It first produces two legal local response rules
with some positive agreement probability gamma. The clean-coordinate
argument proves that every such pair must have agreement tending to zero
as k grows on a NO instance. A positive constant versus a vanishing bound
is enough; neither gamma nor the required k needs to be numerically small.

================================================================================
PART 3. HÅSTAD'S PARITY GAP: THE HARD INPUT
================================================================================

The starting use of satisfiability and polynomial-time reductions belongs
to the NP-completeness framework established by Cook and Karp. The extra
requirement here is a gap: satisfiable inputs must remain almost fully
satisfiable, while unsatisfiable inputs must lose a fixed fraction. [22] [23]

[22] Stephen A. Cook. "The Complexity of Theorem-Proving Procedures." STOC
    1971, pp. 151-158.
    https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~15455/resources/Cook1971-complx-thm-proof.pdf

[23] Richard M. Karp. "Reducibility among Combinatorial Problems." Complexity
    of Computer Computations, 1972, pp. 85-103.
    https://www2.seas.gwu.edu/~simhaweb/champalg/tsp/papers/KarpOriginal.pdf

Every hardness reduction starts with a problem we already know is
hard. We use 3SAT. From it, Håstad's famous work produces a system of
equations over F2, i.e. over the two numbers {0,1} with addition mod 2.

Each equation involves three positions, possibly repeating a variable:

       x_a + x_b + x_c = b,    where b is 0 or 1.

A weighted parity instance is a finite list of such equations.
Different occurrences have distinct IDs, even if two occurrences use
the same variables. Each occurrence has a positive rational weight.
All weights sum to one. The value of an assignment is the total weight
of satisfied equations. The optimum is the largest possible value.

THEOREM 3.1 (Weighted parity gap)
------------------------------------------------------------
For every fixed rational xi > 0, there is a deterministic polynomial-time
reduction from 3SAT to weighted parity instances, with rational weights
of polynomial binary length, such that:

  YES case:  optimum is at least 1 - xi.
  NO case:   optimum is at most 1/2 + xi.

This is the weighted form of Håstad's parity-gap theorem (2001,
Theorem 5.4): its finite verifier uses a fixed dyadic error d>0,
completeness 1-d and soundness (1+d)/2. [19] Choose d<=xi and enumerate
verifier outcomes with their rational probabilities as occurrence weights.
This is an imported hardness theorem, not an assumption about the input
formula. For a real tolerance one may use a smaller positive rational.

A gap is stronger than ordinary NP-completeness. Arora and Safra and Arora,
Lund, Motwani, Sudan, and Szegedy established the PCP framework that connects
local proof checking to such approximation gaps. Dinur later gave a
combinatorial gap-amplification proof of the PCP theorem. Here the imported
statement is specifically Håstad's parity gap, not an unspecified PCP
with unrecorded parameters. [24] [25] [26] [19]

[24] Sanjeev Arora; Shmuel Safra. "Probabilistic Checking of Proofs: A New
    Characterization of NP." Journal of the ACM 45, 1998, pp. 70-122.
    https://research.ibm.com/publications/probabilistic-checking-of-proofs-a-new-characterization-of-np

[25] Sanjeev Arora; Carsten Lund; Rajeev Motwani; Madhu Sudan; Mario Szegedy.
    "Proof Verification and the Hardness of Approximation Problems." Journal
    of the ACM 45, 1998, pp. 501-555.
    https://people.csail.mit.edu/madhu/papers/1992/almss-journ.pdf

[26] Irit Dinur. "The PCP Theorem by Gap Amplification." Journal of the ACM
    54, 2007, Article 12.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/eccc-reports/2005/TR05-046/

Small soundness by itself does not give uniqueness. Moshkovitz and Raz
construct two-query PCPs with subconstant error, but a projection constraint
can admit several left labels for the same right label. Turning a general
projection game into a Unique Game while preserving the required
completeness is a separate task. [27]

[27] Dana Moshkovitz; Ran Raz. "Two-Query PCP with Subconstant Error." Journal
    of the ACM 57, 2010, Article 29; ECCC TR08-071.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2008/071/

This is our starting point. A satisfiable formula becomes a system
where almost every equation can be satisfied. An unsatisfiable formula
becomes a system where no assignment does much better than random.

Why a parity gap is useful. One parity equation has four satisfying
triples, and choosing its first two bits fixes the third. This supplies
the two local coordinates per block used in Part 5. The soundness bound
is about every global assignment simultaneously. It does not say that a
particular equation is hard to satisfy in isolation.

Why weights are harmless at this stage. A finite verifier is a finite
probability experiment. Listing each outcome and its exact rational
probability gives a weighted instance with exactly the same acceptance
probability for every assignment. No sampling approximation is used here.
Part 9 converts the resulting weighted game to an unweighted graph.

We then perform a small cleanup step. Some equations might use the
same variable twice. That is inconvenient. We clone every variable one
hundred times and replace the occurrence by a small weighted family of
occurrences using clones. After this cleanup:

LEMMA 3.2 (distinct positions)
------------------------------------------------------------
From a weighted parity instance we can construct, in polynomial time,
another weighted parity instance in which the three variables of every
equation are distinct, and:

  YES case:  if OPT(E) >= 1 - xi, then OPT(E') >= 1 - xi.
  NO case:   if OPT(E) <= 1/2 + xi and 0 < xi < 1/100,
             then OPT(E') <= 4/5.

The exact upper-bound formula is

        optimum(E') <= (1 + optimum(E))/2 + 3/100.

If optimum(E) <= 1/2 + xi and xi < 1/100, the right-hand side is at
most 4/5. That is the only NO-side fact used in the rest of the proof.
Proof of Lemma 3.2. Replace each variable v by 100 clones (v,c).
For each original occurrence independently choose its three clone indices,
conditioned on distinct clones whenever two positions refer to the same
underlying variable. Enumerate all permitted choices with their conditional
weights. There are at most 100^3 choices per occurrence. Labeling all clones
of v with its original bit preserves every original equation value.

For the reverse bound fix arbitrary clone labels and put
 t_v=(1/100)sum_c (-1)^{x_(v,c)}.
Before conditioning on distinctness, an occurrence has satisfaction
probability (1+(-1)^{b_e}t_{v_1}t_{v_2}t_{v_3})/2. Round each t_v to
its majority bit, breaking zero ties arbitrarily. For every equation this
rounded assignment violates, the signed product is nonpositive, including
when a bias is zero; hence its probability is at most 1/2. Other equations
have probability at most one. The weighted mean is at most
(1+OPT(E))/2. A union bound puts the forbidden-collision probability at
most 3/100. For an event D and a payoff Y in [0,1],
 |E[Y|D]-E[Y]|<=Pr[not D],
as follows by splitting E[Y] over D and its complement. Applying this
within each occurrence gives the claimed additional 3/100. All new
conditional denominators have constant size apart from the old weights,
so the construction has polynomial size and bit complexity.

The reverse inequality does not assume that clones of one variable agree.
Their bias t_v records how an arbitrary clone labeling behaves under a
uniform clone choice. Majority rounding supplies one actual assignment to
the original variables, so the total weight of equations it satisfies is
at most OPT(E). On each of its violated equations, the independent-clone
success probability is at most one half. Conditioning away collisions
then changes that success probability by at most the collision probability.
These are separate losses: the factor (1+OPT(E))/2 comes from rounding;
the additive 3/100 comes only from requiring distinct clones.

For the rest of the paper, "parity instance" means this cleaned-up
instance with distinct positions. Its "ordinary equation-versus-variable
game" chooses an equation and one of its three positions. The equation
side answers with a satisfying triple; the variable side answers with a
bit. If the optimum is at most 4/5, this ordinary game has value at
most 14/15. Indeed, any variable-side strategy is a global assignment.
On each equation it violates, every satisfying equation-side triple differs
at some position, giving rejection probability at least 1/3. Thus
 value(G_E)<=1-(1-OPT(E))/3<=14/15.
Distinct variable IDs within an equation ensure the question pair fixes
its projection. Repeated occurrences remain separate questions. This small
constant gap will be amplified later by parallel repetition.

================================================================================
PART 4. THE LATENT ALPHABET GADGET
================================================================================

This is the new tool that makes completeness near one possible.

4.1. Why ordinary tests only give completeness near one half
------------------------------------------------------------

Testing algebraic structure through a few correlated queries has a long
history. Blum, Luby, and Rubinfeld developed self-testing and self-correction;
Bellare, Coppersmith, Håstad, Kiwi, and Sudan sharpened the characteristic-two
linearity test. Their tests relate acceptance to closeness to a linear
function. The present gadget instead needs a nonlinear output map that is
stable under one specially chosen additive noise law. [28] [29]

[28] Manuel Blum; Michael Luby; Ronitt Rubinfeld. "Self-Testing/Correcting
    with Applications to Numerical Problems." Journal of Computer and System
    Sciences 47, 1993, pp. 549-595.
    https://people.csail.mit.edu/ronitt/papers/selftestcorrect.pdf

[29] Mihir Bellare; Don Coppersmith; Johan Håstad; Marcos Kiwi; Madhu Sudan.
    "Linearity Testing in Characteristic Two." IEEE Transactions on
    Information Theory 42, 1996, pp. 1781-1795.
    https://johanhastad.se/figureless.pdf

Higher-order tests and their decoding through uniformity and influences
were developed by Samorodnitsky and Trevisan. That work is relevant background
for the distinction between linear and nonlinear structure; Lemma 4.1 below
is proved by the explicit quadratic recursion, not imported from those
testing results. [30]

[30] Alex Samorodnitsky; Luca Trevisan. "Gowers Uniformity, Influence of
    Variables, and PCPs." SIAM Journal on Computing 39, 2009, pp. 323-360;
    ECCC TR05-116.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/eccc-reports/2005/TR05-116/index.html

Imagine the game wants to ask about a large binary vector z. The most
natural test is to add a random rank-one matrix:

       M  ->  M + a l^T,

and check whether the answer stays the same. Here a and l are random
binary vectors. For a nonzero intended answer z,

       (M + a l^T) z  =  Mz + a (l^T z).

But l^T z is a fair random bit. Therefore:

       Pr[l^T z = 0] = 1/2.

So even the ideal honest answer satisfies the equality test only with
probability about 1/2. That is not good enough for a Unique Games
reduction. We need failure probability arbitrarily small.

More exactly, if a is uniform in K and z is nonzero, equality holds when
l^T z=0 or when l^T z=1 and a=0. Its probability is

       1/2 + 1/(2|K|).

Thus enlarging K does not make the honest equality test nearly perfect:
it drives this probability toward one half. The issue is the test's
completeness, not the ability to write its constraints as permutations.

4.2. The gadget
------------------------------------------------------------

We invent an alphabet V with a distinguished subspace K and a
nonlinear map C : V -> K. The map is designed so that replacing x by
C(x) is stable under its noise law. More precisely, a sampled additive
perturbation usually
leaves C unchanged, but a sufficiently rich family of linear observations
still detects that perturbation. No Hamming-weight or metric notion of
"small error" is being assumed.

FORMAL LEMMA 4.1 (latent alphabet gadget)
------------------------------------------------------------
For every small p > 0 there is an integer r_* with the following
property. For every sufficiently large ell, there exist:

  * a finite binary vector space V,
  * an identified subspace K = F_2^ell,
  * a map C : V -> K,
  * a rational noise distribution mu on V,

such that:

  (1) TRANSLATION EQUIVARIANCE:
     For every x in V and every k in K,

         C(x + k) = C(x) + k.

  (2) SMALL CHANGE:
     For independent uniform x in V and noise a drawn from mu,

         Pr[ C(x + a) is not equal to C(x) ]  <=  p.

  (3) LINEAR DETECTION:
     For every linear subspace G of the dual space V* whose
     restriction to K has dimension at least r_*,

         Pr_{a ~ mu}[ some g in G has g(a) not 0 ]  >=  1/8.

For rational p the threshold and finite data are effectively constructible.
Sections 4.3-4.5 describe the construction; Section 4.6 proves every part,
including that r_* is fixed before ell and that the construction terminates.

In words: random noise usually leaves the nonlinear answer exactly
unchanged, but any sufficiently rich family of linear tests catches the
noise with constant probability.

Property (1) is why translations can be used as Unique Game
constraints. Property (2) is why completeness can be nearly one.
Property (3) is why cheating strategies still leave a detectable
signal.

A useful coordinate picture. Choose a complement W so V=K direct-sum W.
Equivariance forces C(k,w)=k+c(w) for the function c(w)=C(0,w). If the
noise is a=(b,z), its effect on the output is

       b + c(w+z) + c(w).

The nonlinear term can cancel the K-component b for most w under the
chosen noise law. A nonzero noise vector lying entirely in K could not do
this: equivariance would change the answer by that vector on every input.
This explains why the larger latent space V is needed even though the
actual labels belong only to K.

The detection property concerns a family of linear functionals at once.
If G|_K has rank r, it contains r independent ways of observing the output
translation coordinates. The property says that, once r reaches r_*, the
noise cannot hide from all those observations with probability above 7/8.
It does not require each individual functional to detect noise often.

4.3. Where the gadget comes from
------------------------------------------------------------

The construction starts with a quadratic block. Over a sufficiently
large finite field F = F_{2^d}, define:

       Q(x1,x2,x3) = (x2 x3, x1 x3, x1 x2).

The block answer is:

       C_blk(x,y) = y + Q(x).

The vertical translations are y -> y + k, and they become output
translations. The noise is chosen from special binary linear subspaces where
the quadratic cross-terms cancel.

The relevant cancellation is the characteristic-two polarization
identity. The expression

       Q(x+a) + Q(x) + Q(a)

is bilinear in x and a. For a in the field line Fv, its value lies
in the field hyperplane {w : v dot w = 0}.

4.4. Why the rank stays detectable
------------------------------------------------------------

We need both small-change and detection simultaneously. The proof
uses a harmonic potential:

       H_r = 1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/r.

A generic family of linear observations loses at most one dimension
when moving down the construction, and a uniformly chosen update loses
dimension with probability proportional to 3 r / (number of lines).
But the harmonic function turns that into an expected loss of only a
constant per level.

This is the heart of Lemma 4.1: a tailored random walk on ranks that
decays slowly enough to survive until the leaf while the nonlinear
change probability decays exponentially.

4.5. Quotient and enlargement
------------------------------------------------------------

Finally, two cosmetic but necessary steps:

  * We quotient out shifts that cause the same output translation, so
    the output translations are exactly the subspace K.

  * We enlarge the small output alphabet B to any larger K by using
    many random injections B -> K. This ensures the rank threshold r_*
    does not grow with ell.

The following construction proves Lemma 4.1 and supplies the terminating
deterministic procedure for rational p. Enumeration alone would not
establish existence or termination.

4.6. Full construction and proof of Lemma 4.1
------------------------------------------------------------
All linearity and dimensions below are over F_2 unless explicitly stated
otherwise. For a finite vector space E, E* is its space of linear maps
to F_2. Restricting a family of characters means taking the image of that
family under the restriction map, not its intersection with another dual.

(a) One quadratic block.
Choose an integer r_0 later. Put H_0=0 and H_r=sum_{j=1}^r 1/j. Let
F=F_{2^d}, q=2^d, and X=B=F^3, each of binary dimension b=3d. Identify
their binary duals with F^3 by the nondegenerate pairing

       (z,x) -> Tr_{F/F_2}(z dot x).

The trace satisfies Tr(t^2)=Tr(t), and squaring is an automorphism of F.

There are two scalar fields in this block. X and B have dimension three
over F but dimension b=3d over F_2. The line A=Fv has binary dimension d;
the hyperplane D_A has binary dimension 2d. Characters are F_2-valued,
not F-valued. The trace pairing is what represents every binary character
by a vector z in F^3 without confusing those two notions of dimension.
Use Q and C_blk from Section 4.3. Its polarization D(x,a)=Q(x+a)+Q(x)+Q(a)
has coordinates x_2 a_3+a_2 x_3, x_1 a_3+a_1 x_3, x_1 a_2+a_1 x_2.
For a field line A=Fv with v nonzero define

       D_A={w in B : v dot w=0},
       U_A={(a,Q(a)+w) : a in A, w in D_A},
       theta=1/(q^2+q+1).

Neither space depends on the choice of generator v. When a is a multiple
of v, v dot D(x,a)=0 because each term occurs twice. If a and a' both
belong to A, D(a,a')=0. Thus Q restricted to A is binary linear, U_A
is a binary subspace of dimension d+2d=b, and the trace annihilator of
D_A is A. The spaces U_A span X+B: for coordinate lines Fe_i they
contain (Fe_i,0), and their vertical hyperplanes together span 0+B.
There are q^2+q+1 field lines.

An update (a,Q(a)+w) changes C_blk by w+D(x,a). For a uniform update in
all of U_A, this difference is uniform in D_A conditional on x,a, so
it is nonzero with probability 1-q^(-2). The zero update has probability
q^(-3) and never changes the output. Consequently, for independent
uniform (x,y) and uniform nonzero update in U_A, the change probability is

       (1-q^(-2))/(1-q^(-3)) = 1-theta.                 (4.6.1)

Next take a binary subspace S of B* and a binary-linear assignment of
extensions z -> (gamma_z,z) in X*+B*. Write
 gamma_z(x)=Tr(g(z) dot x), where g:S->F^3 is binary linear.
Let kappa_{A,gamma} send z to the restriction of this extension to U_A.
For z nonzero, the kernel criterion is

       kappa_{A,gamma}(z)=0
       iff A=Fz and z dot g(z)=sqrt(z_1 z_2 z_3).       (4.6.2)

Indeed, annihilating 0+D_A first requires z in A. On the remaining
vectors (tz,Q(tz)), the value is
 Tr(t g(z) dot z+t^2 z_1 z_2 z_3).
The identity Tr(t^2 c)=Tr(t sqrt(c)) and nondegeneracy of trace give
(4.6.2), including its converse.

The kernel test has two logically separate requirements. First, a lifted
character must vanish on every vertical vector (0,w) with w in D_A. That
forces its output coefficient z onto the line A. Second, it must vanish
on the graph vectors (tz,Q(tz)); this is exactly the scalar alignment
equation in (4.6.2). Thus a particular nonzero z can cause a rank loss for
at most one field line, namely Fz. Genericity will limit how many z can
cause such a loss for the same extension family.

(b) Generic character spaces exist, uniformly over their extensions.
Call an r-dimensional binary subspace S of F^3 generic when distinct
nonzero elements are not proportional over F and, for EVERY binary
linear map g:S->F^3, the alignment equation in (4.6.2) has at most
3r nonzero solutions in S. For generic S, restricting any fixed family
of extensions to a uniform U_A loses at most one rank, and loses it
with probability at most 3r theta. If r_A is the surviving rank, then

       E_A[H_r-H_{r_A}] <= 3 theta.                   (4.6.3)

For arbitrary S the corresponding bounds are
 Pr_A[r_A<r] <= (2^r-1)theta and H_r-H_{r_A} <= H_r.
These follow directly from (4.6.2); no independence of g and S is needed.

Why harmonic potential rather than rank? On a generic r-space, a bad line
can kill only one binary dimension because distinct nonzero vectors of S
are not field-proportional. The potential loss when that happens is
H_r-H_{r-1}=1/r. Multiplying by the loss probability at most 3r theta gives
3 theta. The dependence on r cancels. A direct expected-rank estimate
would instead carry a factor r, which is too costly over the required
number of levels.

We prove that for each fixed r, a uniform r-dimensional binary subspace
of F^3 is generic with probability tending to one as d grows. Sample
independent uniform x_{ij} in F, i=1,2,3 and j=1,...,r, and set
 z_i(t)=sum_j x_{ij}t_j for t in F_2^r.
The map t->z(t) fails to be injective with probability at most
(2^r-1)q^(-3). Conditional on injectivity its image is a uniform
r-dimensional subspace. Two distinct nonzero binary vectors are also
linearly independent over F. Their images are independent uniform
vectors of F^3. The probability of proportionality is O(q^(-2)), so a
union bound handles the first genericity condition.

For the second condition fix a set E of 3r+1 distinct nonzero binary
inputs, if such a set exists. Alignment at every t in E for some g
is equivalent to membership of the column

       c_t=sqrt(z_1(t)z_2(t)z_3(t))

in the column space of the |E|-by-3r matrix
 M_{t,(i,j)}=t_j z_i(t).
Its 3r unknown coefficients are the coordinates of the values of g on a
binary basis. Thus this single condition already covers all choices of g.

This is where the word EVERY in the definition of genericity is handled.
Choose the binary basis z(e_1),...,z(e_r). A binary-linear g is completely
determined by the three field coordinates of g(z(e_j)) for each j, giving
3r unknown field elements. Asking for alignment on the selected 3r+1
inputs is one overdetermined linear system in those unknowns. There is no
union bound over the q^(3r) possible maps g. Instead, the proof shows that
the entire system is inconsistent except on a low-probability algebraic
exceptional set.

Temporarily let the x_{ij} be algebraically independent indeterminates
and let L=F_2(x_{ij}). The r^3 elements
 sqrt(x_{1j}x_{2k}x_{3l}) are linearly independent over L: clearing
denominators reduces any dependence to a polynomial identity in the
square roots of the indeterminates, and these monomials have different
exponent parities while coefficients in L have even exponent parities.
The coefficients of these independent elements in c are the vectors
 v_{jkl}=(t_j t_k t_l)_{t in E}.

If c belonged to col(M) over an algebraic closure of L, each L-valued
row annihilating M would annihilate c and hence every v_{jkl}. Therefore
all v_{jkl} would lie in col_L(M). Let V_h be the evaluation span on E
of positive-degree monomials of degree at most h in the binary t_j.
Repeated indices in v_{jkl} give degrees one and two as well as three.
Since every column of M has degree at most two, we would obtain

       V_3 subset col_L(M) subset V_2 subset V_3.

The resulting common space contains degree-one monomials and is closed
under multiplication by each t_j. It contains all positive-degree
monomials. Their evaluations span L^E: the indicator of any nonzero
binary point is a product of factors t_j or 1+t_j with at least one
factor t_j, and therefore has no constant term. This contradicts
rank(M)<=3r<|E|. So the claimed membership is impossible symbolically.

The symbolic contradiction can be read in four steps. The square roots
sqrt(x_{1j}x_{2k}x_{3l}) carry distinct exponent-parity patterns, so an
L-linear relation between them is impossible. Consequently any row
annihilating M must also annihilate every degree-three evaluation vector.
This puts V_3 inside col(M). Since the columns of M already belong to
V_2, it follows that V_2=V_3=col(M).

Now multiplication by any coordinate t_j sends V_2 into V_3, which is the
same space. Starting with degree-one monomials and multiplying repeatedly
therefore gives every positive-degree monomial. On a set E of nonzero
binary points, those monomials span all point indicators. The common
space would have dimension |E|=3r+1, whereas M has only 3r columns.
This is the contradiction. The use of nonzero inputs is essential to the
point-indicator argument: their indicators have no constant term.

Here is an explicit finite-field consequence. Put h=rank_L(M). Square
all entries of M and c; the appended column now has polynomial entries
z_1(t)z_2(t)z_3(t). There is a nonzero (h+1)-minor of the augmented
squared matrix, of degree at most 2h+3<=6r+3. Every unaugmented
(h+1)-minor is identically zero. Membership after specialization therefore
forces this particular nonzero polynomial to vanish. A nonzero polynomial
of total degree D in independent uniform field elements vanishes with
probability at most D/q. This elementary bound follows by induction on
the number of variables, applying the univariate root bound to its
leading coefficient and then to the last variable. [31] [32] Our membership
probability is consequently at most (6r+3)/q. There are only finitely
many possible E for fixed r. A union bound and conditioning on
injectivity prove the assertion. When 2^r-1<=3r there are no such E
and the alignment condition is automatic.

[31] Jacob T. Schwartz. "Fast Probabilistic Algorithms for Verification of
    Polynomial Identities." Journal of the ACM 27, 1980, pp. 701-717.
    https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~cs787-1/Schwartz1980.pdf

[32] Richard Zippel. "Probabilistic Algorithms for Sparse Polynomials."
    EUROSAM 1979, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 72, pp. 216-226.
    https://www.cecm.sfu.ca/~monaganm/teaching/TopicsinCA15/zippel79.pdf

Squaring is used only to turn the obstruction into a polynomial over the
base field. It preserves whether the linear system has a solution,
because Frobenius is a field automorphism after specialization. A nonzero
augmented minor certifies that c cannot be in the column space. If a
specialization did make the system solvable, that minor would have to
vanish. This remains true even if specialization lowers the rank of M.
The exceptional probability is therefore bounded by the degree of one
nonzero polynomial divided by q, followed by a finite union over E.

Choose a rational e>0 with e<1 and H_{r_0}2^{r_0}e<=1. The assertion
just proved lets us fix d with b>=r_0 such that, at every rank
1<=r<=r_0, at most an e fraction of binary r-subspaces of B* are
nongeneric. Fix one generic r_0-subspace. These choices depend only
on r_0, not on the eventual ell.

(c) The recursive map and its additive noise.
Let I be the finite set of ALL pairs (A,J), where A is a field line
and J:B->U_A is a binary linear isomorphism. The number of choices of
J is the same for every A. We construct input spaces P_t, shift
subspaces R_t, surjections lambda_t:R_t->B, and maps C_t:P_t->B
satisfying

       C_t(u+h)=C_t(u)+lambda_t(h)  for h in R_t.       (4.6.4)

At height zero all spaces are B and both maps are the identity.
At positive height take P_t=product_{(A,J) in I} P_{t-1}, and set

       (x,y)=sum_{A,J} J C_{t-1}(u_{AJ}),
       C_t(u)=y+Q(x),
       R_t={h in product R_{t-1}:
              sum_{A,J}J lambda_{t-1}(h_{AJ}) lies in 0+B}.

For h in R_t let lambda_t(h) be the B-component of that sum. The
induction hypothesis proves (4.6.4). The aggregate of the child shifts
is onto X+B because the U_A span it, so lambda_t is onto B. In
particular C_t of a uniform input is uniform in B: its distribution is
invariant under every output translation.

Let xi_0 be a uniform element of B. At height t>0 choose one uniform
child (A,J), place an independent xi_{t-1} there, and put zero in the
other children. Equivalently select a uniform leaf and add a uniform
B-symbol there. All choices are finite and the noise probabilities are
rational. For an independent uniform input u put
 p_t=Pr[C_t(u+xi_t)!=C_t(u)]. We claim

       p_t=(1-q^(-3))(1-theta)^t.                     (4.6.5)

The base case is immediate. For the induction, the selected child's
output difference Delta has a law independent of its index, since all
child functions and inner noises are identical. For any fixed inner
perturbation a, its difference is unchanged by shifting the input by
h in R_{t-1}, whereas the initial child output is translated by
lambda_{t-1}(h). Thus conditional on a and Delta, the initial output
is still uniform in B. The other initial child outputs remain
independently uniform. Hence the entire parent aggregate is uniform
conditional on (A,J,Delta). Conditional on A and Delta!=0, J is uniform,
so J Delta is uniform in U_A minus {0}. The aggregate is independent
of this perturbation. Applying (4.6.1) gives p_t=p_{t-1}(1-theta),
which proves (4.6.5). This conditional-uniformity check is necessary;
a marginally uniform update by itself would not justify the recursion.

The recursion separates the current value from the change in that value.
Shifting a child input by an element of R_{t-1} translates both its old and
new outputs by the same amount. Their difference Delta is unchanged.
Because lambda_{t-1} is onto B, conditioning on Delta still leaves the
old child output uniform on B. This is stronger than ordinary uniformity.

At the parent, the independent uniform child outputs pass through the
maps J and are summed. The images U_A span X direct-sum B, so this sum is
uniform on the whole parent input space. Conditional on a nonzero Delta,
the fresh isomorphism J makes the parent's update uniform among nonzero
vectors of U_A. The parent therefore multiplies the chance of a child
change by exactly 1-theta. Iterating gives exponential decay in the depth.

(d) Detection of every full family of lifted output characters.
A lift of S<=B* to P_t is a binary-linear family z->psi_z in P_t*
with psi_z restricted to R_t equal to z composed with lambda_t. It
is full when S=B*. For any lift at height t>0 there is ONE binary
linear map gamma:S->X*, common to all children, such that the
characters induced on a child's output space are

       phi_{AJ}(z)=J* ((gamma_z,z)|_{U_A}).            (4.6.6)

To prove this, a shift in ker(lambda_{t-1}) supported on one child
is a parent shift with zero output, so every psi_z vanishes on it.
The child restriction therefore factors through lambda_{t-1}. On the
space of child output translations, subtract z applied to the vertical
component of their aggregate from the sum of these induced characters.
The difference vanishes whenever the aggregate's X-component vanishes,
by the defining parent lift identity. It therefore factors through
that X-component, which is onto X. Its unique factor is gamma_z.
Uniqueness also makes gamma linear in z, proving (4.6.6).

Why must the same gamma work for every child? A parent lift respects all
shifts whose aggregate horizontal component is zero, including shifts
that cancel between different children. Independent choices of horizontal
extensions in different children would generally fail this requirement.
Factoring through the single aggregate X-component enforces one common
gamma. The genericity estimate can then be applied to one fixed extension
family before the next line A is sampled.

After choosing a child, let S' be the image of phi_{AJ}. Choose a
linear section S'->S and restrict the corresponding psi_z to the
child input. This is a lift of S' at height t-1. Fix section choices
deterministically using only the path so far. Every surviving functional
is then the restriction of an original functional to the chosen subtree.

Take depth s=floor(H_{r_0}/(8 theta)). Start with an arbitrary full
lift on P_s and restrict its domain to the generic r_0-space fixed
above. Let S_j and r_j be its domain and rank after j random descent
steps; once the rank is zero, keep it zero. Given the previous path,
S_j and gamma_j are fixed. Choose A_j first. The image
 E_j=im(kappa_{A_j,gamma_j}) has fixed rank before J_j is revealed.
The independent uniform isomorphism J_j makes S_{j+1}=J_j*E_j a
uniform binary subspace of B* of that rank. Its probability of being
nongeneric, when nonzero, is at most e. The next lift can depend on
J_j; this causes no problem because genericity bounds EVERY extension.

The order of exposure matters. At a descent step the current S_j and its
extension gamma_j are fixed by the past. Sampling A_j determines the rank
that remains. Only afterward does the independent random J_j rotate that
remaining space into a uniformly random binary subspace of B*. The next
extension may depend on everything revealed so far. Genericity was proved
uniformly over all extensions precisely so this dependence is allowed.
No assumption of independence between the next space and its extension
is being made.

Let B_j indicate a positive-rank nongeneric S_j and put
 Delta_j=H_{r_j}-H_{r_{j+1}}. From (4.6.3) and the arbitrary-space
bound, conditional on the previous path,

       E[Delta_j | previous path]
       <= 3 theta + H_{r_0}2^{r_0} theta B_j.

Initially B_0=0. At each subsequent step E B_j<=e by the fresh
isomorphism argument. Thus E Delta_j<=4 theta at every step, and

       E[H_{r_0}-H_{r_s}] <= 4s theta <= H_{r_0}/2.

Extinction costs the entire potential H_{r_0}, so its probability is
at most 1/2. At a surviving leaf the lift is a nonzero character space
of B itself. An independent uniform B-symbol is detected with
probability 1-2^{-r_s}>=1/2. Leaf detection implies detection by an
original lifted character. EVERY full lift therefore detects xi_s
with probability at least 1/4.

The potential estimate controls extinction, not merely average final rank.
If rank reaches zero, the loss H_{r_0}-H_{r_s} equals H_{r_0}. On every
other path the loss is nonnegative. An expected loss of at most half the
initial potential therefore puts extinction probability at most one half.
Conditional on survival, a uniform leaf symbol escapes the common kernel
of the surviving nonzero character space with probability at least one
half. Multiplying these two lower bounds gives 1/4.

(e) Turn the shifts into a subspace, then enlarge the output.
The kernel N_s=ker(lambda_s) is taken inside R_s<=P_s. By (4.6.4),
C_s is invariant under N_s. Form V_0=P_s/N_s and let Cbar:V_0->B be
the descended map. R_s/N_s is an identified copy of B inside V_0,
and Cbar(x+b')=Cbar(x)+b'. Push the noise xi_s to a law nu on V_0.
Uniform input pushes to uniform input, so its change probability is p_s.

For any G_0<=V_0* whose restrictions fill B*, choose a linear section
B*->G_0 and pull it back to P_s. This is a full lift. The preceding
argument proves that G_0 detects nu with probability at least 1/4.

Given the requested rational p>0, choose an integer m>=0 with
2^{-m}<=p, and choose r_0 with H_{r_0}>=8(m+1). Complete the choices
above and use depth s. Since theta<=1 and s>=H_{r_0}/(8theta)-1,

       p_s <= exp(-theta s)
           <= exp(1-H_{r_0}/8) <= exp(-m) <= 2^{-m} <= p.

Set r_*=b+2, BEFORE choosing ell. For any ell>=b+2, put K=F_2^ell
and let L range over all binary linear injections B->K. On
P=product_L V_0 define

       Ctilde((x_L))=sum_L L Cbar(x_L).

The shift space T=product_L B acts by the surjective map
 Lambda((b_L))=sum_L Lb_L into K. It is surjective because every
nonzero vector of K occurs in some injection's image. Quotient P by
the kernel of Lambda INSIDE T, obtaining V=P/ker(Lambda). Ctilde
descends to C:V->K, and T/ker(Lambda) is the required identified K.
This proves equivariance. The noise chooses a uniform L, updates only
that copy by nu, and passes to V. Its probabilities are rational. Since
L is injective, the output changes precisely when the selected copy's
Cbar changes; uniform inputs pass to uniform quotient inputs. The
small-change probability is p_s<=p.

Finally fix G<=V* and S=G|_K of dimension r>=r_*=b+2. Pull G back to
P and restrict it to copy L; call this family G_L. Its restriction to
that copy's B is exactly L*S. This follows by evaluating a shift b'
in that copy: its image in K is Lb'. Failure of L*S to fill B* means
that some nonzero b' in B has Lb' in S-perp. For each fixed b', Lb'
is uniform among nonzero vectors of K. Therefore

       Pr_L[L*S != B*]
       <= (2^b-1)(2^{ell-r}-1)/(2^ell-1)
       <= (2^b-1)2^{-r} <= 1/4.

On every remaining copy, the base detection bound is 1/4. Detection
by G_L is detection by G on noise supported in that copy. Overall
probability is at least (3/4)(1/4)=3/16>=1/8. This proves all three
properties of Lemma 4.1 for every ell>=r_*.

The two quotients remove only shifts on which the output is invariant.
That is why C descends to each quotient. A uniform vector stays uniform
under a surjective linear quotient map because all fibers have the same
size. Likewise, pushing a finite noise law through the quotient changes
its representation, not the probability of an output change.

The enlargement step is what fixes the quantifier order. The base output
B has dimension b determined by p. Setting r_*=b+2 now fixes the detection
threshold once and for all. For any later ell, a random injection L is
unlikely to put a nonzero vector of B into the annihilator of a rank-r
observation space: the union bound is at most (2^b-1)2^(-r). Therefore most
copies retain full base detection, regardless of how large ell becomes.

(f) Effectiveness.
The choices of m,r_0,e are rational/integer searches. For successive
d with 3d>=r_0, construct F_{2^d}, enumerate its binary subspaces of
ranks 1,...,r_0 and all binary-linear g, and test genericity exactly.
The proof in (b) guarantees a d for which every nongeneric fraction is
at most e; testing those fractions is finite exact arithmetic. Choose
the first such d and a generic r_0-space. All remaining isomorphisms,
products, kernels, quotients, injections, function tables, and pushforward
probabilities are finite enumerations and binary linear algebra.
This proves termination, not merely the existence of a search procedure.
Its cost depends on p and ell only, never on the input 3SAT formula.

The role of the exhaustive search is limited. The genericity argument
proves that a successful field size exists; the search merely finds one.
Every test at a candidate field size involves a finite list and exact
arithmetic. Once p and ell are fixed, all resulting tables and spaces can
be included as constant data in the reduction. A search without the
existence argument would not establish that the algorithm halts.

For an arbitrary real p>0, use the construction for a positive rational
p'<=p. The effectiveness assertion concerns rational p.

PART 5. BUILDING THE OUTER MATRIX GAME

The intermediate Unique Games instance has vertices representing
equivalence classes of tables, rather than the original parity variables.
The equivalence relation is defined in Section 5.2.

The long-code approach to hardness encodes a local assignment by all its
possible evaluations; Bellare, Goldreich, and Sudan developed its use in
PCPs and nonapproximability. Barak, Gopalan, Håstad, Meka, Raghavendra, and
Steurer introduced the shorter-code approach. The tables below use their
own explicit finite linear-map indexing, so their size and sharing rules
must be checked directly. [33] [34]

    https://people.seas.harvard.edu/~madhusudan/papers/1995/bgs-journ.pdf

The purpose of sharing is the same consistency requirement that drives PCP
composition: local representations must support compatible answers on
common information. Assignment testers of Dinur and Reingold and decodable

and folding construction in Section 5.2 is the specific mechanism used
here; those frameworks are not being invoked as a substitute for its
proof. [35] [36]

5.1. Tables
------------------------------------------------------------

Let U = (e_1, ..., e_k) be an ordered tuple of k parity equations,
sampled with replacement. Each block has its own local answer coordinates,
even when IDs repeat. A table on U is an affine map from the satisfying
answers of those equations to the latent space V.

Each equation has four satisfying triples:
   (x,y, b_equation + x + y).

So an equation answer is determined by its first two bits. Adding a
homogeneous coordinate t_0 = 1 gives the answer space dimension
m = 1 + 2k: one coordinate for the constant and two slopes per
equation.

Write L_U = F_2^m. A table P is represented by a matrix in Hom(L_U, V).
In particular, P z means: evaluate the affine table at the valid answer z.
Fix a linear splitting V = K + W (a direct sum), and write P = (M,T)
for its K-valued and W-valued components. These spaces are fixed before k.

For one equation x_1+x_2+x_3=b, the homogeneous answer vector is
(t_0,x_1,x_2), with x_3=b t_0+x_1+x_2. Actual answers have t_0=1.
For k equations there is ONE shared homogeneous coordinate and two local
bit coordinates per block, hence m=1+2k. Homogenization turns every affine
table into an ordinary linear matrix, so evaluations and projections can
be written as matrix products.

A table is not itself a claimed assignment to the parity instance. It is
a linear map that can be evaluated at many possible local answers. A game
label gives one K-valued response associated with that table's key. The
soundness proof must recover assignment information from those responses.

5.2. Two key identifications: keys and folding
------------------------------------------------------------

Different tables can secretly represent the same information. We first
simplify each block.

A slope pair (a_i,d_i) can:

  * have rank two, so the table truly depends on both equation bits;
  * have rank zero, so the block can be omitted;
  * have rank one, so the table depends only on one variable or one
    actual answer bit.

For rank one, (a_i,d_i) is (v,0), (0,v), or (v,v), with v nonzero.
The last case uses the third actual bit z_i = b_{e_i} t_0 + x_i + y_i;
replacing v(x_i+y_i) by v z_i requires adding b_{e_i} v to the intercept.
The RETAINED coordinates form the support, keeping block indices, tags,
and IDs. The REDUCED table is the unique linear map on that support.
The KEY is the support plus the reduced table, including its corrected
intercept. A singleton block of nonzero slope retains its actual-bit
coordinate and variable ID; a zero-slope singleton disappears. Block
indices remain distinct even when variable or occurrence IDs repeat.
For a support, let rho retain t_0 and those coordinates and discard the
others. It is surjective, since coordinates in different blocks are local
copies. The identities above give P=P_reduced rho, which also proves the
uniqueness of the reduced table. An empty support still retains t_0.

The rank-one case is worth checking explicitly. If the slopes in a block
are (v,v), its contribution is v x_i+v y_i. Since the third actual bit is
z_i=b_e t_0+x_i+y_i, the same contribution is v z_i+b_e v t_0. Keeping
z_i but forgetting the added intercept term would change the table when
b_e=1. The key records the corrected intercept as well as the surviving
coordinate and its ID, so it represents the same map, not just the same
pattern of nonzero slopes.

The block index is retained even if two blocks refer to the same global
variable. Their answer coordinates are still separate local copies.
Consistency with one global assignment is a property of the honest
labeling, not an identification imposed on the local vector space.

A crucial compatibility: if a question O is obtained from U by
projecting some equation blocks down to singleton variables, then a
table on O and its pullback to U have the SAME KEY. Thus one vertex
label can serve both questions consistently. To check the intercept,
a third-position singleton of slope v pulls back with the extra term
b_e v t_0; reducing that pulled-back rank-one block adds the same term
again, so the two corrections cancel in characteristic two. Retained
equations reduce identically on both sides, and zero slopes disappear
on both. Hence the entire reduced maps, not just their slopes, agree.
Take the key universe over all tables on all equation tuples. Every
projected key lies in it by pullback from a compatible tuple.

Folding is then immediate. Adding a constant h in K to the intercept
changes the key by h. We group each key with all of its K-translates
into one vertex, represented by the unique key whose reduced intercept
has zero K-component. A labeling of vertices defines, for any table P:

        F(P) = label(representative key) + c(P),

where c(P) is the K-component of the key's intercept. This has the
exact translation property:

        F(P + h e0^T) = F(P) + h.

Adding h changes only the removed intercept component, so the action
on keys is free. The same-key identity also gives, for every permitted
projection pi and table P' on O,

        F_U(P' pi)=F_O(P').                            (sharing)

This is how the final Unique Game constraints become translations of
the alphabet K.

Sharing and folding solve different problems. Sharing identifies a table
with a pullback that represents exactly the same reduced map. Folding
identifies tables that differ by a constant K-translation, while recording
that translation in the restored value F. The first identity lets the two
decoders compare responses on different question spaces. The second gives
both the translation edge constraints and the valid-answer condition in
Fourier decoding.

Neither identity is an extra probabilistic test. They hold for every
labeling because of how the vertices and restored answers are defined.

5.3. The matrix test
------------------------------------------------------------

Sample:

  * U: k independent parity equations,
  * P: a uniform table on U,
  * a: latent noise drawn from mu,
  * l: a uniform bit vector of length m.

Accept if:

        F(P) = F(P + a l^T).

Here a l^T has rank at most one; either factor may be zero. For each
outcome put P'=P+a l^T and add an occurrence between v(U,P) and v(U,P')
with constraint

        label(v(U,P'))=label(v(U,P))+c(U,P)+c(U,P').

This is a translation permutation on K, including when the endpoints
coincide. Give it the exact sampling probability as its rational weight.
Keep repeated occurrences separately and omit zero-probability outcomes.
The weights sum to one, and a labeling's weighted value is exactly its
test acceptance. Vertices not incident to a positive occurrence may be
omitted: for soundness their labels can be extended arbitrarily.

5.4. Completeness
------------------------------------------------------------

Why does a near-satisfying parity assignment make this test pass with
very high probability?

Fix a global assignment satisfying at least 1-xi of the equation weight.
Evaluate each representative reduced table at this assignment on its
support, using a fixed satisfying triple for any violated retained
equation, and label its orbit by C of that evaluation. Equivariance of C
gives the restored value below on every all-satisfied tuple. On such a
tuple, the full answer z is actual and nonzero.
The table's value becomes:

        C(P z)

and after the update:

        C(P z + a (l^T z)).

Since P z is uniform in V and independent of the noise a, the latent
gadget says this changes with probability at most p. But equality only
fails when l^T z = 1 AND the latent change is nonzero. Since l^T z is
a fair bit independent of the rest, the failure probability is at most
p/2.

With probability at least 1 - k xi every equation in U is satisfied.
Therefore completeness is:

        value >= 1 - k xi - p/2.

Both k xi and p/2 can be made arbitrarily small. That is the entire
point of the latent gadget.

There are two separate sources of completeness loss.
The sampled tuple may contain an equation violated by the global
assignment, costing at most k xi by a union bound. On an all-satisfied
tuple, its actual answer z has first coordinate one and is therefore
nonzero. Uniform P then makes Pz uniform in V. Independently, uniform l
makes l^T z a fair bit. The gadget contributes at most p only on the half
of trials with l^T z=1, giving p/2. Repeated equation IDs do not invalidate
the union bound or either of these uniformity statements.

Khot, Minzer, and Safra supply the near-perfect Grassmann expansion theorem

For soundness, we need a structural theorem. It says that any function
which is too stable under uniform rank-one perturbations must be very
simple on a small "window" of matrices.

THEOREM 6.1 (equality soundness for degree-two shortcode)
------------------------------------------------------------
For every eta in (0,1), there are alpha in (0,1], an integer r_s >= 1,
and an integer ell_0, all depending only on eta. For every ell >= ell_0
there is m_0 = m_0(eta,ell) such that, for every m >= m_0, a function

       f : Mat(ell,m) -> F_2^ell

satisfying

       Pr_{M,a,l}[ f(M) = f(M + a l^T) ]  >=  eta,

has a nonempty row-and-column slice S, defined by at most r_s equations
of the form d_i^T M = s_i^T and at most r_s equations Mq_j = t_j,
such that for some z in F_2^m and u in F_2^ell,

       Pr_{M on slice S}[ f(M) = Mz + u ]  >=  alpha.

The test samples M in Mat(ell,m), a in F_2^ell, and l in F_2^m
independently and uniformly; the conclusion uses uniform measure on S. For rational eta,
positive rational alpha and effective dimension thresholds are available.
The derivation and the precise expansion input appear below.

A row-and-column slice is a set of matrices that all agree on some
linear combinations of rows and columns.

For example, a row equation d^T M=s^T fixes one linear combination of the
ell rows. A column equation Mq=t fixes the image of one vector q in the
m-dimensional domain. Their simultaneous solution set, if nonempty, is
an affine space. Uniform measure on that space means every satisfying
matrix receives equal weight; it is not the original matrix distribution
with an arbitrary reweighting.

The theorem promises agreement on ONE slice. It does not say that f is
globally affine, or that the slice occurs with a useful probability under
random row advice. Section 7.2 supplies that second step. The proof below
actually obtains a constant target on the selected slice, corresponding
to z=0. Later folding and the column constraints turn the target's coset
into a nonempty list of valid answers. Those are distinct arguments.

WHY THIS MATTERS
------------------------------------------------------------
The theorem says: if f keeps its value with a fixed positive probability
under a uniform rank-one update, then after zooming into a carefully chosen
finite window, f looks exactly like the honest affine table M z + u.
Thus a cheater who passes often must somewhere be behaving like a real
table.

PROOF OF THEOREM 6.1
------------------------------------------------------------
We use this established Grassmann expansion theorem of Khot--Minzer--Safra:
for every zeta>0 there are a density a>0, an integer r, and a dimension
threshold ell_0, depending only on zeta, such that for each ell>=ell_0
and then sufficiently large ambient dimension n, every nonempty set of
ell-dimensional subspaces of F_2^n with neighbor retention at least zeta
has relative density at least a in some interval

       {L : A_0 subset L subset B_0},
       dim(A_0)+codim(B_0)<=r.

Neighbors intersect in dimension ell-1; retention chooses a uniform
vertex in the set and a uniform neighbor. The constants and thresholds
are effective. This is Theorem 1.12 of the 2018 revision of their
Grassmann expansion paper; its Theorem 2.13, Section 2.4, and Lemmas
2.7-2.8 supply quantitative choices. Only this stated form is imported. [20]

Equality acceptance is the weighted average of the retention of the
nonempty fibers f^{-1}(y) under the matrix step. Select a fiber S with
retention at least eta. The probability of a zero perturbation is
 p_0=2^{-ell}+2^{-m}-2^{-ell-m}.
Increase the thresholds so p_0<=eta/2. Conditional on both factors being
nonzero, S has retention at least (eta-p_0)/(1-p_0)>=eta/2.

Embed M as the subspace
 L_M={(x,M^T x):x in F_2^ell} of E=F_2^ell+F_2^m.
These are precisely the graph-chart subspaces whose projection to the
first summand is invertible. Since
 dim(L_M intersect L_N)=ell-rank(M-N),
nonzero rank-one matrix differences are exactly adjacent chart vertices.
In characteristic two such a matrix has a unique factorization a l^T
with both factors nonzero. A chart vertex has (2^ell-1)(2^m-1) chart
neighbors. Its total Grassmann degree is
 (2^ell-1)(2^{m+1}-2): choose an internal hyperplane, then a different
one-dimensional extension in its quotient. Exactly half its neighbors
are in the chart. Thus the lifted fiber has full-graph retention at
least eta/4.

Apply the expansion input at zeta=eta/4. Intersect its interval with
the chart. This intersection is nonempty, and the relative density of
the lifted fiber cannot decrease because that fiber is entirely in the
chart. A basis (d_i,s_i) of A_0 gives equations d_i^T M=s_i^T.
A basis (t_j,q_j) of B_0-perp gives Mq_j=t_j. The intersection is exactly
the resulting row-and-column slice, with at most r equations of each type.
On an a fraction of it, f equals the selected value y. Take alpha=a,
r_s=max(1,r), z=0, and u=y. This proves the required affine-target
conclusion. No folding was used, which is essential when applying this
theorem to an erased function later.

Why the graph chart loses only a constant. A matrix is represented by the
graph of the map M^T, not by its individual rows as separate vertices.
For two such graphs, their intersection consists of the x with
(M-N)^T x=0, so codimension-one intersection is precisely rank(M-N)=1.
For each internal hyperplane of a chart vertex, half the alternative
one-dimensional extensions remain in the chart. Thus the retention
comparison loses a factor two, independent of ell and m.

The interval supplied by Grassmann expansion may contain subspaces outside
the chart. The selected fiber contains none of those. Removing them only
decreases the denominator in its relative-density calculation. Intersecting
with the chart therefore preserves the density lower bound and translates
the interval conditions into exactly the row and column equations above.

The constants alpha,r_s depend only on eta, ell is chosen next, and
the remaining ambient threshold is absorbed in m_0(eta,ell). For rational
eta, lower alpha to a positive rational using the effective bounds in
the stated expansion theorem. All subsequent dimension requirements
remain effective.

PART 7. MATRIX EXTRACTION AND FOURIER DECODING
================================================================================

Now assume some labeling makes the matrix test pass with probability
at least 0.99. We will use Fourier analysis to extract two local strategies
with a fixed positive agreement probability in an "advice experiment".
Put eta = 2^(-r_*)/16 and take alpha,r_s,ell_0 from Theorem 6.1.
Choose ell above both required thresholds and so that

       2^(-(ell-r_s)) < alpha/8,     2^(-ell) < eta/4.

All latent and shortcode data are fixed before k; take m=1+2k >= m_0
and k sufficiently large for the estimates below. See Section 9.1.

7.1. A change of noise
------------------------------------------------------------
For a real function f on a finite binary space E, use
 fhat(g)=E_{x in E} f(x)(-1)^{g(x)} for g in E*.
Character orthogonality gives
 f(x)=sum_g fhat(g)(-1)^{g(x)},  sum_g fhat(g)^2=E_x f(x)^2.
Also 1[y=y']=E_{sigma in K*}(-1)^{sigma(y+y')}.
These identities fix all Fourier and probability normalizations below. [43]

[43] Ryan O'Donnell. "Analysis of Boolean Functions." Cambridge University
    Press, 2014.
    https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~odonnell/papers/Analysis-of-Boolean-Functions-by-Ryan-ODonnell.pdf

A character of the table space is a parity of its entries. Writing its
frequency as (g_0,...,g_{m-1}) means that its value on P is
sum_i g_i(P e_i), computed in F_2. Under an additive update a l^T, the
change in that parity is sum_i l_i g_i(a). Averaging its sign over uniform
l gives one if all g_i(a) vanish, and zero otherwise. This explains the
eigenvalue formula without any assumption about the distribution of a.

The rank relevant to comparison is dim(span{g_i}|_K). The g_i may be
independent on V yet become dependent or zero when restricted to K.
Uniform K-noise sees only those restrictions. Using their full V-rank
would give the wrong eigenvalue and would not match the gadget's detection
hypothesis.

For a question Q and a linear character sigma in K*, define the sign:

       f_{Q,sigma}(P) = (-1)^{sigma(F_Q(P))}.

A matrix Fourier frequency is a tuple g = (g_i) of linear functionals
on V, one for each column i. The eigenvalue of g under the latent
noise is:

       Lambda_g = Pr_{a ~ mu}[ g_i(a) = 0 for every i ].

This lies in [0,1]. The latent gadget's detection property says:

       if the restriction of the span of the g_i's to K
       has dimension at least r_*, then Lambda_g <= 7/8.

For ordinary uniform rank-one noise with a uniform in K, the
eigenvalue at g is instead:

       2^(-dimension of span of restrictions of g_i to K).

Therefore latent acceptance 0.99 forces at least 0.9 of the squared
Fourier mass, averaged over U and uniform sigma, onto low-rank
frequencies. Mass on low-rank frequencies gives positive acceptance
under the ordinary uniform rank-one test.
Concretely:

LEMMA 7.1 (spectral comparison)
------------------------------------------------------------
If the table family passes the latent test with probability at least
0.99, then under ordinary uniform rank-one updates:

       E_{U,T} Pr_{M,a,l}[ F_U(M,T)=F_U(M+a l^T,T) ]
         >=  0.9 * 2^(-r_*).

In particular, at least 10 eta of the pairs (U,T) have uniform
rank-one acceptance at least 4 eta.

Proof. For each U,sigma, Fourier expansion and uniform P give
 E_{P,a,l} f_{U,sigma}(P)f_{U,sigma}(P+a l^T)
   =sum_g |fhat_{U,sigma}(g)|^2 Lambda_g.
Averaging over l kills a frequency unless every g_i(a)=0, which gives
the displayed eigenvalue. Parseval says each sign function has total
squared mass one. If B is the averaged mass at restricted rank at least
r_*, the latent acceptance is at most 1-B/8. Therefore B<=0.08 and
at least 0.9 of the mass has restricted rank below r_*. Under uniform
a in K the eigenvalue is 2^{-rank}, giving the asserted lower bound.
This perturbation keeps T fixed, so the same average is exactly the
average shortcode acceptance at (U,T). It is at least 14.4 eta.
If q is the fraction of pairs with acceptance at least 4 eta, that
average is at most 4 eta+q; hence q>=10.4 eta>=10 eta.

The comparison is a mass argument, not an assertion that the two noise
laws are close. A high-rank frequency loses at least 1/8 of its contribution
under latent noise. Acceptance at least 0.99 therefore permits at most
0.08 of the total squared Fourier mass at those frequencies. Low-rank
frequencies have ordinary-noise eigenvalue at least 2^(-r_*). Their mass
alone gives a positive lower bound under uniform K-noise, even if the two
noise distributions have almost disjoint supports.

7.2. From one slice to a useful row fiber
------------------------------------------------------------

The inverse shortcode theorem gives one good slice on each such
(U,T). But the U-prover will not see all of M. It sees only the
compressed rows S = AM, where A : K -> F_2^{r_s} is a random linear
map.

A row fiber is called good when some at-most-r_s column specifications
cut out a nonempty slice on which f agrees with an affine target on at
least an alpha/2 fraction of matrices. Here f(M)=F_U(M,T), with U and T
fixed. The witness consists of those column specifications and the target.
The erasure argument proves that good fibers cannot be confined to a
negligible set of matrices.

More precisely, for a positive fraction of (U,T,A,AM), the row fiber

       { M : AM = S }

contains a good slice. Fix (U,T) with acceptance at least 4 eta. Let B
be the union of all good row fibers over all A and S. If Pr[B] < eta,
replace f at each matrix in B by one independent uniform K-value, once
per matrix even where fibers overlap. Acceptance remains at least 2 eta,
since both test endpoints are uniform. For sufficiently large m, a
Hoeffding union bound gives replacements contributing less than alpha/4
agreement on every allowed slice with every affine target. A good row
fiber has been entirely erased; a bad one originally had agreement less
than alpha/2 on every such slice. Thus all modified agreements are less
than alpha, contradicting Theorem 6.1. Consequently Pr[B] >= eta.
Here are the quantitative details. Write r=r_s and m=1+2k. A nonempty
slice with at most r row and r column specifications has direction space
 Hom(F_2^m/span{q_j}, intersection_i ker(d_i^T)).
Its size n_S is at least 2^{(ell-r)(m-r)} for m>=r and ell>r.
Padding with zero equations, there are at most 2^{(2r+1)(ell+m)}
slice-and-target descriptions: each row or column equation uses ell+m
bits, and a target uses another ell+m bits.

For a fixed description, count matches only at randomized entries and
put deterministic zero contributions at untouched entries. These are
independent [0,1]-valued variables with total mean at most
2^{-ell} n_S<alpha n_S/8. Hoeffding's inequality bounds the chance of
at least alpha n_S/4 matches by exp(-alpha^2 n_S/32). [44] A union bound
therefore gives failure probability at most

[44] Wassily Hoeffding. "Probability Inequalities for Sums of Bounded Random
    Variables." Journal of the American Statistical Association 58, 1963, pp.
    13-30, Theorem 2.
    https://www.cs.rpi.edu/academics/courses/spring06/random/hoefding.pdf

 exp((2r+1)(ell+m)log(2) - (alpha^2/32)2^{(ell-r)(m-r)}),

which is less than one for large m. Fix replacements avoiding all these
failures. On a slice whose row fiber is good, every entry was erased,
so modified agreement is less than alpha/4. On any other row fiber,
original agreement on every allowed column slice was less than alpha/2;
untouched matches are a subset of original matches and randomized
matches contribute less than alpha/4. Modified agreement is then less
than 3alpha/4. Zero-padding row systems covers every slice in Theorem
6.1. The modified acceptance is at least 2eta>=eta, contradicting that
theorem. It does not need folding, so the erasure is legitimate.

Why erase the union of good fibers rather than one slice? A single good
slice might be extremely rare under the row advice actually shown to the
U-prover. Erasing the entire union B removes every potential witness of
good row advice at once. If B were small, at most 2 Pr[B] of test pairs
would touch it, so acceptance would remain high. Random replacement then
prevents all allowed slices from having substantial affine agreement,
contradicting Theorem 6.1.

The union bound succeeds because a slice contains exponentially many
matrices in m, while the number of descriptions is only exponential in m.
The probability that one random replacement has unexpectedly large
agreement is exponential in the NEGATIVE slice size. This is much smaller
than the reciprocal of the number of descriptions. The argument produces
one replacement table that simultaneously defeats every slice and target.

For completeness, the concentration inequality used here follows from
 E exp(t(X-E X))<=exp(t^2/8) for X in [0,1]. One proof differentiates
the log moment-generating function twice: its second derivative is a
variance under a tilted law, at most 1/4; integrate twice from zero.
Independence, Markov's inequality, and minimizing over t give
 Pr[sum(X_i-E X_i)>=u]<=exp(-2u^2/n). Taking u=alpha n/8 gives the
stated exp(-alpha^2 n/32) bound.

LEMMA 7.2 (good row advice)
------------------------------------------------------------
Sample U from the equation-product law, then independent uniform T,A,M,
and set S=AM. Good row advice has probability
at least g_0 = 10 eta^2 2^(-ell r_s) > 0. Goodness means that at most
r_s column specifications Mq_j=t_j, together with AM=S, define a
nonempty slice on which F_U(M,T)=Mz+u with probability at least alpha/2
under uniform measure on that slice. Choose a witness by a fixed order
using only (U,T,A,S) and the fixed table family. With Z=span{q_j}, put

       ANS = (z+Z) intersect {v : e_0^T v = 1}.

This set is nonempty and has at most 2^{r_s} elements. The representative
z may be chosen in ANS, adjusting u without changing the target on the
slice. The mass bound follows because each M in B belongs to a good
fiber for at least one of the 2^(ell r_s) choices of A.

Here "valid answer" means a vector whose homogeneous coordinate is 1.
This last step uses folding in a clever way: if every vector in the
naturally produced target coset had first bit zero, then translating
by all h in ker A would permit at most one agreement per orbit. The
agreement would be at most 2^(-(ell-r_s)) < alpha/8, contradicting
goodness. More explicitly, if the coset z+Z had no first-bit-one
vector, both z and every w in Z would have first bit zero. Translation
M->M+h e_0^T with h in H=ker A would then preserve the row equations,
the column equations, and the target Mz+u, but folding would translate
F_U by h. This free H-action permits at most one match per orbit.
Nonemptiness of the slice defines a consistent map t:Z->K by t(w)=Mw.
Choose w in Z making z+w valid and replace (z,u) by (z+w,u+t(w));
this preserves the target on the slice. A fixed finite ordering makes
both the witness and this representative depend only on the visible
row advice and the fixed table family.

The probability calculation now finishes the lemma: qualifying (U,T)
pairs have mass at least 10eta; on each, the union B has mass at least
eta; for each M in B, uniform A hits a good containing row fiber with
probability at least 2^{-ell r_s}. Multiplication gives the claimed
g_0=10eta^2 2^{-ell r_s}. The answer set has size between one and
2^{dim Z}<=2^{r_s}. All thresholds depend only on the fixed parameters.

The extra factor 2^(-ell r_s) has a simple source. There are exactly
2^(ell r_s) binary linear maps A:K->F_2^{r_s}. A matrix in B belongs to a
good fiber for at least one of them. Sampling A uniformly therefore hits
a good containing fiber with at least the reciprocal of that count. This
is a deliberately crude bound, but it is positive and independent of k.

The target vector is determined only modulo Z=span{q_j}, because M is
already fixed on Z throughout the column slice. Replacing z by z+w for
w in Z and adjusting u preserves the same affine target. Folding shows
that this coset must contain a vector with homogeneous coordinate one.
It is the coset, not the initially chosen z, that represents the decoded
list of possible local answers.

7.3. The projected advice experiment
------------------------------------------------------------

Local agreement is a decoding resource, not automatically a global
assignment. Dinur and Steurer's direct-product testing and Dinur and
Kaufman's agreement-expansion results formalize this principle in other
test systems. The experiment below has a different distribution and visible
advice, so its agreement bound is established explicitly in Lemmas 7.3-7.5
rather than borrowed from a general agreement theorem. [45] [46]

[45] Irit Dinur; David Steurer. "Direct Product Testing." CCC 2014.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/dptest/

[46] Irit Dinur; Tali Kaufman. "High Dimensional Expanders Imply Agreement
    Expanders." FOCS 2017; ECCC TR17-089.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2017/089/

We now compare an equation-tuple question U with a sparse projection
question O. Independently in each of the k blocks, with probability
beta, we replace the whole equation by a uniformly chosen one of its
three variables. Let L_O be its homogeneous answer space, with one
coordinate per singleton and two per retained equation, besides t_0.
Write pi also for the linear extension L_U -> L_O: its singleton
coordinate is x_i, y_i, or b_{e_i} t_0+x_i+y_i, respectively.

Sample independent uniform maps A:K -> F_2^{r_s}, T_O:L_O -> W, and
M_O:L_O -> K; put S_O=AM_O. The two provers receive only:

       U-prover gets (U, A, T_O pi, S_O pi),
       O-prover gets (O, A, T_O, S_O).

They must produce actual answers a_U and a_O with pi(a_U) = a_O.

The two inputs deliberately reveal different information. U identifies
all equation occurrences but does not reveal which blocks were projected
or which variable was selected there. O identifies a singleton variable
at each projected block but does not reveal that block's original equation.
Both sides receive compressed maps, not the full hidden K-valued table.
The response rules must use only the displayed data. A witness chosen by
the U-prover may depend on its own row advice, but it cannot depend on the
hidden projection mask or the O-prover's question.

LEMMA 7.3 (padded table law)
------------------------------------------------------------
When beta = k^(-2/3), the distribution of the padded table pulled back
from O to U tends in total variation to a uniformly random table on U
as k grows along cubes, after averaging out the projection choices.
Uniformly in U, writing n=|V|, the distance v_k is at most

       (1/2) sqrt((1 + (n-1) beta^2 / 3)^k - 1) = o(1).

Proof. Combine M and T into one V-valued table and put n=|V|. In a
retained equation block the two slopes have uniform law P_0 on V^2.
For a projected singleton their law Q_0 is the equal mixture of
 (a,0), (0,a), (a,a), with a uniform in V.
The likelihood ratio Q_0/P_0 is n at (0,0), n/3 at the remaining
3(n-1) points on these three lines, and zero elsewhere. Hence
 chi^2(Q_0||P_0)=E_{P_0}[(Q_0/P_0-1)^2]=(n-1)/3.
For the actual mixture (1-beta)P_0+beta Q_0 this is beta^2(n-1)/3.
Independent blocks multiply likelihood ratios, so the full slope
divergence is (1+(n-1)beta^2/3)^k-1. Cauchy-Schwarz gives total
variation at most half its square root. Since k beta^2=k^{-1/3}->0
and n is fixed, this tends to zero.

A third-position projection adds b_{e_i}a_i to the padded intercept.
The original intercept is independent and uniform in V. Adding any
function of the slopes and projection choices leaves it uniform and
independent of those data. Thus the same bound holds for the full table,
including the intercept. Repeated IDs do not couple different blocks:
their local copies and random columns were sampled independently.
The estimate is uniform in U and the parity instance.

The beta-squared gain is essential. A crude union bound would charge beta
for every projected block and give a total error of order k beta, which
must grow rather than shrink. Averaging over projection choices gives a
mixture with likelihood ratio 1+beta(L-1), where E[L-1]=0. Its squared
error is therefore beta^2 E[(L-1)^2], not order beta. Product structure
then yields the bound in Lemma 7.3.

Closeness holds AFTER averaging over the projection choices. It need not
hold after fixing a particular mask. Accordingly, the transfer proof first
compares events determined only by the padded table and its row advice.
Only afterward does it condition on the actual projected questions.

Therefore the good row advice extracted under unrestricted sampling
remains present in the actual experiment.

LEMMA 7.4 (transfer of a witness)
------------------------------------------------------------
For sufficiently large k, a fraction at least

       gamma_1 = (alpha g_0 / 8) 2^(-ell r_s) > 0

of the joint data (U,O,pi,A,T_O,S_O) has good padded U-advice whose
chosen witness transfers to the nonempty slice

       AM_O=S_O,     M_O pi(q_j)=t_j.

On this slice, F_O(M_O,T_O)=M_O pi(z)+u with uniform probability at
least alpha/4. The projected answer set is pi(ANS).

Proof. Under unrestricted uniform (U,A,M,T) let J be the event that
the row advice is good and M satisfies its chosen column equations.
Let E be equality with that chosen target, declared false off good
advice. A nonempty column slice imposes at most ell r_s scalar equations
inside a row fiber, so

 Pr[J]>=g_0 2^{-ell r_s},
 Pr[J and E]>=(alpha/2)Pr[J],
 Pr[J and E]-(alpha/4)Pr[J]>=(alpha g_0/4)2^{-ell r_s}=2gamma_1.

These events depend only on (U,A,M,T), not on projection information.
Adding an independent A preserves the total-variation bound of Lemma
7.3. Thus in the padded experiment the last difference is at least
gamma_1 whenever (1+alpha/4)v_k<gamma_1.

Now condition on D=(U,O,pi,A,T_O,S_O). On good data the witness is
fixed and M_O is still uniform on AM_O=S_O. Let p_D be the conditional
probability of its projected column equations, and q_D the equality
fraction on that column slice. Put p_D=0 off good data and q_D=0 when
p_D=0. Sharing identifies the two table functions, so the padded
difference is exactly E_D[p_D(q_D-alpha/4)]>=gamma_1. The integrand
is nonpositive outside the event of good advice, p_D>0, q_D>=alpha/4,
and is at most one everywhere. That event has probability at least
gamma_1. This proves both nonemptiness and the claimed agreement; no
assumption that every witness survives projection was made.

The factor p_D records whether the witness has enough matrices left after
projection; q_D records its agreement on those matrices. A witness can
collapse to inconsistent column equations, in which case p_D=0, or survive
but lose its useful agreement. Neither outcome is ruled out pointwise.
The positive averaged quantity p_D(q_D-alpha/4) forces a positive measure
of data on which BOTH survival and useful agreement occur. This is why
transferring the weighted difference works whereas transferring a chosen
witness for every projection would be unjustified.

Write z'=pi(z), Z'=pi(Z). Since pi preserves the first coordinate,
 pi(ANS)=(z'+Z') intersect {v:e_0^T v=1}.
Every element on the right has a preimage in z+Z, and that preimage has
the same first coordinate. This proves the stated answer-set identity.

7.4. The two decoders
------------------------------------------------------------

Now we define the strategies.

U-prover:
  If its visible advice is good, it samples an answer uniformly from
  the short valid answer set ANS. If not good, it returns any valid
  answer.

O-prover:
  Set H=ker A and choose M_0 with AM_0=S_O by a fixed rule. The row
  fiber is M_0+X, where X=Hom(L_O,H). Sample sigma uniformly from K*,
  and define f_sigma(N)=(-1)^{sigma(F_O(M_0+N,T_O))} for N in X.
  Sample Phi in X* with probability |f_sigma_hat(Phi)|^2, using Fourier
  coefficients normalized by uniform measure (the probabilities sum to 1).
  If sigma|_H is nonzero, choose h in H with sigma(h)=1 by a fixed rule
  and return Phi(h). Here a frequency is a tuple of functionals phi_i
  on H, and Phi(h) is the vector with coordinates phi_i(h) in L_O.
  If sigma|_H is zero, return any valid answer.

To check validity, folding gives
 f_sigma(N+h e_0^T)=(-1)^{sigma(h)}f_sigma(N).
Changing variables in its Fourier coefficient shows that a nonzero
coefficient requires Phi(h e_0^T)=sigma(h). Under the identification
X*=H* tensor L_O, contraction satisfies Phi(h e_0^T)=e_0^T Phi(h).
Thus the choice sigma(h)=1 forces a valid first-bit-one answer at every
frequency of positive probability.
The choice of h depends on A and sigma, not on the hidden witness.

The O-prover does NOT see the U-prover's witness or answer set. The
witness is used only in the analysis.

What Phi(h) means. Choose the standard basis of L_O. A frequency Phi on
X=Hom(L_O,H) is a list of functionals phi_i in H*, with

       Phi(N)=sum_i phi_i(N e_i).

Fixing h in H turns that list into the vector
(phi_0(h),phi_1(h),...) in L_O, denoted Phi(h). This is contraction of a
matrix frequency against h, not evaluation of Phi on a matrix named h.
Folding forces its first coordinate to be sigma(h). Selecting sigma(h)=1
therefore makes the decoded vector an actual homogeneous answer.

The hidden witness enters only when proving that a useful fraction of
Fourier mass lies in a particular coset. The O-prover samples from the
full Fourier distribution without knowing that coset. The proof shows
that its blind sample lands in the useful set with constant probability.

LEMMA 7.5 (a transferred slice gives agreement)
------------------------------------------------------------
On data satisfying the transferred witness property, the two private
rules agree with probability at least

        2^(-ell r_s) (alpha/8)^2 2^(-r_s).

Proof. Put z'=pi(z) and Z'=pi(Z). The transferred column equations
on the fiber M_0+X have the form N|_{Z'}=b for a fixed linear map
b:Z'->H. Consistency follows from their nonempty solution set. Choose
one solution N_*. For sigma in K* define

 C_sigma=E[f_sigma(N)(-1)^{sigma(Nz')} | N|_{Z'}=b].

Expanding equality with the target M_0z'+Nz'+u gives
 E_sigma[(-1)^{sigma(M_0z'+u)} C_sigma]>=alpha/4,
so E_sigma|C_sigma|>=alpha/4. Exactly a fraction 2^{-dim H} of the
characters restrict trivially to H. Since dim H>=ell-r_s and
|C_sigma|<=1, the parameter choice yields

 E_sigma[1[sigma|_H!=0]|C_sigma|]>=alpha/8.              (7.5.1)

The annihilator of {N:N|_{Z'}=0} is H* tensor Z'. Put
 theta_sigma=(sigma|_H) tensor z'. Fourier expansion on the affine
slice, using character orthogonality, gives

 C_sigma=sum_{Phi in theta_sigma+H* tensor Z'}
            fhat_sigma(Phi)(-1)^{(Phi+theta_sigma)(N_*)}.

The sum contains 2^{dim H dim Z'}<=2^{ell r_s} terms. Cauchy-Schwarz
on each sum, then on the average in (7.5.1), gives

 E_sigma[1[sigma|_H!=0]
       sum_{Phi in theta_sigma+H* tensor Z'} |fhat_sigma(Phi)|^2]
   >=2^{-ell r_s} E_sigma[1[sigma|_H!=0]C_sigma^2]
   >=2^{-ell r_s}(alpha/8)^2.

This is precisely a lower bound on the O-prover's probability of drawing
a nontrivial restriction and a frequency in that coset. Such a frequency
is theta_sigma+Psi with Psi in H* tensor Z'. For the decoder's h,
 Phi(h)=z'+Psi(h) belongs to z'+Z'. Its first coordinate is one by the
validity argument, so it belongs to pi(ANS). Independently the U-prover
chooses at least one compatible preimage with probability at least
1/|ANS|>=2^{-r_s}. Multiplying proves Lemma 7.5.

The three factors in the agreement bound have distinct meanings:

  2^(-ell r_s) bounds the Fourier-mass loss from restricting to a slice.
  (alpha/8)^2 converts average absolute correlation to squared mass.
  2^(-r_s) is the chance that the U-prover picks a compatible list element.

The projected list need not have the same size as ANS: projection can
merge several answers. This only helps. Every O-answer in pi(ANS) has at
least one preimage in ANS, and uniform sampling from a list of size at
most 2^{r_s} picks that preimage with probability at least 2^{-r_s}.

Multiplying gamma_1 by this conditional agreement gives a constant
gamma > 0.

PROPOSITION 7.6 (matrix decoding)
------------------------------------------------------------
For every sufficiently large cube k, with the parameters above fixed,
any table family satisfying folding and sharing
and passing the matrix test with probability at least 0.99 gives local
strategies in the projected advice experiment with agreement at least
the fixed constant gamma = gamma_1 2^(-ell r_s)(alpha/8)^2 2^(-r_s).
Witness selection and Fourier sampling need not be efficient: they are
strategies used only in the soundness proof. The witnessing family is
fixed in advance and may be built into each response rule. Lemmas 7.2-7.5
prove the proposition by multiplication of their probability bounds.
Their thresholds depend only on fixed dimensions and constants, not on
the parity instance or the labeling.

There are three different kinds of existence in this argument. The
reduction must explicitly construct the game. A soundness proof may fix
an arbitrary labeling and reason about it. Its auxiliary decoders need
only exist as local strategies, so they may enumerate witnesses or sample
from a Fourier distribution with no running-time restriction. This does
not hide computation inside the reduction: none of those decoder choices
is part of the output instance. Fixing complete private random tapes
and averaging also yields deterministic strategies of at least this value.

This is the first incompatible lower bound.

PART 8. CLEAN COORDINATES AND THE SOUNDNESS GAP

Now we prove the opposite bound: on a NO parity instance, no pair of
advice strategies can agree often.

8.1. Parallel repetition
------------------------------------------------------------

Parallel repetition is not the assertion that optimal strategies act
independently. Raz proved exponential decay for general two-prover games;
Holenstein gave a simplified proof and treated the no-signaling setting.
Only classical strategies are used here. [47] [48]

[47] Ran Raz. "A Parallel Repetition Theorem." SIAM Journal on Computing 27,
    1998, pp. 763-803.
    https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/a-parallel-repetition-theorem/

[48] Thomas Holenstein. "Parallel Repetition: Simplification and the
    No-Signaling Case." Theory of Computing 5, 2009, pp. 141-172.
    https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v005a008/

Rao obtained an answer-alphabet-independent bound for projection games.
The exact all-t quantitative estimate used below is Dinur and Steurer's
Corollary 1.2. The alphabet independence is what makes the parameter order
in Section 9.1 possible. [49] [21]

[49] Anup Rao. "Parallel Repetition in Projection Games and a Concentration
    Bound." SIAM Journal on Computing 40, 2011; ECCC TR08-013.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2008/013/

THEOREM 8.1 (projection-game repetition, Dinur--Steurer)
------------------------------------------------------------
Let G be a finite classical bipartite projection game with any probability
distribution on its constraint occurrences, including weights and repeated
occurrences. For an integer t>=1 let G^t sample t independent occurrences,
send the question tuples separately, and accept only if all constraints
hold. For every 0<g<1,

       value(G) <= 1 - g   =>   value(G^t) <= (1 - g^2/16)^t.

The bound does not depend on the size of the question sets or answer
alphabets. This alphabet-independence is essential: it lets us choose
the repetition exponent before the final alphabet is chosen. This is
Dinur--Steurer (2014), Corollary 1.2; their Sections 2.1-2.2 allow the
weighted occurrence model and this independent product. It is a stated
external theorem, used both here and for the final amplification. [21]

8.2. Clean coordinates
------------------------------------------------------------

The quadratic dependence on the base gap is substantive. Raz's
counterexample rules out a general strong-repetition conclusion with the
naive linear dependence on that gap. Rounding results of Barak, Hardt,
Haviv, Rao, Regev, and Steurer, and the improved analysis of Steurer, also
show why repeated Unique Games cannot be analyzed by simply multiplying
single-copy optima. [50] [51] [52]

[50] Ran Raz. "A Counterexample to Strong Parallel Repetition." SIAM Journal
    on Computing 40, 2011, pp. 771-777; ECCC TR08-018.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2008/018/

[51] Boaz Barak; Moritz Hardt; Ishay Haviv; Anup Rao; Oded Regev; David
    Steurer. "Rounding Parallel Repetitions of Unique Games." FOCS 2008.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/roundpar/

[52] David Steurer. "Improved Rounding for Parallel Repeated Unique Games."
    RANDOM 2010.
    https://www.dsteurer.org/paper/roundpar2/

Let D_O = (T_O, S_O) be the combined visible advice map from O to
E_A = W + image(A) (a direct sum). Conditional on A and the question
O, every slope column and the intercept are independent uniform values
of E_A.

A coordinate i is CLEAN if the corresponding block was projected to a
singleton and its one advice slope is zero. Conditional on the clean
mask, the equation-variable pairs in the clean coordinates have
exactly the ordinary equation-versus-variable law.

The probability that a coordinate is clean is:

       p_A = beta * |E_A|^(-1).

The factor |E_A|^(-1) is fixed conditional on A; p_A also contains
beta=k^(-2/3) and therefore tends to zero as k grows.

Why zero advice slope makes a coordinate clean. A projected singleton
normally leaks information through the coefficient attached to its chosen
variable. With coefficient zero, its pullback has two zero slopes on the
equation side, regardless of which of the three positions was selected.
The third-position intercept correction is also zero. Thus neither
player needs the other player's hidden clean question to reconstruct its
own advice input. This is the exact locality needed to invoke repetition.

LEMMA 8.2 (clean-coordinate bound)
------------------------------------------------------------
If the original parity instance has optimum at most 4/5, then every
pair of strategies in the advice experiment agrees with probability at
most:

       ( 1 - beta * 2^(-dim W - r_s) / 3600 )^k
 <= exp( - 2^(-dim W - r_s) * k^(1/3) / 3600 ).

PROOF
------------------------------------------------------------
Conditional on A, let R record the clean set I, the original O-intercept,
and all non-clean block data: equation IDs, projection choices, selected
positions, and advice slopes, as well as A itself. Do not condition on responses or
agreement events. In fact, for every e and selected position j,

 Pr[e_i=e, i clean at position j | A]=omega_e beta/(3|E_A|).

Independence across coordinates and independence of the original intercept
imply that conditional on each R=r of positive probability, the question
pairs in I are independent samples of the ordinary equation-variable game.
This remains true with repeated IDs because the occurrence samples are
independent. Fix r as constant data in auxiliary strategies. For each
clean coordinate i, offer the equation to one prover and the
variable to the other. Each prover can reconstruct the entire advice
input it originally received from this outside data plus its own clean
questions.

Why? The equation side sees the equation occurrence and can fill in
the two zero slopes and the full intercept. The variable side sees the
variable and can fill in the singleton slope and the original
intercept. Explicitly, if d_i is a projected singleton's advice slope,

       c_U = c_O + sum_{projected i at position 3} b_{e_i} d_i.

Clean blocks have d_i=0, so all nonzero corrections are determined by
the fixed outside data. These data supply all non-clean blocks. Therefore
full agreement in the original advice experiment implies acceptance in
every clean coordinate of the |I|-fold ordinary game. The equation-side
reconstruction uses no clean variable question or selected position; the
variable-side reconstruction uses no clean equation. Applying each
original response rule to its reconstructed input and retaining its
clean answers therefore defines legitimate local product-game strategies.

The ordinary game has value at most 14/15. Theorem 8.1 at gap 1/15
gives, for every r,
 Pr[full agreement | R=r]<=(1-1/3600)^{|I|}.
For I empty this bound is one. Conditional on A, the clean indicators
are independent with probability p_A=beta 2^{-dim W-rank A}. Thus

 Pr[full agreement]
 <=E_A E[(1-1/3600)^{|I|}|A]
 =E_A(1-p_A/3600)^k
 <=(1-beta 2^{-dim W-r_s}/3600)^k
 <=exp(-2^{-dim W-r_s} k^{1/3}/3600).

The auxiliary strategies may depend on the fixed outside data r; the
bound holds for every such r before averaging. No original prover was
given the other prover's question. This proves Lemma 8.2.

Conditioning here does not secretly give the players shared answers. The
outside record r is fixed first and contains no clean equation-variable
pair. For every such r, the resulting auxiliary strategies face genuinely
independent ordinary-game questions on the clean coordinates. The
repetition bound applies to each fixed r, including strategies that use
all of r as constant information, and only then is r averaged out.

No concentration estimate for the number of clean coordinates is needed.
Conditional on A, that number is binomial. Its probability-generating
function gives the exact identity

       E[(1-c)^|I| | A]=(1-p_A c)^k,

with c=1/3600. This directly supplies exponential decay in k beta.

8.3. The fixed matrix-game gap
------------------------------------------------------------

Choose beta = k^(-2/3). Then:

       k beta^2 = k^(-1/3) -> 0,   needed for padded law comparison,
       k beta   = k^(1/3) -> infinity,   needed for many clean coords.

For large k, the clean upper bound is smaller than gamma from decoding.
This contradiction proves:

PROPOSITION 8.3 (matrix game with adjustable completeness)
------------------------------------------------------------
With the latent and shortcode data fixed as above, for every
sufficiently large cube k and every cleaned parity instance E:

  If OPT(E) >= 1-xi: value(matrix game) >= 1 - k xi - p/2.
  If OPT(E) <= 4/5:  value(matrix game) <= 0.99.

The NO case uses 0.99 as the assumed acceptance threshold in Fourier
decoding. The YES case uses the latent completeness lemma.

This is the central tension. We now turn it into the final theorem.

The two limits are compatible because beta lies between 1/k and
1/sqrt(k). Choosing beta=k^(-2/3) makes the squared perturbation error
k beta^2 tend to zero while the expected number of clean opportunities
k beta tends to infinity. All alphabet-dependent constants are already
fixed. However small the decoded gamma is, the clean upper bound
ultimately drops below it.

================================================================================
PART 9. COMPLETING THE REDUCTION
================================================================================

The matrix game is still weighted and may have loops and parallel
edges. We must turn it into the exact promised object.

LEMMA 9.1 (rounding and subdivision)
------------------------------------------------------------
Let H be any finite permutation-constraint game with h positive
rational occurrence weights summing to one. For any positive rational tau,
there is an unweighted simple bipartite game B with 4Q edges, where
Q = ceil(h/tau), and the same alphabet, such that:

       | value(B) - (1 - (1 - value(H))/4) | <= tau/4.

Construction: start with floor(Q w_e), then add one to the entries
with largest fractional parts until the multiplicities sum to Q. Replace
each occurrence by a four-edge path with three fresh interior
vertices. A satisfied occurrence extends to four satisfied edges; a
violated one can satisfy only three. The interiors are disjoint, so
exact equality holds after rounding.

If the constraints of H are translations on a binary space, those of B
are too. Translation by zero is the identity, and the inverse of a
translation is still a translation.

Proof of the value and graph claims. The rounded multiplicities n_e
satisfy sum n_e=Q and |w_e-n_e/Q|<=1/Q. Their differences sum to zero,
so the satisfied-weight change for any labeling is at most
(1/2)sum_e |w_e-n_e/Q|<=h/(2Q)<=tau. Taking maxima preserves the
bound: if H' is the rounded occurrence multigraph, |value(H')-value(H)|
<=tau. Orient an occurrence u->v with permutation rho and replace it by
 u -- p_e -- q_e -- r_e -- v,
with fresh interior vertices for that occurrence. Use identity on the
first three edges and rho on the last along this path. Put original
vertices and q_e on the left, p_e and r_e on the right; invert an edge's
permutation when its left-to-right orientation reverses the path direction.
Given the original labels, a satisfied occurrence achieves four satisfied
edges. A violated one cannot achieve four, but propagating along the first
three achieves three. Disjoint interiors let these optima be attained
simultaneously, proving
 value(B)=1-(1-value(H'))/4.
A loop becomes a four-cycle with three fresh interior vertices, and
parallel occurrences have disjoint interiors. Thus B is simple and
bipartite even in these cases. For fixed tau, Q=O(h), and exact rational
rounding and listing take polynomial time and bit complexity.

Subdivision changes the objective by an exact affine formula. Once the
original endpoint labels are fixed, the fresh interior vertices can be
optimized independently for every occurrence. If the endpoint constraint
is false, all four path edges cannot be true because their permutations
compose to the original constraint; three can still be true. Thus every
original failure costs exactly one of four path edges. Fresh interior
vertices are also what remove parallel edges without changing that
optimization calculation.

9.1. Parameter order
------------------------------------------------------------

Fix desired errors epsilon, delta in (0,1/2). Choose positive rational
epsilon_0 <= epsilon and delta_0 <= delta.

  1. Choose a repetition exponent t such that

        (1 - 1/10240000)^t <= delta_0.

     This is possible because the post-subdivision NO gap is 1/800 and
     parallel repetition with gap g=1/800 gives g^2/16 = 1/10240000.

  2. Choose positive rationals p and tau with

        p <= epsilon_0 / t,
        tau <= min(1/200, epsilon_0 / t).

  3. From p, obtain the latent rank threshold r_*.
     Put eta = 2^(-r_*)/16.
     Obtain rational alpha>0, r_s, ell_0 and the effective dimension
     thresholds from Theorem 6.1.

  4. Choose ell >= ell_0, above the latent dimension threshold, with
     2^(-(ell-r_s)) < alpha/8 and 2^(-ell) < eta/4. Construct the
     latent gadget with K = F_2^ell. This fixes V, C, mu, and V = K + W.

  5. Choose a cube k >= 8 large enough for:
       - m=1+2k >= m_0(eta,ell);
       - the Hoeffding union bound;
       - the padded-law total variation bound;
       - the clean upper bound to be less than gamma.

     In addition to m>=m_0 and m>=r_s, impose these explicit rational
     inequalities, with gamma_1 and gamma as defined in Part 7:

       (alpha^2/32) 2^{(ell-r_s)(m-r_s)}
           > (2r_s+1)(ell+m),

       (1/4)[(1+(|V|-1)beta^2/3)^k-1]
           < gamma_1^2/(1+alpha/4)^2,

       (1-beta 2^{-dim W-r_s}/3600)^k < gamma.

     The first implies the erasure union bound is less than one since
     log(2)<1. The second gives (1+alpha/4)v_k<gamma_1. The third
     is the soundness contradiction. With k a cube, beta=k^{-2/3}
     is rational, and all checks are exact rational comparisons.
     They hold for all sufficiently large cubes: the first grows
     exponentially in m, the second tends to zero, and the third tends
     to zero. Enumeration is therefore a terminating deterministic
     search depending only on epsilon and delta.

  6. Finally choose rational 0 < xi < min(1/100, epsilon_0/(2kt)).
     Then k xi + p/2 <= epsilon_0 / t.

This order is the entire proof of the quantifier claim. r_* is fixed
before ell, ell before k, and k before xi.

The apparent circularity is resolved by two uniform statements. The latent
rank threshold r_* depends on p but not on ell. The repetition exponent t
depends on the constant soundness gap but not on the alphabet. Those facts
allow t and p to be chosen before the latent and shortcode dimensions.
Only after all those dimensions and the tuple length k are fixed is the
parity tolerance xi chosen small enough to absorb the k-fold completeness
loss. Every arrow in this dependency chain points forward.

9.2. The final theorem
------------------------------------------------------------

Given a 3SAT formula, apply Håstad's parity gap and make positions
distinct. [19] Build the matrix game from Section 5.

For a satisfiable formula:

       value(H) >= 1 - d,
       d = k xi + p/2 <= epsilon_0 / t.

For an unsatisfiable formula:

       value(H) <= 0.99.

Apply rounding and subdivision:

       YES:  value(B) >= 1 - (d + tau)/4.
       NO:   value(B) <= 1 - 1/800.

For the NO estimate, tau<=1/200 and value(H)<=99/100 give
 value(B)<=1-(1/100-tau)/4<=1-1/800.

Finally take the t-fold parallel product G = B^t:

       YES:  value(G) >= 1 - t(d+tau)/4 >= 1 - epsilon.
       NO:   value(G) <= (1 - 1/10240000)^t <= delta.

The output is unweighted, simple, bipartite, and has translation
constraints on K^t = F_2^{ell t}. Thus s = ell t is the required fixed
alphabet dimension. For completeness use the same labeling independently
in every coordinate: a union bound loses at most t(d+tau)/4, which is
at most epsilon_0/2<=epsilon. For soundness use Theorem 8.1, not an
assumption that arbitrary product strategies act independently. [21]
A pair of product vertices determines at most one edge in each coordinate,
since B is simple, and hence at most one product edge. Uniform independent
edge choices are uniform on these product edges. Products of binary
translations are translations by the tuple of their offsets. This checks
the exact graph, sampling, and constraint form in the theorem.

Completeness and soundness use different product arguments. For
completeness, repeating one known good labeling in every coordinate
explicitly supplies a good product labeling; a union bound controls its
failure. For soundness, a player can choose its whole answer tuple as an
arbitrary function of its whole question tuple. Its coordinates need not
be independent. Theorem 8.1 bounds those general strategies, which is why
simply writing value(B^t)=value(B)^t would not be a valid proof.

POLYNOMIAL ENCODING
------------------------------------------------------------
The parity instance size N gives N^k equation tuples. Because k is
fixed after epsilon and delta, the number of matrix-test occurrences
is polynomial in N. The number of tables, noise outcomes, and vectors
per tuple is fixed. Each weight is a product of k parity weights and
fixed rational factors. Keys and folded representatives are computed
by linear algebra in fixed dimensions. Comparison of keys by pairs is
polynomial. Rounding uses Q = O(h) copies, subdivision creates 3Q new
vertices, and the final product has (4Q)^t edges. Permutation tables
on F_2^{ell t} have constant size. Hence the entire reduction is
explicit and deterministic polynomial-time.

All constructed constants are chosen before the input formula; only xi
is chosen after k, and it too is fixed before the input. The parity-gap
and Grassmann expansion theorems and the repetition theorem are the stated
external inputs. The construction and all intermediate proofs needed to
compose them have been given here. This completes the reduction.

10.3. Every CSP has an SDP threshold
------------------------------------------------------------
A weighted constraint satisfaction problem over a fixed finite nonempty
domain D and a fixed finite family of Boolean predicates Lambda has an
explicit basic semidefinite program. Here SDP denotes Raghavendra's
LC relaxation (2009 thesis, Section 4.5): [62] for each applied predicate c,
let V(c) be the variables on which that applied payoff depends. Choose
vectors b_{v,a} and a probability distribution mu_c on D^{V(c)} for
every occurrence, subject to

[62] Prasad Raghavendra. "Approximating NP-hard Problems: Efficient Algorithms
    and Their Limits." Ph.D. thesis, University of Washington, 2009; Section
    4.5 and Theorem 7.9.
    https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~prasad/Files/thesis.pdf

 <b_{u,a},b_{v,b}>=Pr_{X~mu_c}[X_u=a,X_v=b]
       for u,v in V(c), a,b in D.

Maximize sum_c w_c E_{mu_c} P_c(X). The vectors share one positive
semidefinite Gram matrix. This specifies the relaxation including repeated
variables and ignored arguments. For any fixed Lambda-instance J with

       A = OPT(J) < B = SDP(J),

and every fixed 0 < eta < (B-A)/2, it is NP-hard to separate instances
of value at least B - eta from instances of value at most A + eta.
Raghavendra's SDP-based algorithm gives the optimal approximation
threshold, up to arbitrarily small positive error; excluding better
polynomial-time algorithms uses P != NP. [63] [64]

[63] Prasad Raghavendra. "Optimal Algorithms and Inapproximability Results for

This statement needs a stronger promise form of Unique Games: the

How to read A< B. The instance J is a FIXED witness to a gap between
integral assignments and the stated SDP. It is not the growing instance
produced by the reduction. An actual assignment gives a feasible SDP
solution by taking point-mass local distributions and indicator vectors,
so SDP(J)>=OPT(J). A strict gap means the relaxation can do better than
any assignment on J. The imported theorem turns that fixed numerical gap
into hardness for a family of growing instances with thresholds B-eta
and A+eta.

This is an additive promise-gap statement. It should not be read as a
multiplicative approximation guarantee when the optimum is arbitrarily
close to zero. The explicit inequality eta<(B-A)/2 keeps the two promised
ranges disjoint.

The fixed-instance statement is a value gap, not a claim that every
predicate has the same approximation ratio or is hard to beat by random
assignment. Approximation resistance is predicate-specific. Austrin and

Finite encoding also needs an argument: a real-valued relaxation or proof
certificate is not automatically a polynomial-bit output. Raghavendra and
Weitz analyze this issue for sum-of-squares proofs. The argument below
handles a narrower requirement directly, by replacing one fixed finite
verifier distribution by rational weights with controlled total variation.
It does not assume rationality of an optimal SDP solution. [73]

[73] Prasad Raghavendra; Benjamin Weitz. "On the Bit Complexity of
    Sum-of-Squares Proofs." ICALP 2017; arXiv:1702.05139.

Proof of the finite rational CSP consequence. The input from Raghavendra's
fixed-instance theorem (2009 thesis, Theorem 7.9; 2008 paper, Theorem 1.1)
is a reduction at tolerance eta/2 with YES value at least B-eta/2 and
NO value at most A+eta/2. [62] [63] Its strong-left game errors depend only on
J and eta, before the game alphabet is chosen. For the common-arity
convention, pad predicates with ignored trailing arguments, filled with
any fixed variable of J. Such a variable exists when A<B. Neither an
applied payoff nor its actual dependence set changes, so OPT and the
specified relaxation are unchanged. Remove only these added trailing
positions when emitting the original predicates.

Theorem 11.1 supplies a nonempty simple strong-left graph with common
left degree h and alphabet [R]. After R is fixed, the imported verifier's
local randomness is a fixed finite probability law theta on records s.
Each record specifies an original predicate, a(s) query strings in D^R,
and the roles occupied by their replies. This law may have real weights,
but it depends only on the fixed parameters. Choose one rational
probability table theta_hat with

       (1/2)sum_s |theta_hat(s)-theta(s)|<eta/2.

This is possible by density of rational probability vectors in a finite
simplex. Hardcode that finite table into the reduction. Let A_left and
B_right be the game vertex sets. Output variables are B_right x D^R.
For each record s, each a in A_left, and each ordered independent
neighbor tuple of length a(s), emit its relabeled predicate on the query
variables with weight

       theta_hat(s)/(|A_left| h^{a(s)}).

Keep repeated occurrences separate. Fill any syntactically present but
ignored argument with the first output variable. The graph is nonempty,
so an output variable exists. The weights are rational and sum to one.
For fixed local data and R, the counts |B_right||D|^R and
|A_left|sum_s h^{a(s)} are polynomial, and the weights have polynomial
binary length. The construction uses only explicit neighbor lists,
permutations, and the fixed local table.

For any assignment let g(s) in [0,1] be its conditional acceptance over
the outer choices. Changing theta to theta_hat changes its value by
at most their total variation, less than eta/2. Taking maxima preserves
this bound. The imported gap therefore becomes YES value at least
B-eta and NO value at most A+eta, as claimed. This proves the extra
finite rational formulation, rather than assuming real verifier weights
are a valid encoded output.

The rational approximation is chosen once for the finite local law, not
separately for each output instance or each assignment. Total variation
bounds the expectation of EVERY payoff in [0,1] by the same amount.
Taking the maximum over assignments therefore preserves the bound. This
uniform estimate is what permits the irrational analytic verifier to be
replaced by one explicit rational table without losing the promised gap.

10.4. Ordering CSPs
------------------------------------------------------------
Fix k >= 2 and a nonempty proper subset Pi of S_k. An ordering CSP
asks for a global order on variables; an ordered k-tuple is satisfied
if the order induced on it belongs to Pi. The random-ordering value
is rho = |Pi|/k!.

It is NP-hard to approximate the weighted finite ordering CSP at
every fixed ratio r in (rho,1]. The threshold is rho. [74]

[74] Venkatesan Guruswami; Johan Håstad; Rajsekar Manokaran; Prasad
    Raghavendra; Moses Charikar. "Beating the Random Ordering Is Hard: Every
    Ordering CSP Is Approximation Resistant." SIAM Journal on Computing 40,
    2011, pp. 878-914; ECCC TR11-027.
    https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2011/027/

The baseline rho is an acceptance fraction, hence a maximization ratio.
Every tuple has k distinct variables, so their relative order in a uniform
global ordering is uniform in S_k. Exactly |Pi| of the k! orders satisfy
the predicate. For hardness at ratio r>rho, it is not enough merely to
state a NO bound rho+delta: the YES bound must also be included. The
inequality r(1-delta)>rho+delta below is what separates the two cases.

Indeed compose Theorem 11.2 with Theorem 11.1 and the main theorem.
This gives YES value at least 1-delta and NO value at most rho+delta
for every fixed 0<delta<(1-rho)/2. Given r>rho choose delta also below
(r-rho)/(1+r), so r(1-delta)>rho+delta; an r-approximation would separate
the cases. A uniform order has expected value rho and hence is a
rho-approximation since OPT<=1. The conditional-expectation procedure
at the end of Section 11.2 makes this guarantee deterministic.

The ordering reductions were developed first for Maximum Acyclic Subgraph
by Guruswami, Manokaran, and Raghavendra and for all arity-three permutation
CSPs by Charikar, Guruswami, and Manokaran. The general theorem is due to
Guruswami, Håstad, Manokaran, Raghavendra, and Charikar. [75] [76] [74]

[75] Venkatesan Guruswami; Rajsekar Manokaran; Prasad Raghavendra. "Beating
    the Random Ordering Is Hard: Inapproximability of Maximum Acyclic
    Subgraph." FOCS 2008.
    https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~venkatg/pubs/papers/mas.pdf

[76] Moses Charikar; Venkatesan Guruswami; Rajsekar Manokaran. "Every
    Permutation CSP of Arity 3 Is Approximation Resistant." CCC 2009.
    https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~venkatg/pubs/papers/perm-3csp.pdf

Austrin, Manokaran, and Wenner also prove NP-hardness results for ordering
CSPs without a Unique Games assumption. Those are separate reductions;
the full random-ordering threshold stated here is obtained through the

[77] Per Austrin; Rajsekar Manokaran; Cenny Wenner. "On the NP-Hardness of

The statement below is specifically nonuniform. [78] [79]
For every fixed constant C > 1, it is NP-hard to approximate within
C:

[78] Prasad Raghavendra; David Steurer. "Graph Expansion and the Unique Games

For Multicut, the input is an undirected graph with positive rational

For Correlation Clustering, the hard Multicut family in that construction
has demands between antipodes within cubes of dimension at least two.
Graph edges are cube edges or join different cubes, so these demand pairs
are nonedges. Here is the cost-preserving transformation (Demaine--
Emanuel--Fiat--Immorlica, 2006, Theorem 4.7). [83] Make graph edges positive
with their costs, and add a negative edge at each demand pair with
weight M=1+sum_e c_e. There are no opposite-signed parallel edges.
A multicut yields a component partition with disagreement cost no larger
than its cut cost. Conversely, a clustering separating every demand
yields a multicut by deleting positive edges between clusters, with the
same cost. A clustering joining a demand pays at least M; replace it
by all singletons, whose cost is sum_e c_e<M, and delete all graph
edges. Thus every clustering converts to a multicut of no greater cost,
and the two optimum costs coincide. The transformation is rational and
polynomial, so every fixed-factor approximation would transfer back to
the hard Multicut family. This proves the fourth claim.

================================================================================
PART 11. THE APPENDIX: FINITE STRONG-LEFT AND ORDERING TRANSFER
================================================================================

Some reductions need the Unique Games promise in a stronger, finite
left-degree form. This appendix states those finite formulations and
gives the constructions, parameter choices, and proofs.

A nearly satisfied edge set need not give nearly all left vertices with
every incident edge satisfied: a small number of failures can touch many
vertices. The strong-left normalization explicitly repairs this mismatch.
The ordering transfer then uses its equal left degrees to make an average
over left vertices agree with an average over edges. Neither property is
being inferred silently from the edge-value theorem.

11.1. Simple strong-left games
------------------------------------------------------------

THEOREM 11.1 (simple strong-left Unique Games)
------------------------------------------------------------
For every 0 < zeta, nu < 1 and integers d,H >= 1, there are integers
h,R >= 1 with h >= H and d | h, and a deterministic polynomial-time
reduction from 3SAT to nonempty explicit unweighted simple bipartite
Unique Games instances over [R] with common left degree h, such that:

  YES case: a labeling strongly satisfies at least 1 - zeta of left
            vertices. Here "strongly satisfies" means every edge
            incident to that left vertex is satisfied.

  NO case:  value < nu.

The construction follows the normalization and sequence method of
Khot--Regev, with distinct tags ensuring simplicity. [58]

The construction has four jobs, in order: make left questions comparable
in weight; replace local rational weights by equally many occurrences;
attach tags so repeated sampling can still produce a simple graph; and
turn each sampled sequence of h occurrences into one left question. The
same fixed h is used for every left vertex. Its choice must precede the
alphabet so the later ordering reduction can ask for a prescribed degree
multiple without changing its game-error parameters.

Proof of Theorem 11.1.
The parameters in this proof are local to this section. Choose a multiple
h of d with h>=max(H,2) and 1/h<nu/4. Choose an integer T>=h so that
p_tag=(T)_h/T^h>3/4, where (T)_h=T(T-1)...(T-h+1). This is possible
since the chance of a collision among h independent uniform tags is at
most binom(h,2)/T. Choose positive rationals beta,gamma,u and an integer
L_2 with

 beta<nu/4,  h^2 gamma<nu/4,  (h+1)u<zeta/2,
 1/L_2<min(beta gamma/2, zeta/(2h)).

Choose rational gamma_0,zeta_0 in (0,1/2) with
2gamma_0<beta gamma/2 and 2zeta_0<u^2. Invoke the main theorem with
completeness error zeta_0 and soundness error gamma_0. All parameters
so far, including h, precede the resulting alphabet [R]. Let its
nonempty simple bipartite graph be (X_0,Y,E_0), with uniform edge weights.

Normalize the left marginal. Let w_x be the total edge weight at x,
and create n_x=floor(2|X_0| w_x) copies of x. Give each copy local
edge weights w_xy/w_x. Vertices with w_x=0 have no copies. The copy
set X_1 has |X_0|<=|X_1|<=2|X_0|, and every copy has incident weight
one. For any right labeling, copies of the same original x have the
same local objective, so replacing their labels by a common best label
cannot decrease satisfied weight. Since n_x/w_x<=2|X_0|, normalized
copy-game value is at most twice the original value, hence below
2gamma_0 in the NO case. For a lifted YES labeling the same calculation
on unsatisfied weights gives average local loss at most 2zeta_0<u^2.
Markov's inequality leaves more than a 1-u fraction of X_1 with local
satisfied weight at least 1-u.

Remove these local weights. Put a=L_2|Y|. At each copy x pick one
positive-weight neighbor y_0. Round a w_xy/w_x down for y!=y_0 and
assign all remaining occurrences to y_0. There are exactly a occurrences
at each x. For every subset of neighbors the change in its local weight
is at most |Y|/a=1/L_2, by summing rounding losses on that subset or
its complement. Thus the unweighted occurrence game has NO value
below 2gamma_0+1/L_2<beta gamma. On a YES input the lifted labeling
satisfies at least 1-u-1/L_2 of the occurrences at more than 1-u of
the left vertices.

Replace each right vertex y by T tagged copies (y,t). Replace every
occurrence by its T tagged versions, with the same permutation. This
preserves the value: old labels extend identically to tags, while from
any tagged labeling one can choose an independent uniform tag at each
original y and obtain the same expected original satisfied fraction.
Hence the tagged game's NO value is still below beta gamma.

Now replace each x by one left vertex for every ordered sequence of h
incident tagged occurrences. At position i connect it to that occurrence's
right endpoint with that occurrence's permutation. Each original x
contributes (aT)^h sequence vertices of degree h. To bound the full
sequence game's value, fix arbitrary right labels. Each occurrence at
x determines a unique satisfying left label; let p_x(l) be the fraction
determining label l. Then E_x max_l p_x(l)<beta gamma. Fewer than a
beta fraction of x have max_l p_x(l)>=gamma. At any other x, two of h
independent determined labels coincide with probability at most
 binom(h,2)sum_l p_x(l)^2 <= h^2 gamma.
Without such a collision, any sequence-dependent left label satisfies
at most one of the h edges. Consequently the full sequence game has
value at most beta+1/h+h^2 gamma<3nu/4, even when each left label
depends arbitrarily on the entire occurrence and tag sequence.

The sequence test does not assume that the left player commits to its
original label before seeing the sequence. For fixed right labels, every
sampled occurrence demands one particular left label. If those h demanded
labels are all different, even the best sequence-dependent answer satisfies
only one edge. A collision is the only way to do better. The small
maximal atom of p_x bounds the collision probability. This is the reason
the soundness bound survives arbitrary new left strategies.

Retain only sequence vertices whose h tags are distinct. Every x and
every untagged occurrence sequence retain the same fraction p_tag>3/4
of tag sequences. The h right neighbors are now distinct, so the graph
is simple and has common left degree h. Any labeling of the retained
graph extends to the full sequence graph, and its full edge value is
at least p_tag times its retained edge value. The NO value of the
retained graph is therefore less than (3nu/4)/p_tag<nu.

For completeness give each sequence vertex the old label of its base x
and use tag-independent right labels. Distinct-tag conditioning does
not change the independent uniform untagged occurrence sequence.
If f_x is its original satisfied occurrence fraction, a fraction f_x^h
of retained sequences at x are strongly satisfied. The total fraction is
at least

 (1-u)(1-u-1/L_2)^h
 >=1-u-h(u+1/L_2)
 =1-(h+1)u-h/L_2 > 1-zeta.

Every x contributes equally many retained vertices. There are
|X_1| a^h (T)_h such vertices and h edges per vertex. Here a is linear
in |Y|, while h,T,L_2 are fixed before the alphabet. Thus this is a
nonempty explicit deterministic polynomial-size construction; rational
rounding and permutation tables also have polynomial bit complexity.
This proves all claims of Theorem 11.1.

Distinct tags mean distinct right vertices (y,t), even when the same base
neighbor y is sampled several times. The tag filter discards the same
fraction of sequences for every x and every untagged sequence. It
therefore preserves the uniform left weighting and the completeness
calculation. Soundness pays only the reciprocal retention factor
1/p_tag<4/3. No assumption that the original h sampled neighbors were
distinct is needed.

11.2. Finite ordering transfer
------------------------------------------------------------

THEOREM 11.2 (finite ordering transfer)
------------------------------------------------------------
For every k >= 2 and nonempty proper Pi subset S_k, put rho_Pi=|Pi|/k!.
For every 0 < delta < (1-rho_Pi)/2, there are integers q,H >= 1 and
rationals zeta,nu in (0,1), depending only on k,Pi,delta, with the
following property.

For every fixed R >= 1 and every nonempty explicit unweighted simple
bipartite Unique Games instance over [R] with common left degree h >= H
and q | h, there is a deterministic transformation, polynomial in the
input size for fixed R, producing an explicit list of Pi-ordering
constraints on distinct variables, with nonnegative rational weights
summing to one, such that:

  YES: if a labeling strongly satisfies at least 1-zeta of the left
       vertices, then OPT_Pi >= 1-delta.
  NO:  if the input game has value at most nu, then OPT_Pi <= rho_Pi+delta.

The constants q,H,zeta,nu do not depend on R or h.

Keep the four scales separate in this appendix. k is the arity of an
ordering constraint; q is the number of coarse rank buckets; m is the
symbol alphabet of a fixed local construction; R is the Unique Games
alphabet size and hence the length of a query string. The parameters
k,q,m are fixed before R, while h is the common left degree of the input
game. None of these is the tuple length k from Parts 5-9.

The output variables are pairs (b,z) in B x [m]^R. The proof below
constructs their rational constraint list and chooses every gap parameter
before the alphabet. It also supplies the finite local-data and analytic
comparison arguments used in the construction.

Proof of Theorem 11.2.
Here k is the ordering arity, not the tuple length in Parts 5-9. We give
the finite construction and both directions of the reduction. Its analytic
inputs are the established increasing-tuple theorem, Gaussian stability,
and vector invariance estimates specified below, not the conclusion of
Theorem 11.2 itself.

(a) Normalize the predicate and define ties.
Let P be the indicator of Pi and choose pi_0 in Pi. Define
P_0(sigma)=P(pi_0 composed with sigma), so P_0(id)=1. For ranks
r=(r_1,...,r_k), let Ref(r) contain all permutations listing positions
in nondecreasing rank order, with arbitrary orders inside tied groups.
Use the payoff

       Pbar_0(r)=sum_{sigma in Ref(r)}P_0(sigma)/|Ref(r)|.

It lies in [0,1]. A raw tuple w becomes an original-Pi tuple v by
v_{pi_0(j)}=w_j. This maps Ref(r) bijectively by sigma->pi_0 composed
with sigma, so it preserves the payoff, also with ties. It suffices to
construct P_0 instances and make this position permutation at the end.
For a finite weighted instance J on variables U, let opt_q(J) be its
maximum Pbar_0 payoff over maps U->[q].

(b) Fixed rational local data.
We use the following finite increasing-tuple result of Guruswami,
Håstad, Manokaran, Raghavendra, and Charikar (2011), Theorem 11.1,
Definition 11.2, and the proof of Theorem 2.5: for fixed k,Pi,q and
eta>0, there is a finite rationally weighted instance J* with distinct
variables in each tuple, whose tuples are all increasing in one total
order, and with opt_q(J*)<=rho_Pi+eta. This is the only imported
combinatorial existence statement about the local instance. [74]

Take L disjoint copies of J*, each with weight 1/L, and place their
satisfying orders consecutively in sigma:U->[m], where m=L|U*|>=2.
Choose L and rational s_0 in (0,1) with 1/L+s_0<eta. Make a random
assignment X:U->[m] by choosing a uniform cyclic shift of sigma with
probability 1-s_0, and independent uniform labels with probability s_0.
A cyclic cut can split at most one copy, so its payoff is at least
1-1/L. Thus the mixture's expected payoff exceeds 1-eta.

Write c for a local tuple, omega_c for its rational weight, and mu_c
for the restriction of this mixture to that tuple. Every marginal of
mu_c is uniform on [m]. If n_c(x) counts shifts giving tuple x, then

       mu_c(x)=(1-s_0)n_c(x)/m+s_0/m^k.

All atoms are rational and exceed alpha=s_0/(2m^k)>0. The copied
instance still has opt_q(J)<=rho_Pi+eta, since each q-assignment has
that upper bound on each copy. Its local expected payoff v_loc is
at least 1-eta. These laws have common Gram data: in the finite mixture
probability space put I=1 and b_{u,j}=1[X(u)=j] as L^2 vectors. Then

 sum_j b_{u,j}=I,  ||I||=1,
 <b_{u,i},b_{u,j}>=0 for i!=j,  ||b_{u,j}||^2=1/m,
 <b_{u_{c,r},i},b_{u_{c,s},j}>=Pr_{mu_c}[X_r=i,X_s=j].

This supplies compatible first and second moments for every local tuple.

Why the local instance can be ordered well but not q-bucketed well. Its
strict satisfying order may use arbitrarily many ranks. A q-assignment
has only q buckets, and equal bucket ranks are evaluated by averaging all
consistent tie-breaks. The increasing-tuple theorem produces an instance
where that coarse information has payoff at most rho_Pi+eta. There is no
contradiction between a satisfying fine order and a poor bounded-bucket
optimum.

The cyclic shift has two roles. It makes every variable's label uniform
on [m], while spoiling the increasing order in at most one of the L
copies. Mixing in independent labels gives every tuple atom positive
probability. Because all tuple laws are marginals of one finite random
assignment X, their moment vectors belong to one common inner-product
space. That last feature is needed for the global Gaussian comparison.

(c) The two uniform analytic estimates used in the composition.

Further smoothing decreases every influence.

Collision estimate. Fix 0<kappa<1/2. There is mu_0(kappa)>0,
independent of m and R, such that, for each fixed m, a function
r_c(t)->0 as t decreases to zero has this property: if F,G take values
in [0,1], both have mean mu<=mu_0(kappa), and all influences of
T_{1-kappa}F and T_{1-kappa}G are at most t, then for ANY coupling
(Z,Z') with both marginals uniform on [m]^R,

 E[(T_{1-2kappa}F)(Z)(T_{1-2kappa}G)(Z')]
       <= mu^{1+kappa/2}+r_c(t).                     (11.2.1)

Here is its derivation from the stated stability input. The noise-stability
theorem of Mossel--O'Donnell--Oleszkiewicz (2010), Theorem 4.4, gives [55]
for H:[m]^R->[0,1] of mean mu and influences at most t,

 Stab_{1-kappa}(H)<=Gamma_{1-kappa}(mu)+E_{m,kappa}(t),

where E_{m,kappa}(t)->0 uniformly in R and mu. Gamma is Gaussian
halfspace noise stability. For sufficiently small t an available bound
is a constant times [log(2m)/kappa] log(log(1/t))/log(1/t).
Their Theorem B.5 gives
Gamma_{1-kappa}(mu)=mu^{2/(2-kappa)+o(1)} as mu->0. Since
2/(2-kappa)>1+kappa/2, a cutoff mu_0 depending only on kappa makes
Gamma<=mu^{1+kappa/2}. For H=T_{1-kappa}F the Fourier formula and
(1-2kappa)^2<=(1-kappa)^3 show
||T_{1-2kappa}F||_2^2<=Stab_{1-kappa}(H).
The same holds for G. Cauchy-Schwarz under any coupling gives (11.2.1).
Extend the error by the trivial bound one outside the small-t range.
This proves the uniformity required before m or R is chosen.

The coupling in (11.2.1) can be arbitrary because the final step uses only
its marginals. Cauchy-Schwarz bounds the coupled expectation by the product
of the two L^2 norms. Each norm is then controlled by a one-function
noise-stability estimate. No independence between Z and Z' is assumed.
The small-mean cutoff comes from Gaussian halfspace stability and depends
only on kappa, so q can be chosen before the later local alphabet m.

Local simplex estimate. Let S_q={p in [0,1]^q:sum_j p_j=1}, and let

 P_0^ml(p_1,...,p_k)=sum_{r in [q]^k}Pbar_0(r) product_i p_i^{r_i}.

Choose ONE local tuple c with law omega, then sample each of R
coordinate tuples independently from mu_c, obtaining strings Z_1,...,Z_k.
Independently resample every symbol at every position with probability
lambda in (0,1), producing Ztilde_i. If one function
F:[m]^R->S_q is used at all positions and tuples and
Inf_l(T_{1-lambda}F^j)<=t<1/(kq) for every l,j, then

 E P_0^ml(F(Ztilde_1),...,F(Ztilde_k))
       <=opt_q(J)+r_s(kqt),                          (11.2.2)

where r_s(u)->0 depends only on the fixed local data,k,q,lambda,
not R. The notation r_s here is an error function, unrelated to the
shortcode rank bound in Parts 6-9.

To justify (11.2.2), put H=T_{1-lambda}F. Independent position noise
and multilinearity replace the left side by E_c E_{mu_c^{product R}}
P_0^ml(H(Z_1),...,H(Z_k)). The following standard vector invariance
input is used: for atom bound alpha, a D-component multilinear product
expansion with component variances at most one, coordinate vector
influences at most u, and degree tails at most (1-lambda)^{2d},
replacing its finite ensembles by matching Gaussian ensembles changes
expectation of an L-Lipschitz test by at most

       C_D L u^{lambda/(18 log(1/alpha))}.

The bound is independent of the number R of coordinates. This is the
form of Isaksson--Mossel (2009), Theorem 3.6, used in Guruswami et al.,
Theorem 10.1; the local application is their Lemma 10.5. [96] [74] We now check
all needed hypotheses rather than treating the local estimate as an
unproved new lemma.

The Gram data in (b) give one Gaussian ensemble shared by ALL variables

It has the same first and second moments as the symbol indicators.

to an unrelated Gaussian experiment would only produce unrelated local
assignments, not an assignment to J. The Gram vectors in part (b) instead
define Gaussian variables for ALL vertices of J simultaneously. After

outcome is a legitimate global q-assignment and has payoff at most

the multilinear test being compared. This is the source of the global
upper bound in (11.2.2).

The Gaussian experiment is an analysis device. The actual reduction uses
only the finite rational local laws and rational resampling probabilities.
No Gaussian samples or real-valued vectors need to be encoded in the
output ordering instance.

We also need a finite bound on influential coordinates for ALL intervals
of one strict order o of [m]^R. If 0<u<1, at most

       400/(kappa u^3)                              (11.2.3)

coordinates l have Inf_l(T_{1-kappa}1[o(z) in I])>u for some rank
interval I. An elementary proof suffices. An interval is a difference
of two prefix indicators, so one prefix has influence greater than u/4.
Choose a grid of at most 34/u prefixes such that each prefix differs
from a grid prefix on at most u/16 of the uniform measure. For N=m^R
one can use all prefixes when uN/16<1 and otherwise spacing
floor(uN/16). Smoothing is an L^2 contraction and square-root influence
is a seminorm, so the corresponding grid prefix has influence greater
than u/16. For a smoothed indicator f, the product expansion gives

 sum_l Inf_l(T_{1-kappa}f)
 <=(1/(2kappa))Var(f)<=1/(8kappa),

using n(1-kappa)^{2n}<=1/(2kappa). Each grid prefix therefore has at
most 2/(kappa u) such coordinates. Their union has size at most
68/(kappa u^2)<=400/(kappa u^3), proving (11.2.3).

The interval bound must be uniform over all rank intervals, not just a
single chosen interval. Directly listing intervals would introduce a
factor depending on m^R. The prefix grid avoids that dependence: every
prefix is close in uniform measure to one of O(1/u) grid prefixes.
Smoothing and the influence seminorm transfer any large influence to a
grid prefix. The total-influence bound then limits the union of their
influential-coordinate sets by a quantity depending only on kappa and u.

(d) The independent-neighbor experiment.
Orient the input game's permutations from A to B. Their string action
is (pi_{a->b}z)_{pi_{a->b}(l)}=z_l. The output variables will be
V_out=B x [m]^R. For now fix kappa,q and the data above, and use
lambda=2kappa. Sample a uniformly from A; choose b_1,...,b_k
independently uniformly in N(a); independently run the local experiment
from (11.2.2); and form raw queries

       w_i=(b_i,pi_{a->b_i} Ztilde_i).

For any integer-valued rank map f on V_out let V(f) be the expected
Pbar_0 payoff of the ranks f(w_i), and V_a(f) its conditional value.
The tie payoff also defines this intermediate experiment when IDs repeat.

If a labeling L strongly satisfies at least 1-zeta of A, take
f(b,z)=z_{L(b)}. At a strongly satisfied a all neighbors read the
same coordinate L(a), because pi_{a->b}(L(a))=L(b). With probability
(1-2kappa)^k none of the k symbols in this coordinate is resampled,
and their tuple then has law mu_c. Hence

       V(f)>=(1-zeta)(1-2kappa)^k v_loc.              (11.2.4)

For soundness fix any strict global order O of V_out. For each a,
sort the hm^R IDs of N(a) x [m]^R by that order and split them into
q consecutive equal-size blocks C_{a,j}. This is possible because q|h.
Define

 F_a^j(z)=(1/h)sum_{b in N(a)}1[(b,pi_{a->b}z) in C_{a,j}].

The vector F_a(z) belongs to S_q and each component has uniform mean
1/q, since edge permutations are bijections on strings. For any global
rank interval I define the analogous averaged indicator F_a^I. Call a
good for this analysis when Inf_l(T_{1-kappa}F_a^I)<=t for all I,l.
This meaning of good is local to the ordering proof.

At a good a, conditional on the query strings the independent neighbor
choices make the coarse block payoff exactly
P_0^ml(F_a(Ztilde_1),...,F_a(Ztilde_k)). Further smoothing only reduces
influences, so (11.2.2) bounds its expectation by opt_q(J)+r_s(kqt).
Fine and coarse payoff agree unless two query IDs are in the same block.
For each pair, (11.2.1), with component mean 1/q, bounds this probability
by q^{-kappa/2}+q r_c(t). This is valid even for correlated local strings,
since their two marginals are uniform. A union bound gives

 V_a(O)<=S_loc:=opt_q(J)
       +binom(k,2)(q^{-kappa/2}+q r_c(t))+r_s(kqt).    (11.2.5)

Repeated IDs in the unconditioned experiment are covered by the same
coarse-tie event.

If a is not good, select an interval I_a and coordinate l_a witnessing
influence greater than t. For each right vertex b let o_b be the relative
order of its own [m]^R strings and let L_b contain coordinates whose
smoothed interval influence in o_b exceeds t/2. By (11.2.3),
|L_b|<=3200/(kappa t^3). Influence is a convex squared norm, and noise
commutes with permutations and averages. Therefore

 t<Inf_{l_a}(T_{1-kappa}F_a^{I_a})
 <=(1/h)sum_{b in N(a)}
   Inf_{pi_{a->b}(l_a)}(T_{1-kappa}1[O(b,z) in I_a]).

Every summand is at most one, so more than t/2 of the neighbors have
summand greater than t/2. Restricting a global rank interval to one right
code is an interval of its relative order; these neighbors consequently
have pi_{a->b}(l_a) in L_b. Label a by l_a and label each b independently
uniformly from its nonempty L_b, using an arbitrary label for an empty
set. Each non-good a has expected satisfied fraction greater than
kappa t^4/6400. This is one shared right labeling, not separate labelings
for different left vertices.

The left witnesses I_a and l_a may differ from one left vertex to another.
The right lists L_b cannot depend on a. They are built from all intervals
of the single order o_b, so every left witness is covered by the same
list at its neighbor b. Choosing one random label from each L_b therefore
produces a valid global game labeling. Allowing a separate right label
for each incident left vertex would destroy this deduction.

If V(O)>S_loc+chi, more than chi of the left vertices are non-good,
because payoffs lie in [0,1]. Their common degree makes the edge
fraction the same as this left average. Some labeling would then have
value greater than chi kappa t^4/6400. It follows that

 value(Phi)<=nu<chi kappa t^4/6400
       implies V(O)<=S_loc+chi for every strict O.    (11.2.6)

(e) Distinct variables, parameter order, and exact output weights.
Condition on D={b_1,...,b_k distinct}. Its probability is
p_D=(h)_k/h^k, independent of a and of the local randomness, and
1-p_D<=binom(k,2)/h=:d_h. All output IDs are then distinct. For every
rank map f, splitting over D and its complement gives
|V_D(f)-V(f)|<=d_h, since payoffs lie in [0,1].
For the coarse YES map in (11.2.4), refine each tied fiber by an independent
uniform permutation of its distinct IDs and concatenate the fibers.
For each conditioned tuple its expected strict payoff is exactly the
tie-extended payoff, so one global strict order attains at least V_D(f).
For a strict order O the conditioned payoff already is strict. Thus

 YES: OPT >=(1-zeta)(1-2kappa)^k v_loc-d_h,
 NO:  OPT <=S_loc+chi+d_h, provided nu<chi kappa t^4/6400.

Permuting tuple positions as in (a) gives the original Pi with these same
bounds. Choose the constants in this order, all before R and h:

  * Pick rational eta>0 with 10eta<delta, and rational kappa in (0,1/2)
    with 2kappa k<eta. Put K_pairs=binom(k,2).
  * Pick integer q with 1/q<=mu_0(kappa) and K_pairs q^{-kappa/2}<eta.
  * Construct the rational local data J,m,alpha at error eta. Now r_c
    and r_s have fixed parameters, independent of R.
  * Pick rational 0<t<1/(kq) with K_pairs q r_c(t)<eta and r_s(kqt)<eta.
    Set chi=eta. Pick rational 0<zeta<eta and
    0<nu<min(1,eta kappa t^4/6400).
  * Pick integer H>=max(k,q) with K_pairs/H<eta.

The vanishing error bounds guarantee these choices. For every h>=H,
Bernoulli's inequality and the bounds above give

 YES: OPT >=1-zeta-2kappa k-eta-d_h >1-4eta>1-delta,
 NO:  OPT <rho_Pi+6eta<rho_Pi+delta.

Finally, for each fixed R, all local choices, the product law mu_c across
R coordinates, and the independent rational resampling form a fixed
finite rational law theta_R on outcomes xi. Explicitly list V_out.
For each a, each ordered distinct neighbor tuple, and each positive
local outcome xi, emit the corresponding position-permuted Pi tuple
with weight

       theta_R(xi)/(|A|(h)_k).

The weights sum to one. There are |B|m^R variables and at most
|A|(h)_k |support(theta_R)| occurrences. For fixed R,k and local data,
these are polynomial in the graph size, even if h varies. Each weight
has O(log|A|+k log h) bits plus a fixed constant; IDs and permutations
are equally explicit. The Gaussian variables in the proof are not part
of the output. This completes the deterministic finite rational reduction.

Two final conversions should not be conflated. Conditioning on distinct
neighbors removes repeated output variables and costs at most d_h in
acceptance. Refining the coarse YES ranks into a strict order handles
ties; its expected payoff equals the tie-averaged payoff because the
conditioned tuple has distinct IDs. The output then enumerates the entire
finite experiment with exact rational weights. Randomness is used to
prove existence of a good ordering, not to select which constraints are
written down by the reduction.

To finish the random-ordering threshold claim, expose a global order one
next variable at a time. If s roles of a k-tuple have been exposed, its
conditional satisfaction probability is the number of permutations in Pi
whose first s roles occur in that exposed order, divided by (k-s)!.
For each candidate next variable compute the weighted sum and choose one
whose conditional expectation is at least the average. Since k is fixed,
all these quantities are exact rationals computable in polynomial time.
The final value is at least the initial expectation |Pi|/k!.

PART 12. A SHORT DICTIONARY OF NOTATION
================================================================================

  F_2          the field {0,1} with addition mod 2.
  [n]          {1,2,...,n}.
  K            final Unique Games alphabet, often F_2^ell or F_2^s.
  V            latent alphabet containing K.
  C            nonlinear map V -> K.
  mu           rational noise distribution on V.
  value(G)     maximum satisfied fraction of a game G.
  val(G)       maximum satisfied fraction.
  OPT          optimum value of an optimization instance.
  F_Q(P)       restored answer determined by a labeling, question Q,
               and table P.
  Hom(E,F)     linear maps from E to F.
  rank M       dimension of the image of the linear map M.
  eps, delta   desired completeness error and soundness error.
  xi           parity-gap error, chosen small after k.
  k            tuple length used in the outer game.
  beta         k^(-2/3), sparse projection probability.
  r_*          latent rank threshold.
  r_s          bound on the number of row and column specifications
               in the shortcode theorem.
  alpha        slice agreement lower bound.
  gamma        decoded advice agreement lower bound.
  W            a linear complement so V = K + W.
  A            random linear map K -> F_2^{r_s}.
  S            compressed matrix rows AM.
  T            W-valued part of a table.
  M            K-valued part of a table.
  pi           answer projection U -> O.
  e_0^T        homogeneous-coordinate functional; a valid answer z has e_0^T z=1.
  V*           binary dual: linear functionals V -> F_2.
  G|_K         image of G under restriction to K, not an intersection of duals.
  Z            span of the column-query vectors q_j in a slice witness.
  ANS          (z+Z) intersect {v:e_0^T v=1}, the U-decoder's short answer list.
  H            ker A in the Fourier decoder; H_r is instead a harmonic sum.
  TV(P,Q)      (1/2) sum_x |P(x)-Q(x)|, total-variation distance.
  chi^2(Q||P)  E_P[(Q/P-1)^2], when Q is supported on the support of P.
  [R]          Unique Games labels in Part 11; R is query-string length there.
  q            coarse rank-bucket count in Section 11.2, not the field size
               q=2^d used locally in Section 4.6.
  m            local symbol count in Section 11.2, not the table-domain
               dimension m=1+2k used in Parts 5-9.
