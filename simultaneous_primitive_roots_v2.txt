===============================================================================
 SIMULTANEOUS PRIMITIVE ROOTS: A CONDITIONAL LOWER BOUND FOR PRIME BASES
===============================================================================

 

 

 Notation used from here on, once and for all:

   p, q, ell, Q, j   primes (lowercase q for the bases we prescribe)
   M, r, d, m, n     positive integers
   x                 a large real number; the primes we want lie in (x,2x)
   L                 log x  (natural log throughout)
   log               natural logarithm
   "a = b (mod c)"   c divides a-b
   "d | n"           d divides n
   "n <= X << Y"     n is at most an absolute constant times Y
   "n = O(Y)"        same thing
   "n ~ Y"           n/Y tends to 1
   "n = o(Y)"        n/Y tends to 0
   F_p               the integers mod p; F_p^* = {1,2,...,p-1} under
                     multiplication mod p
   #S                the number of elements of the finite set S
   phi(M)            Euler's totient: the number of units mod M

===============================================================================


 The question is short. Fix a prime q, for concreteness q = 2. For which
 primes p do the powers of 2 run through *every* nonzero residue mod p?

        p     powers of 2 mod p (until 1)              order   index
        3     2, 1                                     2       1
        5     2, 4, 3, 1                                4       1
        7     2, 4, 1                                   3       2
       11     2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 6, 3, 1            10       1
       13     2, 4, 8, 3, 6, 12, 11, 9, 5, 10, 7, 1    12       1

 The primes that pass -- 3, 5, 11, 13, 19, 29, 37, 53, 59, 61, 67, 83, ... --
 are the subject of Artin's primitive root conjecture (1927): they should
 have density about 0.3739... among all primes. Nobody can prove even that
 this set is infinite. What we prove here is a weaker statement with a
 hypothesis: a positive proportion of x/(log x)^2 primes below x have a
 whole prescribed finite set of primes as simultaneous primitive roots.

 

 Two habits that make the rest easy.

 (i) Always think in terms of *index*, not order. The index of q mod p is
     (p-1)/ord_p(q), an integer dividing p-1. The index is 1 exactly when
     q is a primitive root, and every failure has a prime divisor.

 (ii) Always think in terms of *constructions of p*. We do not study a
     random prime and ask about its order; we build p out of pieces
     (Part 5) so that every possible way q could fail is visibly rare.

===============================================================================
 PART 1. ORDERS, PRIMITIVE ROOTS, AND THE ONE TRICK
===============================================================================

 1.1 The stage. Fix a prime p. The set F_p^* = {1,2,...,p-1} is a group
 under multiplication mod p, with p-1 elements. Two standard facts, used
 constantly:

   (G1) F_p^* is cyclic: there is a g with
        F_p^* = {1, g, g^2, ..., g^{p-1}}.

   (G2) Lagrange: the number of elements of a subgroup divides the number
        of elements of the group. Consequence: if a^d = 1 (mod p) for some
        d >= 1, then ord_p(a) divides d.

 1.2 Order and index. For a prime q not divisible by p,

        ord_p(q) = the least d >= 1 with q^d = 1 (mod p).

 By (G2) and Fermat's little theorem q^{p-1} = 1, the order divides p-1.
 Its complement

        iota_p(q) = (p-1) / ord_p(q)

 is called the index of q mod p. Then q is a primitive root mod p if and
 only if ord_p(q) = p-1, if and only if iota_p(q) = 1. The index is the
 measure of failure: it is an integer, it divides p-1, and every index
 greater than 1 has a prime divisor. The entire proof consists of making
 each possible prime divisor of the index impossible or rare.

 1.3 A table for p = 13, listing powers until they return to 1.

        q    powers                                       ord   iota
        2    2 4 8 3 6 12 11 9 5 10 7 1                   12     1
        6    6 10 8 9 2 12 7 3 5 4 11 1                   12     1
        3    3 9 1                                        3     4
        4    4 3 12 9 10 1                                 6     2
        5    5 12 8 1                                      4     3
        1    1                                            1    12

 Check a couple by hand. 2^6 = 64 = 52 + 12 = 12 = -1 (mod 13), so 2 is
 not a square mod 13; yet 2 is a primitive root. 3^3 = 27 = 1 (mod 13), so
 ord_13(3) = 3 and 3 fails badly. And 6 = 2*3 is a primitive root while
 3 is not: nothing here is multiplicative, because a generator times a
 non-generator can be a generator.

 1.4 Squares, non-squares, Euler's criterion. For p odd and p not dividing
 q, exactly half of F_p^* is a square. Euler's criterion:

        q^{(p-1)/2} = +1 (mod p)   if q is a square mod p,
        q^{(p-1)/2} = -1 (mod p)   if q is not a square mod p.

 (Reason: the squares form a subgroup of index 2, so q^{(p-1)/2} = 1
 exactly when q lies in it.) Write (q|p) for the Legendre symbol: +1 for a
 square, -1 for a non-square, 0 when p | q. It is multiplicative in q:
 (q_1 q_2|p) = (q_1|p)(q_2|p).

 1.5 THE TRICK. If q is a non-square mod p, then iota_p(q) is ODD.

 Proof. Let d = ord_p(q) and suppose iota = (p-1)/d is even. Then d
 divides (p-1)/2, so q^{(p-1)/2} = 1 (mod p), contradicting Euler's
 criterion, which says the value is -1.                        QED

 Two lines, and it is the hinge of the whole paper. Read it twice: it
 turns a condition we can create with congruences on p (q is a non-square)
 into a condition strong enough to enumerate (the index is odd, so it is
 a product of odd primes only, and it divides p-1, whose factorization we
 will know exactly).

 Plan it suggests, and the plan we follow:

    (a) build p so that q is a non-square mod p;
    (b) build p so that p-1 has very few odd prime factors (Part 5), and
        kill each of them one at a time (Parts 7 and 8).

 1.6 Quadratic reciprocity, and the supplement for 2. For distinct odd
 primes p and q,

        (q|p)(p|q) = (-1)^{((p-1)/2)((q-1)/2)},

 and

        (2|p) = +1 if p = 1 or 7 (mod 8),  -1 if p = 3 or 5 (mod 8).

 So if p = 1 (mod 4) the exponent ((p-1)/2)((q-1)/2) is even, the sign is
 +1, and (q|p) = (p|q): for such p the symbol depends only on p mod q.
 Consequently, if p = a (mod q) with a a non-square mod q, then q is a
 non-square mod p. One congruence on p handles one base; the Chinese
 remainder theorem handles all of them at once. This is exactly how
 Part 6 will arrange condition (a) for a whole finite set of bases.

===============================================================================
 PART 2. THE THEOREM, AND THE TWO INGREDIENTS IT NEEDS
===============================================================================

 Fix a finite set of distinct prime bases q_1, ..., q_k once and for all.
 Everything below depends on them only through finitely many constants.

 THEOREM 1. Assume Inputs A, B, C, D (Part 3). There are constants
 c = c(q_1,...,q_k) > 0 and x_0 = x_0(q_1,...,q_k) such that for every real
 x >= x_0, at least

        c x / (log x)^2

 primes p with x < p < 2x satisfy

        p does not divide q_1 q_2 ... q_k,   and
        ord_p(q_i) = p-1   for every i = 1, ..., k.

 In words: a prescribed finite set of primes is simultaneously a set of
 primitive roots for at least a fixed multiple of x/(log x)^2 primes below
 x, forever.

 How large is that? The number of primes in (x,2x) is about x/(2 log x),
 so the theorem delivers a fraction E of them with E about x/L^2 divided by
 x/L, that is E of order 1/log x. The claim says nothing about which
 proportion asymptotically; it says the count never drops below a fixed
 multiple of x/(log x)^2. Two powers of the logarithm are lost, one to the
 sieve (Part 9) and one to the marking device (Part 5).

 The two ingredients are the following propositions. Their proofs are
 Parts 5-9; here we only state them and note what they say in words.

 PROPOSITION 2 (a reservoir of primes in a fixed progression). Assume
 Inputs B, C, D. Let M >= 1 be odd and squarefree, and let a be a residue
 class mod M with gcd(a(a-1), M) = 1. Then there are constants
 c_{M,a} > 0 and x_{M,a} > 1 such that for every real x >= x_{M,a}, at
 least c_{M,a} x / L^2 primes p satisfy

        x < p < 2x,   p = a (mod M),   p = 4 r Q + 1,

 where r is a positive integer, Q > x^{0.9} is prime, and every prime
 divisor ell of r satisfies

        exp(L^{0.1}) < ell < exp(L^{0.3}).

 The case M = 1 is allowed and means the trivial progression.

 In words: we can manufacture primes that are 1 more than four times a
 number with exactly one enormous prime factor Q, all remaining prime
 factors r sitting in a narrow window far from both 1 and Q, and with p
 landing in any prescribed residue class that avoids 0 and 1. The window
 is what makes Parts 7 and 8 work: the factors of r are large enough that
 the analytic estimate of Part 8 applies to them, and small enough that
 there are not many of them.

 PROPOSITION 3 (a uniform bound for order obstructions). Assume Input A.
 Fix a positive prime q. Then for all sufficiently large real x, uniformly
 over primes ell with exp(L^{0.1}) < ell < exp(L^{0.3}), the number of
 primes p with

        x < p < 2x,   p does not divide q ell,   p = 1 (mod ell),
        q^{(p-1)/ell} = 1 (mod p)

 is at most

        C_q ( x / (ell (ell-1) L) + x^{1-eta} ),        eta = 10^{-6}.

 The constant C_q depends only on q; the threshold in x depends on q but
 not on ell.

 In words: for a fixed base q, the primes where ell divides the index of q
 are genuinely rare -- density about 1/ell^2 instead of 1/ell. If ell
 divides iota_p(q) then q^{(p-1)/ell} = 1, so Proposition 3 counts exactly
 the primes we must exclude. The condition p = 1 (mod ell) has density
 1/ell, and the extra condition cuts it down by another factor 1/ell; that
 saving is what makes the sum over all admissible ell converge (Part 6).

 Three remarks on Theorem 1, to place it.

 (i) One base is already hard. Take k = 1 and q_1 = 2. Then Theorem 1 says
     that, conditionally on Inputs A-D, the primes for which 2 is a
     primitive root number at least c x/L^2 up to x. Unconditionally, the
     infinitude of that set is still unknown. What is known unconditionally
     is only collective: Gupta and Murty (1984) and Heath-Brown (1986)
     proved that for certain small sets of bases at least one member works
     for infinitely many p.

 (ii) The conditional history. Hooley (1967) proved the full Artin
     asymptotic for one base under the generalized Riemann hypothesis;
     Matthews (1976) treated simultaneous sets the same way; Anwar and
     Pappalardi (2017) obtained an infinitude criterion from Schinzel's
     Hypothesis H. Theorem 1 is of this family: strong hypothesis, strong
     conclusion, no effective constants.

 (iii) Input A is a consequence of the generalized Riemann hypothesis,
     which forbids zeros with real part above 1/2 for all these
     L-functions; Input A only forbids zeros with real part above
     1 - 10^{-6}. Inputs C and D are combinatorial sieve facts. Input B is
     a bilinear (Type II) estimate of Bombieri-Vinogradov flavour. Where
     the four come from is the business of the companion paper; here they
     are hypotheses, quoted verbatim in Part 3.

===============================================================================
 PART 3. THE FOUR INPUTS
===============================================================================

 Three of the four inputs are stated in terms of one fixed package of data,
 used again in Parts 5 and 9. Nothing in this section is proved; it is the
 bookkeeping that later parts refer to.

 3.0 The marked data. Throughout, x is real and tends to infinity, L = log x,
 and

        W = exp(L^{0.24}),        V(y) = prod_{p <= y} (1 - 1/p),

 the product over primes p. So V(y) ~ e^{-gamma_E}/log y by Mertens'
 theorem, where gamma_E = 0.577... is Euler's constant.

 Fix an integer K >= 1 and fixed exponents

        0.1 < a_1 < a_2 < ... < a_K < 0.2.

 The i-th group of primes and its mass are

        P_i = {p prime : exp(L^{a_i}) <= p <= exp(2 L^{a_i})},
        V_i = sum_{p in P_i} 1/p = log 2 + o(1).

 The last equality is Mertens' theorem again, since the interval
 [L^{a_i}, 2L^{a_i}] in the exponent has logarithmic length log 2. For
 large x the groups are disjoint (they live at different heights), and
 every group prime lies in the window

        exp(L^{0.1}) < p < exp(L^{0.3}).

 Write G for the set of positive integers all of whose prime factors are
 group primes; call these the group integers; 1 is included. For a
 positive integer h put

        h_P = prod_{p in P_1 u ... u P_K} p^{v_p(h)},
        omega_i(h) = #{p in P_i : p | h},

 where v_p(h) is the exponent of p in h. So h_P collects the full powers
 of the group primes inside h, and h/h_P removes them completely -- all
 exponents, not just the first power.

 The mark of h, with mark parameter 1/2, is

        W(h) = 2^{K - sum_i omega_i(h)} * prod_{i=1}^K omega_i(h)/V_i,
        h >= 1,

 with the convention that it is zero as soon as some omega_i(h) = 0. Two
 facts about marks, used repeatedly.

  (M1) 0 <= W(h) <= B_K = (2/log 2)^K for all h.
       Proof: the bound j 2^{1-j} <= 1 for integers j >= 1 gives
       W(h) = prod_i (2^{1-omega_i} omega_i / V_i) <= prod_i 1/V_i, and
       V_i = log 2 + o(1), so prod_i 1/V_i <= B_K for large x.     QED

  (M2) A function F on the positive integers satisfies
       F(p h) = F(h) for every group prime p and every h >= 1 if and only
       if F(h) depends only on h/h_P.
       Proof: multiplying by p changes nothing exactly when p is in a
       group; and (M2) says F may not see the group part at all, including
       how many times each group prime occurs.                     QED

 Marks are a weighting device: they let us count integers that pick up at
 least one prime from every group, with weights that are bounded (M1) and
 multiplicative-ish. The reason for wanting one prime from each of K
 disjoint groups will appear in Part 9 -- it is a way of making a
 bilinear (Type II) estimate apply to a sieve problem.

 Finally, for w > gamma > 0, define the rough-number density

        D_gamma(w) = 1/w
          + sum_{j >= 2} (1/j!) * INT_{t_i >= gamma, sum t_i <= w - gamma}
                  ( 1/(w - sum_{i<j} t_i) ) * prod_{i=1}^{j-1} dt_i/t_i,

 the integral over j-1 variables t_1,...,t_{j-1}. The term with index j
 requires w >= j gamma, so the sum is finite for fixed w. D_gamma is what
 one gets by writing down the measure of integers whose least prime factor
 is prescribed, in the standard Buchstab decomposition; its two properties
 we need are recorded as Input D and used in Part 9. It is nonnegative and
 jointly continuous on the region w > gamma > 0, which we check now because
 Part 9 needs it.

 Lemma 3.1. D_gamma(w) is nonnegative and continuous on w > gamma > 0.

 Proof. Nonnegativity is clear. For continuity fix a compact set inside
 w > gamma > 0 and a j >= 2. Put s = w - j gamma and substitute
 t_i = gamma + s y_i for i = 1,...,j-1. When s >= 0 the j-th summand
 becomes

        s^{j-1}/j! * INT_{y_i >= 0, sum y_i <= 1}
             prod dy_i / ( prod_{i<j} (gamma + s y_i) * (gamma + s(1 - sum y_i)) )

 and we declare it zero when s < 0. On the compact set the denominators
 stay bounded away from zero (all factors are at least gamma times
 something), so the expression is continuous in s, hence in w, including at
 s = 0 where it tends to zero. Each summand is continuous and only finitely
 many are nonzero on a bounded w-set, so the sum is continuous.     QED

  Now the four inputs. Each is quoted in the exact form used, followed by a
 plain-English gloss. Inputs A and B are analytic (L-functions and
 bilinear sums); Inputs C and D are combinatorial sieve facts.

 INPUT A (uniform Hecke zero-free assertion).
 Let F be a cyclotomic number field containing all 12-th roots of unity.
 Let chi be a finite-order Hecke character of F. Then the meromorphic
 continuation of the L-function L_F(s, chi) has no zero with
 Re s > 1 - 10^{-6}. The pole at s = 1 is permitted when chi is principal.

 Gloss. A number field is a finite extension of the rationals; F = Q(mu_m)
 is the field generated by the m-th roots of unity. Its ring of integers
 has prime ideals P, each with a norm N(P), and an L-function is a product

        L_F(s, chi) = prod_P (1 - chi(P) N(P)^{-s})^{-1},

 convergent for Re s > 1, with a meromorphic continuation. A finite-order
 Hecke character is the number-field analogue of a Dirichlet character; it
 is a well-defined multiplicative function on the prime ideals, of finite
 order. You only need one thing from this input: a fixed-width zero-free
 strip, with a width that does not depend on F or on chi.

 Why the uniformity is the content. For a single F, classical theory gives
 a zero-free region of the shape Re s > 1 - c/log(disc F), whose width
 shrinks as the discriminant grows. Input A insists on the same width
 10^{-6} for all such F at once, including the fields Q(mu_{12 ell}) used
 in Part 8, where ell runs up to exp(L^{0.3}) and the discriminants are
 astronomical. The generalized Riemann hypothesis (zeros only at
 Re s = 1/2) implies Input A immediately; the converse is false.

 INPUT B (marked Type II estimate). Fix the marked data of 3.0. The input
 has two parts.

 (i) Coefficient criterion. Fix delta, C, D_* > 0. Let H_m, H_n be real
 with H_m, H_n >= x^delta, and put X = H_m H_n, assumed to be of size x:
 the ratio X/x lies between two fixed positive constants. Let F be a
 function on the positive integers with |F(h)| <= 1 for all h and
 F(p h) = F(h) for every group prime p and every h >= 1. Let (alpha_m),
 (beta_n) be complex sequences supported on W-rough integers in
 [H_m, 2H_m] and [H_n, 2H_n] respectively, where alpha_m may be nonzero
 only for m in an arbitrary interval I inside [H_m, 2H_m], and with
 |alpha_m|, |beta_n| <= L^C. Assume that for every fixed A_0, B, A > 0,
 every Dirichlet character chi modulo k with k <= L^{A_0}, and every real
 |t| <= 2 X L^B,

        | (1/H_m) sum_m alpha_m chi(m) m^{it} | <<_A L^{-A},

 uniformly in the interval I, in the character chi, and in the frequency t.
 (The implied constant and the threshold in this estimate may depend on
 A_0, B, A, on the marked data, and on all further fixed parameters that
 enter the definition of the coefficients.) Then

        | sum_{m,n} alpha_m beta_n F(m n - 1) W(m n - 1) | << X L^{-D_*}

 provided K is large enough in terms of the fixed data, and independently of
 the particular exponents a_i. The implied constant and the threshold in
 this last inequality may depend on all fixed data, including the common
 constants and thresholds in the estimate just displayed and the a_i. With
 those data fixed, the bound is uniform over the permitted F, dyadic
 intervals, intervals I, and coefficients.

 (ii) Rough coefficient cancellation. Keep the marked data and the
 hypotheses on F, beta, H_m, H_n, but do not assume any alpha has been
 chosen. Fix 0 < gamma < w_- < w_+ < 1 and assume

        [ log H_m / L, log(2 H_m)/L ] is contained in [w_-, w_+].

 Then for every interval I inside [H_m, 2H_m] and every real |v| <= L^C, the
 coefficient

        alpha_m^(0) = m^{iv} * ( 1{P^-(m) > x^gamma}
                       - (D_gamma(log m / L) / (L V(W))) * 1{P^-(m) > W} ),
        m in I,

 extended by zero outside I, satisfies the displayed estimate of (i) with
 alpha^(0) in place of alpha, for every fixed A_0, B, A > 0. The assertion
 is uniform in the permitted dyadic intervals, intervals I, characters,
 frequencies t, and real v. Its constant and threshold may depend on the
 fixed parameters, including gamma, w_-, w_+; in particular gamma and
 w_- - gamma are fixed before x grows.

 Gloss, part (i). This is a "Type II" (bilinear) estimate: a statement that
 a double sum over m and n, weighted by a factor F(mn-1) that sees only the
 non-group part of mn-1, is small. The hypothesis is purely about
 cancellation of the single sequence alpha_m against Dirichlet characters
 twisted by m^{it}: alpha_m is orthogonal to every character and every
 frequency in a wide range. That is the kind of input that
 Bombieri-Vinogradov-type theorems supply for the difference between a
 prime count in a progression and its main term.

 Gloss, part (ii). The two indicators in alpha^(0) are "m has no prime
 factor below x^gamma" and "m has no prime factor below W". Their
 difference is a Buchstab-type comparison: the first is the exact test we
 want, the second is its expected density times a smoother test at the
 smaller threshold W, and Input B(ii) says the difference has square-root
 cancellation against characters. This is what lets Part 9 replace an
 awkward exact indicator by a tractable one, at the cost of one factor 1/L
 (the density factor 1/(L V(W)) has size about L^{0.24}/L).

 Note what is NOT assumed: nothing about F beyond boundedness and group
 invariance (M2); nothing about beta beyond support and size. That
 flexibility is why the estimate survives being multiplied by a Dirichlet
 character in Part 9 (Lemma 9.2) and why it applies in as many as O(L)
 dyadic boxes at once.

 INPUT C (block sieve). Let z > 1. Let A be a finite set with nonnegative
 weights lambda_nu for nu in A, and let D be a set of designated primes, all
 at most z. For each p in D let A_p be the subset of A on which the bad
 condition at p holds. For a squarefree product ell of primes in D put
 A_ell = the intersection of the sets A_p over primes p dividing ell, and
 A_1 = A. Suppose that, for ell = 1 as well,

        sum_{nu in A_ell} lambda_nu = Y g(ell) + R(ell),        Y >= 0,

 where g is multiplicative on these squarefree products with g(1) = 1, and
 g(p) is extended by zero at primes outside D. Suppose that for fixed
 constants eta_0 > 0 and C_0 < infinity,

        0 <= g(p) <= 1 - eta_0,     sum_{u < p <= u^2} g(p) <= C_0  (u > 1),

 sums over primes p in D. Let

        S = sum of lambda_nu over nu in A but in no A_p

 be the weight that avoids every bad condition. Then for every sufficiently
 large even integer H,

        S = Y prod_{p <= z} (1 - g(p)) * (1 + O(e^{-H}))
            + O( sum_{ell <= z^{4H+2}, ell squarefree,
                     p | ell => p in D} |R(ell)| ),

 where both remainder sums run over squarefree products of designated
 primes, ell = 1 included, and the thresholds and implied constants depend
 only on eta_0 and C_0. In particular the constants are uniform when H
 grows, the main term factorizes over primes, and the remainder has no
 extra coefficient or multiplicity factor. For H = 2 the same method gives
 the upper bound

        S << Y prod_{p <= z} (1 - g(p))
             + sum_{ell <= z^{10}, ell squarefree, p | ell => p in D} |R(ell)|.

 Gloss. A sieve is a machine for counting what survives when you forbid
 divisibility by small primes. You feed it the sizes of the sets A_ell of
 survivors against all products ell of forbidden primes; the machine
 returns the count S. The main term is always the product of the local
 densities 1 - g(p) over the forbidden primes, because divisibility by
 distinct small primes behaves as if independent. The input has exactly
 two hypotheses: a uniform gap 0 <= g(p) <= 1 - eta_0 (so no prime is
 forbidden almost completely, which would make the product worthless), and
 a bounded total mass C_0 on dyadic ranges of primes (so the product cannot
 be destroyed by a burst of density somewhere). The word "block" refers to
 the shape of the remainder: the sum of |R(ell)| is over ell up to a fixed
 power of z, with no multiplicity factor. That shape is what makes Part 9's
 remainder bookkeeping a clean comparison with Lemma 9.3.

 INPUT D (rough-number density). For the density D_gamma(w) of 3.0 there
 are absolute constants b_d, c_d, C_d > 0 such that, for every fixed
 0 < b < min(b_d, 1/2),

        INT_b^{1/2} D_t(1 - t) dt/t = D_b(1) - 1,
        D_b(1) <= (e^{-gamma_E}/b) * (1 + C_d e^{-c_d/b}).

 The value of the integrand at t = 1/2 is immaterial.

 Gloss. The first line is a bookkeeping identity from the Buchstab
 decomposition: it says that the density at parameter b equals 1 (the
 contribution of prime inputs) plus the accumulated contribution of
 composites, integrated over the bins for their least prime factor. The
 factor dt/t is exactly the measure of "the least prime factor lies in the
 exponent bin [t, t+dt]". The second line says the density is not much
 larger than e^{-gamma_E}/b, the sieve-dimension-1 prediction, with a
 relative error that is exponentially small in 1/b. That exponential
 smallness is what Part 10 spends to make the final constant positive.

 Where each input is used:

        Input A    Proposition 3 only (the Kummer field count, Part 8)
        Input B    Proposition 2, via Lemmas 9.2 and 9.4 (Part 9)
        Input C    Proposition 2, via the two sieves of Part 9
        Input D    Proposition 2, in the least-prime-factor removal of
                   Part 9 and in the constant selection of Part 10

 Theorem 1 uses Input A through Proposition 3 and Inputs B, C, D through
 Proposition 2; nothing else is assumed anywhere.

===============================================================================
 PART 4. THE PROOF IN ONE PAGE
===============================================================================

 This part proves Theorem 1 from Propositions 2 and 3, nothing else. If you
 read only one part of this note, read this one: it is the whole idea, and
 the remaining parts are the two ingredients' proofs.

 The shape of the argument. To prove ord_p(q_i) = p-1 for every i, it is
 enough, by Lemma 1.5, to produce p such that

        (a) every q_i is a non-square mod p (this makes the index odd), and
        (b) no prime divides the index of any q_i.

 Step (a) is one congruence on p. Step (b) needs to know the prime
 factorization of p-1: if p - 1 = 4 r Q with Q a huge prime and all prime
 factors of r in a narrow window, then the odd primes dividing p-1 are the
 factors of r together with Q, and there are few of them. That is exactly
 the shape Proposition 2 delivers. So:

        Proposition 2 gives lots of p of shape 4 r Q + 1 in a chosen
        progression; Step (a) is then automatic; Step (b) splits into two
        cases, Q or a factor of r, and each is rare by a different
        argument (Q: elementary, Part 7; factors of r: Proposition 3).

 Now the details.

 Proof of Theorem 1. Let q_1, ..., q_k be the distinct prime bases.

 1. The progression. Let M be the product of the odd members of
 {q_1, ..., q_k}, with M = 1 if all of them are 2. For each odd q_i choose
 a nonzero quadratic nonresidue c_i mod q_i; this is possible because
 exactly half of the nonzero residues are nonresidues. By the Chinese
 remainder theorem there is a residue class a mod M with a = c_i (mod q_i)
 for every odd q_i; if M = 1 take the unique class. Since 1 is a square,
 a is not 1 mod any q_i, and a is not 0 mod any q_i, so

        gcd(a (a-1), M) = 1.

 This is precisely the admissibility condition of Proposition 2, so that
 proposition applies with this M and this a.

 Note what step 1 buys. Reducing mod q_i is the same as asking for p = a
 (mod q_i), and a is a non-square mod q_i. Later, in step 4, quadratic
 reciprocity turns this into "q_i is a non-square mod p".

 2. Apply Proposition 2. Get a set R(x) of primes with

        x < p < 2x,   p = a (mod M),   p = 4 r Q + 1,
        Q > x^{0.9} prime,   every prime ell | r has exp(L^{0.1}) < ell < exp(L^{0.3}),

 and with #R(x) >= c_{M,a} x / L^2. For x large every p in R(x) exceeds
 every q_i, so p does not divide q_1...q_k.

 Since r and Q are odd (Q > x^{0.9} > 2, and all prime factors of r are
 odd), the product r Q is odd, hence

        p = 4 r Q + 1 = 5 (mod 8),   and in particular p = 1 (mod 4).

 The congruence 5 mod 8 is not an accident: it is exactly the case in which
 2 is a non-square (see 1.6), so the base 2 is handled at the same time as
 the odd bases. That is why the shape contains the factor 4 rather than 2.

 3. Every base is a non-square mod p. Fix p in R(x) and one base q = q_i.
 If q is odd then q = q_i for some i, and q divides M, so

        (q|p) = (p|q) by reciprocity, because p = 1 (mod 4) kills the sign,
              = (a|q) because p = a (mod M) and q | M,
              = -1    by the choice of a in step 1.

 If q = 2, the supplementary law for 2 and p = 5 (mod 8) give (2|p) = -1.
 Either way, q is a non-square mod p.

 4. Hence the index is odd. By Lemma 1.5, iota_p(q) is odd. Since
 iota_p(q) divides p - 1 = 4 r Q and is odd, it divides r Q. So either
 iota_p(q) = 1, or iota_p(q) has an odd prime divisor lambda with

        lambda | r Q,   that is   lambda = Q or lambda | r.

 For such lambda, the definition of the order gives

        q^{(p-1)/lambda} = 1 (mod p),                        (4.1)

 because order divides (p-1)/lambda. So the primes p we must throw away are
 exactly the p for which one of finitely many conditions of the form (4.1)
 holds. Two cases.

 5. Case A: lambda = Q. Then Q | iota_p(q), so the integer

        j = (p-1)/Q = 4 r

 satisfies 1 <= j < 2 x^{0.1}, because p < 2x and Q > x^{0.9}. Also
 q^j = 1 (mod p) by (4.1), so p divides q^j - 1. Count such p by counting
 prime divisors of a product. Every p in (x,2x) dividing the integer

        P = prod_{1 <= j < 2x^{0.1}} (q^j - 1)

 contributes at least log p > L to log P, so the number of them is at most
 (log P)/L. And

        log P <= sum_{j < 2x^{0.1}} log(q^j) = log q * sum_j j
               <<_q (x^{0.1})^2 = x^{0.2}.

 Hence

        #{p in (x,2x) : Q | iota_p(q)} = O_q( x^{0.2} / L ) = o( x / L^2 ),

 since x^{0.2} L < x^0.5 L < x for large x. Case A is negligible.

 6. Case B: lambda | r. Here lambda is a prime in the window
 exp(L^{0.1}) < lambda < exp(L^{0.3}). For each such lambda, the primes p
 we are counting satisfy, by (4.1) and by lambda | p-1,

        p = 1 (mod lambda),   q^{(p-1)/lambda} = 1 (mod p),

 and lie in (x,2x); they also satisfy p > q and p > lambda, so p does not
 divide q lambda. Those are exactly the hypotheses of Proposition 3, which
 bounds their number by

        C_q ( x / (lambda (lambda-1) L) + x^{1-eta} ).

 Sum over the primes lambda in the window. For the first term, the sum of
 1/(lambda(lambda-1)) over primes lambda at least exp(L^{0.1}) is at most
 the same sum over all integers in that range, which telescopes:

        sum_{lambda > exp(L^{0.1})} 1/(lambda(lambda-1))
              <= sum_{n > exp(L^{0.1})} 1/(n(n-1))
              = 1 / exp(L^{0.1}),

 using 1/(n(n-1)) = 1/(n-1) - 1/n. For the second term, there are at most
 exp(L^{0.3}) primes in the window (crudely: at most exp(L^{0.3}) integers).
 Hence

        #{p in (x,2x) : some lambda | r divides iota_p(q)}
        <= C_q ( x / L * exp(-L^{0.1}) + exp(L^{0.3}) x^{1-eta} )
        = o( x / L^2 ).

 Indeed, multiplying the first term by L^2/x gives L exp(-L^{0.1}), and
 multiplying the second by L^2/x gives L^2 exp(L^{0.3} - eta L); the second
 exponent tends to minus infinity, since eta = 10^{-6} beats L^{0.3}, and
 the first clearly tends to 0.

 7. Assemble. We have shown, for one fixed base q:

        #{p in R(x) : iota_p(q) > 1} = o( x / L^2 ).

 The bases are finitely many, k of them, and the sum of k quantities that
 are each o(x/L^2) is still o(x/L^2). Therefore, for all large x,

        # {p in R(x) : iota_p(q_i) = 1 for every i}
        >= #R(x) - k * o(x/L^2)
        >= ( c_{M,a} - o(1) ) x / L^2
        >= ( c_{M,a}/2 ) x / L^2.

 Every such p satisfies x < p < 2x and ord_p(q_i) = p - 1 for all i, which
 is Theorem 1 with c = c_{M,a}/2.                            QED

 Three things to notice about this proof, because they are the reason the
 later parts are long and this part is short.

 (i) The counting in Case A uses nothing but the size of q^j - 1; there is
 no analytic input. It works because Q was forced to be enormous (bigger
 than x^{0.9}), which forces j = (p-1)/Q to be small (below 2x^{0.1}).
 That single number 0.9 is where the strength of Proposition 2 matters.

 (ii) The counting in Case B is the only place where a real theorem about
 L-functions enters, and it enters in the pleasant direction: an upper
 bound of size x/(lambda^2 L) for a set of primes of natural size x/(lambda L).

 (iii) Case B needs the window exp(L^{0.1}) < lambda < exp(L^{0.3}) for two
 different reasons: lambda > exp(L^{0.1}) makes the telescoping sum small,
 and lambda < exp(L^{0.3}) keeps the number of terms small enough that the
 error term x^{1-eta} does not accumulate. Both constraints are exactly
 what Proposition 2 hands over.

 It remains to prove the two propositions. Proposition 3 is Part 8; it is
 the shortest complete proof in the note if you accept Input A. Proposition
 2 is Parts 5 and 9, and it is where all the sieve machinery lives.
