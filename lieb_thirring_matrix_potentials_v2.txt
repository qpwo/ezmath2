Fix $m \ge 1$, let $1/2 < \gamma < 3/2$, and put $p = \gamma + 1/2$.  A matrix potential is a measurable function $W : \mathbb R \to \mathbb C^{m\times m}$ with Hermitian positive semidefinite values and

$$\int_{\mathbb R} \operatorname{tr}(W(x)^p)\, dx < \infty.$$

The one-dimensional Schrodinger operator $H_W = -d^2/dx^2 - W$, defined as the self-adjoint operator of the form $h_W[\psi] = \int_{\mathbb R} \|\psi'\|^2\, dx - \int_{\mathbb R} \langle \psi, W\psi\rangle\, dx$ on $H^1(\mathbb R; \mathbb C^m)$, then has only isolated negative eigenvalues, each of finite multiplicity, with possible accumulation only at zero.  Write $\lambda_j > 0$ for the absolute values of the negative eigenvalues of $H_W$, repeated according to multiplicity.  The inequality under study is

$$\sum_j \lambda_j^\gamma \le C_\gamma \int_{\mathbb R} \operatorname{tr}(W(x)^p)\, dx, \qquad C_\gamma = \left(\frac{\gamma - 1/2}{\gamma + 1/2}\right)^{\gamma - 1/2} \frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\gamma + 3/2)}.$$

The constant is independent of $m$.  It is sharp.  With $r = (\gamma - 1/2)^{-1} > 1$ and $w(x) = (r+1)\operatorname{sech}^2(rx)$, the diagonal potential $W(x) = \operatorname{diag}(w(x), 0, \dots, 0)$ has exactly one negative eigenvalue, namely $-1$, and equality holds in the inequality.

The one-bound-state case, $\lambda_1^\gamma \le C_\gamma \int \operatorname{tr}(W^p)$, was computed by Keller for scalar potentials in 1961 and appeared again in Lieb and Thirring's 1975 proof that the kinetic energy of a fermion gas is dominated by its density, which settles the stability of matter.  The 1976 contribution of Lieb and Thirring to a Bargmann volume extended the scalar inequality to every $\gamma \ge 1/2$ with the semiclassical constant and conjectured that the one-bound-state constant remains sharp when all negative eigenvalues are summed.  In one dimension the endpoints were treated first.  At $\gamma = 1/2$, Weidl proved finiteness of the moment, and Hundertmark, Lieb and Thomas obtained the constant $1/2$ for scalar potentials; Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl extended the result to operator-valued potentials.  At $\gamma = 3/2$, Laptev and Weidl proved the sharp constant for matrix potentials by trace identities, and Benguria and Loss found an alternative proof by Riccati factorization; Aizenman-Lieb integration covers every $\gamma > 3/2$.  In the open interval, Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl proved a bound with twice the semiclassical constant, Dolbeault, Laptev and Loss sharpened the constant at $\gamma = 1$ using orthonormal trial functions, and Read and Schulz obtained the sharp value at $\gamma = 1$ for operator-valued potentials.  The scalar case of the conjecture in the interval $1/2 < \gamma < 3/2$ was settled affirmatively in 2026.  The present argument covers every finite matrix size in the same interval.

The argument separates into three parts.  A trace comparison shows that an integrated resolvent difference $N$ associated with a Hermitian pair $(B, V)$ satisfies $\operatorname{tr}(NV) \ge \operatorname{tr}(N^q)$ whenever $N \ge 0$; this is the only step missing from the scalar theory, where the corresponding matrix density has rank one.  A smooth matrix field $M_\delta$ on $\operatorname{Sym}_n$ is then constructed so that the primitive $J_\delta$ satisfies $J_\delta(-K) - J_\delta(K) = \sum_i \int_{-k_i}^{k_i} (k_i^2 - s^2)^\sigma\, ds$ for a positive diagonal $K = \operatorname{diag}(k_1, \dots, k_n)$.  A penalized differential equation drives a path from $K$ to $-K$ whose finite-parameter action is bounded by the potential integral.  Letting the penalty vanish and then the smoothing parameter $\delta$ vanish yields the sharp constant.

Throughout, put $\sigma = \gamma - 1/2 \in (0, 1)$, $p = 1 + \sigma$, $q = 1 + 1/\sigma$, and $\beta = \sigma - 1/2$, so that $p^{-1} + q^{-1} = 1$ and $\beta + 1/2 = \sigma$.  The Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a matrix is $\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}} = (\operatorname{tr}(A^* A))^{1/2}$, and $\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ denotes the operator norm.  The space $\operatorname{Sym}_n$ consists of the real symmetric $n \times n$ matrices, with inner product $\operatorname{tr}(BH)$.  Complex inner products are linear in the second variable.  The constant

$$c_\sigma^{-1} = \int_0^\infty t^\beta \left(t^{-1/2} - (t+1)^{-1/2}\right)\,dt$$

is finite, because the integrand is $O(t^{\beta-1/2})$ at zero and $O(t^{\beta-3/2})$ at infinity.  The substitution $t \mapsto yt$ gives

$$c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta \left(t^{-1/2} - (t+y)^{-1/2}\right)\,dt = y^\sigma$$

for every $y \ge 0$.

Two computational facts are used repeatedly.  The first establishes the form domain and isolates the finiteness of the negative spectrum that the definition of $\sum_j \lambda_j^\gamma$ requires.

Lemma.  Let $m \ge 1$, let $p > 1$, and let $W : \mathbb R \to \mathbb C^{m\times m}$ be measurable, Hermitian, positive semidefinite, and satisfy $\int_{\mathbb R}\operatorname{tr}(W^p) < \infty$.  The form $h_W$ is closed and bounded below on $H^1(\mathbb R; \mathbb C^m)$.  Multiplication by $W^{1/2}$ is compact from $H^1(\mathbb R; \mathbb C^m)$ to $L^2(\mathbb R; \mathbb C^m)$, and $H_W$ has only isolated negative eigenvalues of finite multiplicity.

Proof.  Put $w = \|W\|_{\mathrm{op}}$.  Since $w^p \le \operatorname{tr}(W^p)$, $w \in L^p(\mathbb R)$.  The Sobolev estimate

$$\|\psi\|_\infty^2 \le 2 \|\psi\|_2 \|\psi'\|_2$$

holds for vector-valued functions, by applying the scalar estimate to $x \mapsto \|\psi(x)\|$.  Holder's inequality and interpolation then give, for any $\epsilon > 0$,

$$\int_{\mathbb R}\langle\psi, W\psi\rangle\, dx \le \|w\|_p \|\psi\|_{2p/(p-1)}^2 \le 2^{1/p} \|w\|_p \|\psi'\|_2^{1/p} \|\psi\|_2^{2 - 1/p} \le \epsilon \|\psi'\|_2^2 + C_\epsilon \|\psi\|_2^2,$$

by Young's inequality.  A shifted form norm is thus equivalent to the $H^1$ norm, so $h_W$ is closed and bounded below.

Compactness: for $R, L > 0$ put $T_{R,L}\psi = 1_{\{|x| \le R,\ w(x) \le L\}} W^{1/2}\psi$.  Restriction to $[-R, R]$ and the compact embedding $H^1([-R, R]; \mathbb C^m) \subset L^2([-R, R]; \mathbb C^m)$ show that $T_{R,L}$ is compact, and

$$\|(T - T_{R,L})\psi\|_2^2 \le C \|w\, 1_{\{|x| > R\} \cup \{w > L\}}\|_p \|\psi\|_{H^1}^2$$

tends to zero uniformly, so $T$ is compact.

Isolated spectrum: suppose the spectral subspace of $H_W$ on $(-\infty, -a]$ were infinite dimensional for some $a > 0$.  An orthonormal sequence $(\psi_j)$ in it lies in the form domain with uniformly bounded shifted form norm, hence in $H^1(\mathbb R; \mathbb C^m)$ with uniformly bounded norm.  A weakly convergent subsequence in $H^1$ has $H^1$-weak limit zero, because the original sequence is $L^2$-orthonormal.  Compactness of $T$ would then send $\|T\psi_j\|_2 \to 0$.  But $-a \ge h_W[\psi_j] = \|\psi_j'\|_2^2 - \|T\psi_j\|_2^2 \ge -\|T\psi_j\|_2^2$, a contradiction.  So each such spectral subspace is finite dimensional.

The second ingredient isolates the matrix step.  Fix $n \ge 1$ and two Hermitian matrices $B, V \in \mathbb C^{n\times n}$.  Suppose

$$X_s = (sI - B)^2 + V \ge b I \qquad (s \in \mathbb R)$$

for some $b > 0$.  Set

$$Y_s = (sI - B)^2, \qquad R(t) = \pi^{-1}\int_{\mathbb R}(X_s + tI)^{-1}\, ds, \qquad P(t) = t^{-1/2} I - R(t), \qquad N = c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta P(t)\, dt.$$

Diagonalizing $B$ gives $\pi^{-1}\int (Y_s + tI)^{-1}\, ds = t^{-1/2}I$.  From $X_s + tI \ge bI$ and quadratic growth, the difference of resolvents is $O((s^2 + t)^{-2})$ uniformly, so $\|P(t)\|_{\mathrm{op}} = O(t^{-1/2})$ at zero and $O(t^{-3/2})$ at infinity; the matrix $N$ is thus well defined and Hermitian.

Theorem (integrated trace comparison).  With this notation, if $N \ge 0$ then

$$\operatorname{tr}(NV) \ge \operatorname{tr}(N^q).$$

When $V = v > 0$ and $B = 0$, $R(t) = (t+v)^{-1/2}$ and $N = v^\sigma$, so both sides equal $v^q$.  The proof rewrites the difference $\operatorname{tr}(NV) - \operatorname{tr}(N^q)$ as an integral of nonnegative terms.  Solutions of a constant-coefficient differential equation on the two half-lines will express $R(t)$ in a form that separates into commuting pieces and a positive remainder.

Half-line solutions.  Fix $t > 0$ and put $\nabla = \partial_x - iB$.  Consider the differential equation

$$(-\nabla^2 + V + tI)\psi = 0, \qquad \psi \in H^1(\mathbb R; \mathbb C^n).$$

Lemma.  For every $v \in \mathbb C^n$ the equation above has a unique $H^1$ solution on each half-line $x > 0$ and $x < 0$ with boundary value $v$ at zero.  There exist Hermitian matrices $S = S(t)$ and $T = T(t)$, independent of $v$, such that

$$\nabla\psi(0+) = -Sv, \qquad \nabla\psi(0-) = Tv,$$

and

$$V + tI = S^2 + i[B,S] = T^2 - i[B,T], \qquad \frac{S + T}{2} = R(t)^{-1}.$$

Every eigenvalue of $B + iS$ has strictly positive imaginary part.

Proof.  Define the sesquilinear form

$$a_t(\phi, \psi) = \int\left(\langle \nabla\phi, \nabla\psi\rangle + \langle \phi, (V+tI)\psi\rangle\right) dx$$

on the trace-zero subspace of $H^1$ on each half-line.  The Fourier symbol of $a_t$ on the whole line is $X_s + tI$.  The lower bound $X_s + tI \ge bI$ combined with $X_s = s^2 I - 2sB + B^2 + V$ produces $c_t > 0$ with $X_s + tI \ge c_t(1 + s^2)I$; the same bound holds on each half-line by zero extension.  Coercivity follows, and Riesz on the trace-zero space produces a unique solution for any boundary value.  The equation forces $\psi'' \in L^2$, so $\psi$ and $\psi'$ have boundary traces and both tend to zero at the infinite end of each half-line.

Define $S$ and $T$ by $\nabla\psi(0+) = -Sv$ and $\nabla\psi(0-) = Tv$.  Integration by parts gives $a_t(\psi_v, \psi_w) = \langle v, Sw\rangle$ on the right and $a_t(\psi_v, \psi_w) = \langle v, Tw\rangle$ on the left.  Since $a_t$ is Hermitian, both matrices are Hermitian.  Translation invariance and uniqueness identify the derivative relations

$$\nabla\psi = -S\psi \quad (x > 0), \qquad \nabla\psi = T\psi \quad (x < 0).$$

Substituting the first into the equation and evaluating at zero for arbitrary $v$ gives $V + tI = S^2 + i[B, S]$; the second similarly gives $V + tI = T^2 - i[B, T]$.

Glue the two solutions with a common boundary value $v$ at zero.  The resulting function is continuous, its derivative has jump $-(S+T)v$, and it satisfies

$$(-\nabla^2 + V + tI)\psi = \delta_0 (S+T)v$$

as a distribution.  Using the Fourier transform with forward kernel $e^{-isx}$ and evaluating the inverse transform at zero gives

$$v = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}(X_s + tI)^{-1}\, ds\, (S+T)v = \frac{1}{2}R(t)(S+T)v,$$

so $(S+T)/2 = R(t)^{-1}$ since $R(t) > 0$.

For the last claim, $\nabla\psi = -S\psi$ gives $\psi' = i(B+iS)\psi$ on $(0, \infty)$.  If $(B+iS)w = zw$ with $w \ne 0$, uniqueness for this first-order initial value problem gives $\psi(x) = e^{izx}w$.  The function is in $L^2(0, \infty; \mathbb C^n)$ only when $\operatorname{Im} z > 0$.

The resolvent decomposition.  Put

$$L = \frac{S+T}{2} = R(t)^{-1}, \qquad Z = \frac{S-T}{2}.$$

Both are Hermitian and $L > 0$.  Adding and subtracting the two forms of $V + tI$,

$$V + tI = L^2 + Z^2 + i[B,Z], \qquad LZ + ZL + i[B,L] = 0.$$

In an eigenbasis of $L$ the second identity has diagonal entries $2L_{jj}Z_{jj} = 0$, so $\operatorname{tr} Z = 0$.  Multiplying the second identity on both sides by $R = L^{-1}$ gives $i[R, B] = -(ZR + RZ)$; cyclicity of the trace yields $i\operatorname{tr}(R[B, Z]) = -2\operatorname{tr}(RZ^2)$, and therefore

$$\operatorname{tr}(R(V+tI)) = \operatorname{tr} L - \operatorname{tr}(RZ^2).$$

The quantity $\operatorname{tr}(RZ^2) = \operatorname{tr}(Z R Z)$ is nonnegative.

The scalar integrals $F$ and $G$.  Introduce

$$F(t) = \frac{\operatorname{tr} V}{\sqrt t} - \pi^{-1}\int_{\mathbb R}\log\frac{\det(X_s + tI)}{\det(Y_s + tI)}\, ds.$$

The determinants are positive.  Factoring with $(Y_s + tI)^{1/2}$ shows the logarithm is $O(|s|^{-2})$ at infinity, so the integral converges absolutely.  Its derivative in $t$ is $\operatorname{tr}(t^{-1/2}I - R(t))$ and has an integrable bound locally uniform in $t$; hence $F$ is continuously differentiable on $(0, \infty)$.

The first Riccati identity factors

$$X_s + tI = (sI - B + iS)(sI - B - iS).$$

Let $u_j + i v_j$, $1 \le j \le n$, be the eigenvalues of $B + iS$.  From the last part of the half-line lemma, $v_j > 0$.  Taking determinants gives

$$\det(X_s + tI) = \prod_{j=1}^n ((s - u_j)^2 + v_j^2).$$

The denominator has the same form, with centres the eigenvalues of $B$ and widths all equal to $\sqrt t$.  Symmetric-cutoff integrations move the centres to zero without changing the infinite integral; the scalar identity

$$\int_{\mathbb R}\log\frac{s^2 + v^2}{s^2 + w^2}\, ds = 2\pi(v - w) \qquad (v, w > 0)$$

then gives

$$F(t) = \frac{\operatorname{tr} V}{\sqrt t} - 2\operatorname{tr}(L - \sqrt t\, I).$$

Set

$$G(t) = \operatorname{tr}(L - 2\sqrt t\, I + tR(t)).$$

Each scalar eigenvalue $r > 0$ of $R(t)$ contributes $r^{-1} - 2\sqrt t + tr = (1 - \sqrt t\, r)^2 / r \ge 0$, so $G(t) \ge 0$.  Taking traces in $V + tI = L^2 + Z^2 + i[B, Z]$ and substituting the formula for $F$ gives

$$F(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt t}\operatorname{tr}\left((L - \sqrt t\, I)^2 + Z^2\right) \ge 0.$$

Comparing the two expressions for $F$ with $\operatorname{tr}(P(t)V)$,

$$\operatorname{tr}(P(t)V) = F(t) + G(t) + \operatorname{tr}(R(t)Z^2).$$

The bounds already proved place both $F$ and $G$ at $O(t^{-1/2})$ near zero and $O(t^{-3/2})$ near infinity; both are integrable against $t^\beta\, dt$, and $t^{\beta+1} F(t) \to 0$ at both endpoints.  Differentiating the definition of $F$ and simplifying gives $tF'(t) = -F(t)/2 - G(t)$, and integrating the derivative of $t^{\beta+1}F(t)$ over a compact subinterval then passing to the endpoints gives $\int t^\beta G(t)\, dt = \sigma \int t^\beta F(t)\, dt$.  Consequently

$$\operatorname{tr}(NV) = q c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta G(t)\, dt + c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta\operatorname{tr}(R(t)Z^2)\, dt.$$

Comparison with an external square.  Fix a positive semidefinite matrix $D$ and put

$$F_0(t, D) = \frac{\operatorname{tr} D}{\sqrt t} - 2\operatorname{tr}\left((tI + D)^{1/2} - \sqrt t\, I\right).$$

For a scalar eigenvalue $d$ of $D$ the contribution to $F_0$ is $\int_0^d (t^{-1/2} - (t+y)^{-1/2})\, dy \ge 0$, so by Tonelli and the identity $c_\sigma\int t^\beta (t^{-1/2} - (t+y)^{-1/2})\, dt = y^\sigma$,

$$c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta F_0(t, D)\, dt = p^{-1} \operatorname{tr}(D^p).$$

The Hilbert-Schmidt square

$$\mathcal D_D(t) = \|R^{-1/2} - R^{1/2}(tI + D)^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 = \operatorname{tr}(R^{-1} + R(tI+D) - 2(tI+D)^{1/2})$$

is nonnegative and equals $G(t) - \operatorname{tr}(P(t)D) + F_0(t, D)$.  Each of the three terms is integrable against $t^\beta\, dt$, so

$$c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta G(t)\, dt = \operatorname{tr}(ND) - p^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(D^p) + c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta \mathcal D_D(t)\, dt.$$

Assume $N \ge 0$ and choose $D = N^{1/\sigma}$.  Since $p/\sigma = q$, $\operatorname{tr}(ND) - p^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(D^p) = q^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(N^q)$.  Combining with the weighted identity for $F$ and $G$ gives the deficit formula

$$\operatorname{tr}(NV) - \operatorname{tr}(N^q) = c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta\left(\operatorname{tr}(R(t)Z(t)^2) + q\,\mathcal D_{N^{1/\sigma}}(t)\right)\, dt.$$

Both integrands are nonnegative, so $\operatorname{tr}(NV) \ge \operatorname{tr}(N^q)$.  If $B$ and $V$ commute both integrals vanish, recovering the scalar equality; the two nonnegative terms in the deficit carry the discrepancy in the noncommuting case.

The trace comparison just proved is the missing matrix step.  The remaining ingredients follow the finite-interval method used for the scalar case, with one modification: where the scalar method invokes a comparison for positive densities of rank at most one, the present argument relies entirely on the comparison at positive values of a regularized matrix field.

The field.  Fix a positive diagonal matrix $K = \operatorname{diag}(k_1, \dots, k_n)$ and put $\kappa = \max_i k_i$.  For each $\delta > 0$ the argument requires a field $M_\delta : \operatorname{Sym}_n \to \operatorname{Sym}_n$ with three properties.  First, $M_\delta$ is locally Lipschitz and has a $C^1$ primitive $J_\delta$ with $dJ_\delta(B)[H] = -\operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)H)$ for all $B, H \in \operatorname{Sym}_n$.  Second, $M_\delta$ is equivariant under $B \mapsto SBS$ for any diagonal sign matrix $S$, and its action on the standard basis vector $e_i$ is diagonal whenever $B$ is diagonal.  Third, whenever $M_\delta(B) \ge 0$,

$$\operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)(K^2 - B^2)) \ge \operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)^q) - E_\delta, \qquad E_\delta = n(\delta \kappa^{2\sigma} + \delta^\sigma(\kappa^2 + \delta)),$$

and $E_\delta \to 0$ as $\delta \downarrow 0$ for fixed $K$.

Construction.  For $y \ge 0$ set

$$f_\delta(y) = \frac{c_\sigma}{\pi}\int_0^\infty t^\beta\left(\frac{1}{t + \delta} - \frac{1}{t + \delta + y}\right) dt.$$

For $y < 0$ put $f_\delta(y) = f_\delta'(0)y$, the tangent-line extension at zero.  Differentiation under the integral and the substitution $t = (y + \delta)u$ give

$$f_\delta'(y) = a_\sigma (y + \delta)^{\beta - 1}, \qquad a_\sigma = \frac{c_\sigma}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{u^\beta}{(1 + u)^2}\, du > 0 \qquad (y \ge 0).$$

Both integrals converge because $-1/2 < \beta < 1/2$.  Hence $f_\delta$ is $C^1$, strictly increasing, and concave on $\mathbb R$, and the tangent extension brings every real symmetric matrix, including matrices with negative eigenvalues, into the domain of $f_\delta$.

For $s \in \mathbb R$ and $B \in \operatorname{Sym}_n$ put

$$Q_s(B) = s^2 I - 2s B + K^2, \qquad M_\delta(B) = \lim_{r \to \infty}\int_{-r}^r (f_\delta(Q_s(B)) - f_\delta(s^2) I)\, ds.$$

The symmetric cutoff cancels the leading odd-in-$s$ term.

Lemma (fields and primitives).  The limit defining $M_\delta(B)$ exists locally uniformly on $\operatorname{Sym}_n$, and $M_\delta$ is locally Lipschitz.  There is a $C^1$ function $J_\delta : \operatorname{Sym}_n \to \mathbb R$, normalized by $J_\delta(0) = 0$, with

$$dJ_\delta(B)[H] = -\operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)H) \qquad (B, H \in \operatorname{Sym}_n).$$

Proof.  Two facts about Hilbert-Schmidt norms are used.  If $h$ is scalar Lipschitz on an interval containing the spectra of Hermitian matrices $X$, $Y$, then

$$\|h(X) - h(Y)\|_{\mathrm{HS}} \le \operatorname{Lip}(h)\, \|X - Y\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.$$

Indeed, in eigenbases $(e_i)$ of $X$ and $(v_j)$ of $Y$ the $(i,j)$ entries of the two matrix differences are $(h(\lambda_i) - h(\nu_j))\langle e_i, v_j\rangle$ and $(\lambda_i - \nu_j)\langle e_i, v_j\rangle$; the Lipschitz bound, followed by summation of squared moduli, proves the inequality.

Restrict $B$ to a bounded set.  For large $|s|$ the spectrum of $E_s(B) = -2s B + K^2$ lies in $[-a|s|, a|s|]$ with $a$ independent of $B$.  On this interval put

$$h_s(y) = f_\delta(s^2 + y) - f_\delta(s^2) - f_\delta'(s^2)y.$$

By the derivative formula, $\sup_{|y| \le a|s|} |h_s'(y)| = O(|s|^{2\beta - 3})$.  Since $h_s(0) = 0$ and $\|E_s(B)\|_{\mathrm{HS}} = O(|s|)$, the Hilbert-Schmidt Lipschitz calculus gives

$$f_\delta(Q_s(B)) - f_\delta(s^2)I = -2s f_\delta'(s^2)B + T_s(B),$$

with $\|T_s(B)\|_{\mathrm{HS}} = O(|s|^{2\beta - 2})$ and $\|T_s(B_1) - T_s(B_2)\|_{\mathrm{HS}} \le C|s|^{2\beta - 2}\|B_1 - B_2\|_{\mathrm{HS}}$ uniformly on the chosen bounded set.  The linear term integrates to zero on $[-r, r]$, and $2\beta - 2 = 2\sigma - 3 < -1$ makes both remainder bounds integrable at infinity.  Local Lipschitz continuity on bounded $s$-intervals is immediate.  Convergence and regularity follow.

To construct the primitive, choose $g_\delta$ with $g_\delta' = f_\delta$.  The trace differentiation identity

$$d(\operatorname{tr} g_\delta(X))[H] = \operatorname{tr}(f_\delta(X) H)$$

follows first for polynomials by cyclicity of the trace, then for $g_\delta$ by $C^1$ polynomial approximation on compact intervals containing the relevant spectra.  For $s \ne 0$ the function

$$B \mapsto \frac{\operatorname{tr}(g_\delta(Q_s(B)) - g_\delta(K^2))}{2s} + f_\delta(s^2)\operatorname{tr} B$$

has differential $-\operatorname{tr}((f_\delta(Q_s(B)) - f_\delta(s^2)I)H)$; at $s = 0$ the field vanishes and the primitive is linear.  Hence the path integral of the negative field around every piecewise smooth closed curve vanishes at every finite cutoff, and local uniform convergence passes the identity to $M_\delta$.  Path integration on the vector space $\operatorname{Sym}_n$ produces $J_\delta$ with the required relation, and continuity of $M_\delta$ gives $J_\delta \in C^1$.

Scalar shifts and matrix directions.  The scalar companion of the field is

$$\mu_\delta(z) = \int_{\mathbb R}(f_\delta(s^2 + z) - f_\delta(s^2))\, ds, \qquad z \in \mathbb R.$$

The integral converges absolutely, and on bounded sets of $z$ its integrand and its Lipschitz constant in $z$ are $O(|s|^{2\beta - 2})$ at infinity; so $\mu_\delta$ is locally Lipschitz.  Strict increase of $f_\delta$ makes $\mu_\delta$ strictly increasing, and $\mu_\delta(0) = 0$.  For $z \ge 0$, Tonelli's theorem and $\pi^{-1}\int_{\mathbb R}(s^2 + a)^{-1}\, ds = a^{-1/2}$ give

$$\mu_\delta(z) = c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta\left((t + \delta)^{-1/2} - (t + \delta + z)^{-1/2}\right) dt = (z + \delta)^\sigma - \delta^\sigma.$$

The last equality is the difference of $y^\sigma$ at $y = \delta + z$ and $y = \delta$.

Field properties.  A translation in the integration variable extends the previous formula for $\mu_\delta$ to all $z$.  For fixed $z$ and $a$, put $F_z(s) = f_\delta(s^2 + z)$.  This function is even, and $F_z'(s) = O(|s|^{2\beta - 1}) \to 0$.  For $a \ge 0$, pairing the intervals $[r, r+a]$ and $[r-a, r]$ gives

$$\left|\int_{-r}^r (F_z(s-a) - F_z(s))\, ds\right| \le |a|^2 \sup_{r - |a| \le u \le r + |a|} |F_z'(u)| \to 0 \qquad (r \to \infty),$$

and therefore

$$\lim_{r \to \infty}\int_{-r}^r \left(f_\delta((s-a)^2 + z) - f_\delta(s^2)\right) ds = \mu_\delta(z).$$

Lemma (field properties).  For every $B \in \operatorname{Sym}_n$ and every unit vector $e \in \mathbb R^n$,

$$e^{\mathrm T} M_\delta(B) e \le \mu_\delta\left(e^{\mathrm T} K^2 e - (e^{\mathrm T} B e)^2\right).$$

If $B e_i = y e_i$ for a standard basis vector $e_i$ and $y \in \mathbb R$, then

$$M_\delta(B) e_i = \mu_\delta(k_i^2 - y^2) e_i.$$

If $S$ is a diagonal matrix with entries in $\{1, -1\}$, then $M_\delta(S B S) = S M_\delta(B) S$.  Finally,

$$J_\delta(-K) - J_\delta(K) = \sum_{i=1}^n \int_{-k_i}^{k_i} \mu_\delta(k_i^2 - s^2)\, ds.$$

Proof.  Scalar concavity, applied in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $Q_s(B)$, gives

$$e^{\mathrm T} f_\delta(Q_s(B)) e \le f_\delta\left(e^{\mathrm T} Q_s(B) e\right).$$

With $a = e^{\mathrm T} B e$ and $z = e^{\mathrm T} K^2 e - a^2$ the right side reads $f_\delta((s - a)^2 + z)$.  Subtracting $f_\delta(s^2)$, integrating over $[-r, r]$, and applying the shift identity produces the first claim.

If $B e_i = y e_i$, then $e_i$ is an eigenvector of $Q_s(B)$ with eigenvalue $(s - y)^2 + k_i^2 - y^2$, and functional calculus together with the shift identity gives the second claim.

For the third, $S K^2 S = K^2$ gives $Q_s(SBS) = S Q_s(B) S$, and conjugating inside the definition of $M_\delta$ proves equivariance.

On the diagonal subspace, the coordinate formula and the primitive identity give

$$dJ_\delta(\operatorname{diag}(b_1, \dots, b_n)) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \mu_\delta(k_i^2 - b_i^2)\, db_i,$$

and integrating from $(k_1, \dots, k_n)$ to $(-k_1, \dots, -k_n)$ within this subspace proves the endpoint formula.

Comparison at positive field values.  Riccati equations in matrix form also underlie the commutation approach to matrix Schrodinger inequalities.  The trace engine fits here: given a field value $B$ at which the field is positive, the trace engine bounds the quadratic form of the field against $\operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)^q)$.

./arxiv-math-ph_9906024.pdf

Proposition.  For every $B \in \operatorname{Sym}_n$ with $M_\delta(B) \ge 0$,

$$\operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)(K^2 - B^2)) \ge \operatorname{tr}(M_\delta(B)^q) - E_\delta.$$

Proof.  Write $M = M_\delta(B)$.  By the Jensen bound and strict increase of $\mu_\delta$, positivity of $M$ implies $e^{\mathrm T} K^2 e \ge (e^{\mathrm T} B e)^2$ for every unit vector $e$; consequently

$$e^{\mathrm T} Q_s(B) e = (s - e^{\mathrm T} B e)^2 + e^{\mathrm T} K^2 e - (e^{\mathrm T} B e)^2 \ge 0.$$

So $Q_s(B) \ge 0$ for every $s \in \mathbb R$, and complexification by $z^* Q_s(B) z = u^{\mathrm T} Q_s(B) u + v^{\mathrm T} Q_s(B) v$ for $z = u + iv$ gives positivity on $\mathbb C^n$ as well.  The same directional estimate combined with $\mu_\delta(z) \le z^\sigma$ gives

$$0 \le M \le \mu_\delta(\kappa^2) I \le \kappa^{2\sigma} I.$$

Put

$$D = K^2 - B^2, \qquad V = D + \delta I, \qquad Y_s = (sI - B)^2,$$

so that $X_s = Y_s + V = Q_s(B) + \delta I \ge \delta I$.  With $R(t)$, $N$, $P(t)$ as in the trace engine, it is claimed that

$$N = M + \delta^\sigma I.$$

To see this, diagonalize $B$ and apply the shift identity at $z = 0$ to each diagonal entry, replacing $f_\delta(s^2)I$ with $f_\delta(Y_s)$ in the definition of $M_\delta$.  Both $Q_s(B)$ and $Y_s$ are positive semidefinite, so the difference has the resolvent expression

$$\frac{c_\sigma}{\pi}\int_0^\infty t^\beta\left((Y_s + (t+\delta)I)^{-1} - (Q_s(B) + (t+\delta)I)^{-1}\right) dt.$$

The matrices inside the inverses dominate $c(1 + t + s^2)I$, their difference before inversion is the constant $D$, and the resolvent identity bounds the weighted difference by $C\|D\|_{\mathrm{op}}\, t^\beta(1 + t + s^2)^{-2}$, which is integrable on $(0, \infty) \times \mathbb R$.  Hence Fubini and the substitution $y = \delta$ in the scaling identity give

$$M = c_\sigma \int_0^\infty t^\beta\left((t + \delta)^{-1/2} I - R(t)\right) dt,$$

and the definition of $N$ gives $N = M + \delta^\sigma I$.

Since $N \ge 0$, the trace engine applies:

$$\operatorname{tr}(MD) = \operatorname{tr}(NV) - \delta \operatorname{tr} M - \delta^\sigma \operatorname{tr} V \ge \operatorname{tr}(N^q) - \delta \operatorname{tr} M - \delta^\sigma \operatorname{tr} V.$$

Because $N = M + \delta^\sigma I$, the matrices $N$ and $M$ share an eigenbasis and $\operatorname{tr}(N^q) \ge \operatorname{tr}(M^q)$.  From $0 \le M \le \kappa^{2\sigma} I$ the trace of $M$ is at most $n\kappa^{2\sigma}$, and from $V = K^2 - B^2 + \delta I \le (\kappa^2 + \delta)I$ the trace of $V$ is at most $n(\kappa^2 + \delta)$.  Substitution gives the stated comparison.

The comparison handles the nonlinear term $\operatorname{tr}((A A^{\mathrm T})^q)$ at any pair $(A, B)$ with $M_\delta(B) = A A^{\mathrm T}$.  The remaining step turns such pairs into the endpoints $K$ and $-K$ by a penalized ordinary differential equation.

The connecting path.  The action estimate requires a path from $K$ to $-K$ whose finite-parameter equation ties the field to a matrix density.  Let $\Omega = (x_-, x_+) \subset \mathbb R$ be a bounded interval, fix $n, d \ge 1$, and let $U \in H^1_0(\Omega; \mathbb R^{n \times d})$ satisfy

$$\int_\Omega U(x) U(x)^{\mathrm T}\, dx = I_n.$$

The rows of $U$ are orthonormal as $\mathbb R^d$-valued functions.  Let $\mathcal L$ denote the real lower triangular $n \times n$ matrices.

Proposition (connecting paths).  For every $\delta > 0$ and $\epsilon > 0$ there exist $C_\epsilon \in \mathcal L$ and $B_\epsilon \in C^1(\bar\Omega; \operatorname{Sym}_n)$ such that, with $A_\epsilon = C_\epsilon U$,

$$\epsilon B_\epsilon' = M_\delta(B_\epsilon) - A_\epsilon A_\epsilon^{\mathrm T}, \qquad B_\epsilon(x_-) = K, \qquad B_\epsilon(x_+) = -K,$$

and

$$\sup_{x \in \bar\Omega}\|B_\epsilon(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le \kappa, \qquad \sup_{0 < \epsilon \le 1}\|C_\epsilon\|_{\mathrm{HS}} < \infty.$$

Proof.  Fix $\delta > 0$.  The rows of $U$ have continuous representatives, because $\Omega$ is bounded and $U \in H^1$.  Choose a smooth nonnegative function $\chi$ on $\operatorname{Sym}_n$, compactly supported, equal to one on $\{B : \|B\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le \kappa\}$, and invariant under conjugation by diagonal sign matrices; averaging any cutoff over the $2^n$ sign matrices produces such a function.  By the field lemma, the field $\hat M_\delta(B) = \chi(B) M_\delta(B)$ is bounded and globally Lipschitz.

The endpoint map.  For fixed $\epsilon > 0$, $C \in \mathcal L$, and $0 \le \theta \le 1$, solve

$$\epsilon B' = \theta \hat M_\delta(B) - (CU)(CU)^{\mathrm T}, \qquad B(x_-) = K,$$

and set $\Phi(C, \theta) = B(x_+) + K \in \operatorname{Sym}_n$.  A zero of $\Phi(\cdot, 1)$ satisfies the required endpoint and equation.  The bounded Lipschitz field and the continuous forcing give a unique $C^1$ solution, and continuous dependence on parameters makes $\Phi$ continuous.

Let $\mathcal G$ be the group of diagonal matrices with entries in $\{1, -1\}$.  It acts on $\mathcal L$ by $C \mapsto SC$ and on $\operatorname{Sym}_n$ by $B \mapsto S B S$.  The field is equivariant under these actions, and $S K S = K$; uniqueness of the ODE solution then gives

$$\Phi(S C, \theta) = S \Phi(C, \theta) S \qquad (S \in \mathcal G).$$

Zeros of $\Phi$ lie in a bounded set.  Integrating the ODE at a zero and using $U U^{\mathrm T}$ orthonormality gives

$$C C^{\mathrm T} = 2\epsilon K + \theta \int_\Omega \hat M_\delta(B(x))\, dx,$$

so for $0 < \epsilon \le 1$,

$$\|C\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \le 2\operatorname{tr} K + n|\Omega| \sup_B \|\hat M_\delta(B)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.$$

The joint zero set is compact.

The sign action is free at every zero.  If a row of $C$ vanished, the sign matrix $S_i$ flipping only that row would fix $C$, and equivariance together with uniqueness would force $S_i B(x) S_i = B(x)$ for all $x$, so $B(x) e_i = y(x) e_i$ for a scalar function $y$.  The coordinate identity gives

$$\epsilon y' = \theta \chi(B(x)) \mu_\delta(k_i^2 - y^2), \qquad y(x_-) = k_i.$$

Since $\mu_\delta$ is locally Lipschitz with $\mu_\delta(0) = 0$ and $\chi(B(x))$ is continuous, this scalar equation has the unique solution $y \equiv k_i$, contradicting $y(x_+) = -k_i$.

At $\theta = 0$ the map is explicit:

$$\Phi(C, 0) = 2K - \epsilon^{-1} C C^{\mathrm T}.$$

Its zeros in $\mathcal L$ are exactly the diagonal matrices $C = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n)$ with $d_i = \pm\sqrt{2\epsilon k_i}$.  The first row of $C C^{\mathrm T} = 2\epsilon K$ determines $d_1$ and forces every other entry in the first column of $C$ to vanish; induction gives the remaining rows.  These $2^n$ zeros form one $\mathcal G$-orbit.  Each is regular: for a lower triangular variation $H$, the $C$-derivative of $\Phi(\cdot, 0)$ has diagonal entries $-2\epsilon^{-1} d_i H_{ii}$ and entries $-\epsilon^{-1} d_j H_{ij}$ for $i > j$, giving an isomorphism of $\dim \mathcal L = n(n+1)/2$ onto $\dim \operatorname{Sym}_n = n(n+1)/2$.

Continuation.  Suppose $\Phi(C, 1)$ never vanishes.  Then compactness produces a bounded invariant open set $O \subset \mathcal L$ containing the projection of every zero, with $\bar O$ contained in the open set where no row vanishes; on $\Gamma = (\partial O \times [0, 1]) \cup (\bar O \times \{1\})$ the map $\Phi$ has a positive lower bound in Hilbert-Schmidt norm.  Reparametrize $\theta$ by a smooth $\rho : [0, 1] \to [0, 1]$ that equals zero near $0$ and $\rho(1) = 1$, so $F(C, \theta) = \Phi(C, \rho(\theta))$ agrees with the smooth $\Phi(\cdot, 0)$ on an initial collar $\{0 \le \theta \le a\}$.

Approximate $F$ uniformly on $\bar O \times [0, 1]$ by a smooth equivariant $\Psi$ that equals $\Phi(\cdot, 0)$ on a smaller initial collar; the approximation is a smoothing in the finite-dimensional variables followed by an equivariant averaging, and can be as close as needed.  The positive gap on $\Gamma$ persists.

Next make zero a regular value in the interior by a small equivariant perturbation.  Around any $C_0 \in \bar O$ choose a ball whose sign-translates are disjoint and a bump function supported in that ball with $b(C_0) = 1$.  For $H \in \operatorname{Sym}_n$, the map $V_H(C) = \sum_{S \in \mathcal G} b(SC) S H S$ is smooth and equivariant, with $V_H(C_0) = H$.  A finite covering produces equivariant maps $V_1, \dots, V_N$ whose values span $\operatorname{Sym}_n$ at every point of $\bar O$.

Choose $h : [0, 1] \to [0, \infty)$ smooth, vanishing near zero inside the initial collar, positive afterwards, and put

$$\Psi_\alpha(C, \theta) = \Psi(C, \theta) + h(\theta)\sum_{j=1}^N \alpha_j V_j(C).$$

Restrict $\alpha$ to a small ball about zero so the gap on $\Gamma$ survives.  At an interior zero of the joint map $(C, \theta, \alpha) \mapsto \Psi_\alpha(C, \theta)$, the differential is onto: when $h(\theta) > 0$ the parameter directions span $\operatorname{Sym}_n$, when $h(\theta) = 0$ the $C$-derivative is the isomorphism already computed for $\Phi(\cdot, 0)$.

The interior zero set of this parameter family is a smooth manifold.  Its projection onto the parameter ball is smooth; Sard's theorem provides regular values of that projection arbitrarily close to zero.

./sard-1942.pdf

At a regular value $\alpha$, zero is a regular value of the map $(C, \theta) \mapsto \Psi_\alpha(C, \theta)$.  Indeed, write the differential of the parameter family as $(L_1, L_2)$ in the $(C, \theta)$ and $\alpha$ directions; if the projection is onto, then for every $w$ there exists $v$ with $L_1 v + L_2 w = 0$, so $\operatorname{ran} L_2 \subset \operatorname{ran} L_1$, and since $(L_1, L_2)$ is onto so is $L_1$.

For this $\alpha$ the zero set of $\Psi_\alpha$ in $O \times [0, 1]$ is a compact smooth one-dimensional manifold with boundary.  There are no zeros on $\Gamma$, and near $\theta = 0$ the map agrees with $\Phi(\cdot, 0)$ and has an invertible $C$-derivative, so the boundary consists precisely of the $2^n$ initial zeros.  The sign group acts freely; the quotient is a compact one-dimensional manifold with boundary, because a small neighbourhood of each point can be chosen disjoint from its nontrivial translates and inherits the same local interval or half-interval coordinate.  All initial zeros lie in one orbit, so the quotient has exactly one boundary point.  Every compact one-dimensional manifold is a finite disjoint union of circles and closed intervals, and hence has an even number of boundary points.  This contradiction proves that $\Phi(\cdot, 1)$ has a zero.  The argument is the standard regular-value proof of parity invariance after quotienting by row signs.

Confinement.  Let $C, B$ arise from such a zero.  For a real unit vector $e$ put $y(x) = e^{\mathrm T} B(x) e$.  From the Jensen bound, whenever $|y(x)| > \kappa$,

$$e^{\mathrm T} M_\delta(B(x)) e \le \mu_\delta(e^{\mathrm T} K^2 e - y(x)^2) < 0.$$

Since $\chi \ge 0$ and $(CU)(CU)^{\mathrm T} \ge 0$, the differential equation gives $y'(x) \le 0$ on this set.  Both endpoints $y(x_\pm)$ lie in $[-\kappa, \kappa]$.  A connected interval on which $y > \kappa$ cannot start at $y = \kappa$ with $y$ nonincreasing, and an interval on which $y < -\kappa$ cannot end at $y = -\kappa$ with $y$ nonincreasing.  Hence $|e^{\mathrm T} B(x) e| \le \kappa$ for every unit vector $e$ and every $x$, so $\|B(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le \kappa$, $\chi(B(x)) = 1$, and the penalized equation reduces to the one in the statement.  The earlier $H^1$ bound gives the uniform bound on $C_\epsilon$.

The action inequality.  Assemble the previous pieces on a bounded interval.  Fix $n, d \ge 1$, a bounded interval $\Omega = (x_-, x_+) \subset \mathbb R$, a positive diagonal matrix $K = \operatorname{diag}(k_1, \dots, k_n)$, and $U \in H^1_0(\Omega; \mathbb R^{n \times d})$ with $\int_\Omega U U^{\mathrm T}\, dx = I_n$.  For a lower triangular $C \in \mathcal L$ put $A = CU$ and

$$\mathcal E(C) = \int_\Omega \left(\|A'\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 + \operatorname{tr}(K^2 A A^{\mathrm T}) - \operatorname{tr}((A A^{\mathrm T})^q)\right) dx.$$

The integral is finite, because one-dimensional $H^1$ functions on a bounded interval are continuous.

Theorem (action inequality).  For every $0 < \sigma < 1$,

$$\sup_{C \in \mathcal L} \mathcal E(C) \ge \sum_{i=1}^n \int_{-k_i}^{k_i} (k_i^2 - s^2)^\sigma\, ds.$$

The order of the $k_i$ does not affect this statement.

Proof.  Fix $\delta > 0$ and $\epsilon \in (0, 1]$.  Let $C_\epsilon, B_\epsilon, A_\epsilon = C_\epsilon U$ be as in the connecting path proposition.  Temporarily suppress the subscript.  Since $A \in H^1_0$ and $B \in C^1$, the function

$$x \mapsto J_\delta(B(x)) + \operatorname{tr}(A(x)^{\mathrm T} B(x) A(x))$$

is absolutely continuous.  The primitive identity and the path equation give, almost everywhere,

$$\left(J_\delta(B) + \operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathrm T} B A)\right)' = 2\operatorname{tr}((A')^{\mathrm T} B A) - \operatorname{tr}\left((M_\delta(B) - A A^{\mathrm T}) B'\right)$$

$$= 2\operatorname{tr}((A')^{\mathrm T} B A) - \epsilon^{-1}\|M_\delta(B) - A A^{\mathrm T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.$$

The Hilbert-Schmidt Cauchy-Schwarz inequality followed by $2ab \le a^2 + b^2$ bounds this by

$$\|A'\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 + \operatorname{tr}(B^2 A A^{\mathrm T}) - \epsilon^{-1}\|M_\delta(B) - A A^{\mathrm T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.$$

Both $A(x_-)$ and $A(x_+)$ vanish because $U$ is compactly supported.  Integration over $\Omega$ gives

$$J_\delta(-K) - J_\delta(K) \le \mathcal E(C) + \int_\Omega\left(\operatorname{tr}((A A^{\mathrm T})^q) - \operatorname{tr}((K^2 - B^2) A A^{\mathrm T}) - \epsilon^{-1}\|M_\delta(B) - A A^{\mathrm T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\right) dx.$$

Let

$$F(A, B) = \operatorname{tr}((A A^{\mathrm T})^q) - \operatorname{tr}((K^2 - B^2) A A^{\mathrm T}), \qquad r(A, B) = \|M_\delta(B) - A A^{\mathrm T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.$$

The bounds in the connecting path place all $(A_\epsilon(x), B_\epsilon(x))$, $x \in \bar\Omega$, $0 < \epsilon \le 1$, in one compact set $\mathcal K_\delta \subset \mathbb R^{n \times d} \times \operatorname{Sym}_n$.  If $r(A, B) = 0$, then $M_\delta(B) = A A^{\mathrm T} \ge 0$ and the comparison at positive fields gives $F(A, B) \le E_\delta$.  Compactness then implies

$$\limsup_{\epsilon \downarrow 0} \sup_{(A, B) \in \mathcal K_\delta}\left(F(A, B) - \epsilon^{-1} r(A, B)\right) \le E_\delta.$$

A failure would give $\eta > 0$, a sequence $\epsilon_j \downarrow 0$, and $(A_j, B_j) \in \mathcal K_\delta$ with $F(A_j, B_j) - \epsilon_j^{-1} r(A_j, B_j) > E_\delta + \eta$.  Boundedness of $F$ forces $r(A_j, B_j) = O(\epsilon_j)$; a convergent subsequence has limit $(A_*, B_*)$ with $r(A_*, B_*) = 0$ and $F(A_*, B_*) \ge E_\delta + \eta$, contradicting $F \le E_\delta$ at $r = 0$.

Apply the limsup bound inside the penalized inequality, replace $\mathcal E(C_\epsilon)$ by the supremum over lower triangular $C$, and let $\epsilon \downarrow 0$.  The result is

$$J_\delta(-K) - J_\delta(K) \le \sup_{C \in \mathcal L} \mathcal E(C) + |\Omega| E_\delta,$$

uniformly in $\delta$.  The endpoint formula gives

$$J_\delta(-K) - J_\delta(K) = \sum_{i=1}^n \int_{-k_i}^{k_i} \mu_\delta(k_i^2 - s^2)\, ds.$$

For $z \ge 0$, $0 \le \mu_\delta(z) \le z^\sigma$ and $\mu_\delta(z) \to z^\sigma$ as $\delta \downarrow 0$.  Dominated convergence sends the endpoint difference to $\sum_i \int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2 - s^2)^\sigma\, ds$, and $E_\delta \to 0$, so

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2 - s^2)^\sigma\, ds \le \sup_{C \in \mathcal L} \mathcal E(C).$$

This finishes the action inequality.

Spectral application.  Suppose first that $W$ is real symmetric.  Let $n \ge 1$ and let $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_n$ be orthonormal real eigenfunctions of $H_W$ corresponding to negative eigenvalues $-\lambda_1, \dots, -\lambda_n$ with $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n > 0$, repeated according to multiplicity.  Complex conjugation preserves each eigenspace because $W$ is real, so real eigenfunctions may be chosen.  Approximate each $\phi_i$ by a smooth compactly supported real function in $H^1$; the Gram matrix converges to $I_n$ and the form matrix converges to $\operatorname{diag}(-\lambda_i)$.  Multiplying the approximating family on the left by the inverse square root of its Gram matrix restores $\int_\Omega U U^{\mathrm T} = I_n$ without changing the common compact support, and the form matrix stays negative definite.  Diagonalizing it by a real orthogonal change of rows puts the form matrix in the form $-\delta_{ij} k_i^2$.

For such a family, with $U$ the matrix whose rows are $u_i$, the orthonormality holds, the polarised form satisfies $h_W[u_i, u_j] = -k_i^2 \delta_{ij}$, and $k_i \to \sqrt{\lambda_i}$ as the approximation improves.  Set $K = \operatorname{diag}(k_1, \dots, k_n)$.

For any lower triangular $C \in \mathcal L$, the $i$th row of $A = CU$ is $a_i = \sum_{j \le i} C_{ij} u_j$.  Then

$$h_W[a_i] + k_i^2 \|a_i\|_{L^2}^2 = \sum_{j \le i} C_{ij}^2(k_i^2 - k_j^2) \le 0,$$

since $k_1 \ge \dots \ge k_n$.  Consequently

$$\|a_i'\|_2^2 + k_i^2 \|a_i\|_2^2 \le \int_\Omega \langle a_i, W a_i\rangle\, dx.$$

Summing over $i$ and using $\operatorname{tr}(W A^{\mathrm T} A) = \sum_i \langle a_i, W a_i\rangle$,

$$\mathcal E(C) \le \int_\Omega\left(\operatorname{tr}(W A^{\mathrm T} A) - \operatorname{tr}((A^{\mathrm T} A)^q)\right) dx,$$

because $A A^{\mathrm T}$ and $A^{\mathrm T} A$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues.

The pointwise factor.  For $y, z \ge 0$ the maximum of $yz - z^q$ over $z$ at fixed $y$ gives

$$yz - z^q \le D_\sigma y^p, \qquad D_\sigma = \frac{\sigma^\sigma}{(1 + \sigma)^{1 + \sigma}}.$$

Diagonalize $Z = A^{\mathrm T} A$ in an orthonormal basis $(v_j)$ with eigenvalues $z_j$ and set $y_j = \langle v_j, W v_j\rangle$.  Convexity of $t \mapsto t^p$ together with the spectral measure of $W$ in $v_j$ gives $y_j^p \le \langle v_j, W^p v_j\rangle$.  Hence

$$\operatorname{tr}(W Z) - \operatorname{tr}(Z^q) = \sum_j (y_j z_j - z_j^q) \le D_\sigma \sum_j y_j^p \le D_\sigma \operatorname{tr}(W^p),$$

pointwise in $x$.  Using $Z = A^{\mathrm T} A$ inside $\mathcal E(C)$,

$$\sup_{C \in \mathcal L} \mathcal E(C) \le D_\sigma \int_\Omega \operatorname{tr}(W^p)\, dx.$$

Combining the two inequalities.  The action theorem gives

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2 - s^2)^\sigma\, ds \le \sup_{C \in \mathcal L} \mathcal E(C),$$

and the Young bound gives $\sup_{C \in \mathcal L} \mathcal E(C) \le D_\sigma \int_\Omega \operatorname{tr}(W^p)\, dx$.  The substitution $s = k_i t$ gives

$$\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2 - s^2)^\sigma\, ds = k_i^{2\sigma+1}\int_{-1}^1 (1 - t^2)^\sigma\, dt = k_i^{2\gamma}\,\mathrm{B}(\tfrac12, 1 + \sigma),$$

with Euler's beta function $\mathrm B$.  Hence

$$\sum_{i=1}^n k_i^{2\gamma} \le \frac{D_\sigma}{\mathrm B(\tfrac12, 1 + \sigma)}\int_\Omega \operatorname{tr}(W^p)\, dx.$$

The coefficient equals the constant $C_\gamma$.  Indeed, using $\mathrm B(1/2, 1 + \sigma) = \sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(1+\sigma)/\Gamma(\sigma + 3/2)$ and $(1+\sigma)\Gamma(1+\sigma) = \Gamma(\sigma + 2)$,

$$\frac{D_\sigma}{\mathrm B(\tfrac12, 1+\sigma)} = \frac{\sigma^\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{1+\sigma}}\frac{\Gamma(\sigma+3/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\sigma+1)} = \left(\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}\right)^\sigma\frac{\Gamma(\sigma+3/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\sigma+2)} = C_\gamma.$$

Since $\sigma = \gamma - 1/2$, the exponent $2\gamma$ on $k_i$ gives the sharp constant in the theorem.  Letting the approximating family converge,

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i^\gamma \le C_\gamma \int_{\mathbb R} \operatorname{tr}(W^p)\, dx.$$

The full sum $\sum_j \lambda_j^\gamma$ is the supremum of these finite sums over all finite lists of negative eigenvalues, so the same bound holds for it.  In particular the trace is finite.

Complex potentials.  A complex Hermitian $W = E + iF$ has $E^{\mathrm T} = E$ and $F^{\mathrm T} = -F$, so

$$\tilde W = \begin{pmatrix} E & -F \\ F & E \end{pmatrix}$$

is real symmetric and positive semidefinite.  The constant unitary

$$\mathcal U = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} I & iI \\ I & -iI \end{pmatrix}$$

satisfies $\mathcal U \tilde W \mathcal U^* = W \oplus \bar W$, so $\operatorname{tr}(\tilde W^p) = 2\operatorname{tr}(W^p)$ and, because $\mathcal U$ commutes with $d/dx$, $H_{\tilde W} \simeq H_W \oplus H_{\bar W}$.  Complex conjugation intertwines the two summands and preserves eigenvalue multiplicities, so the sum of negative eigenvalue moments of $H_{\tilde W}$ is twice that of $H_W$.  Applying the real inequality in dimension $2m$ and dividing both sides by two gives the asserted bound for complex $W$.

Sharpness.  Put $r = 1/\sigma > 1$ and let

$$V(x) = (r+1)\operatorname{sech}^2(rx), \qquad \psi(x) = \operatorname{sech}^{1/r}(rx),$$

be the scalar potential from the opening.  The identity $\psi'(x)/\psi(x) = -\tanh(rx)$ gives $H_V\psi = -\psi$, and $\psi \in L^2(\mathbb R)$.  With $Q = \partial_x + \tanh(rx)$ and $Q^* = -\partial_x + \tanh(rx)$, direct multiplication gives

$$H_V = Q^* Q - 1, \qquad QQ^* - 1 = -\partial_x^2 + (r - 1)\operatorname{sech}^2(rx) \ge 0.$$

So $H_V \ge -1$, and the eigenspace at $-1$ is $\ker Q = \mathrm{span}(\psi)$.  If $\lambda \in (-1, 0)$ were an eigenvalue with eigenfunction $u$, then $Qu \ne 0$.  The bounded potential gives $u \in H^2$, so $Qu \in L^2$, and the intertwining identity $(QQ^* - 1)Qu = \lambda Qu$ holds in distributions; boundedness of the partner potential places $Qu \in H^2$, contradicting nonnegativity of $QQ^* - 1$.  Hence $-1$ is the only negative eigenvalue and it is simple.

Finally,

$$\int_{\mathbb R} V^p\, dx = \frac{(r+1)^p}{r}\mathrm{B}(\tfrac12, p) = \frac{\mathrm B(\tfrac12, 1+\sigma)}{D_\sigma} = C_\gamma^{-1},$$

so the scalar inequality is an equality for $V$.  Embedding $V$ in one diagonal channel and letting the other channels vanish gives equality in every matrix dimension, which proves that $C_\gamma$ is optimal.
