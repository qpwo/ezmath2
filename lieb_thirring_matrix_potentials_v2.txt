Lieb-Thirring inequalities bound eigenvalue moments of a Schrodinger operator by an integral of the potential.  Take $m ge 1$ and $1/2 < gamma < 3/2$, and put $p = gamma + 1/2$.  A matrix potential is a measurable function $W : mathbb RbbR to mathbb C^bbC^{mtimes m}$ with Hermitian positive semidefinite values and

$$int_{mathbb R}bbR tr(W(x)^p), dx < ∞.$$

The one-dimensional Schrodinger operator $H_W = -d^2/dx^2 - W$, defined as the self-adjoint operator of the form $h_W[psi] = int_{mathbb R}bbR |psi'|^2, dx - int_{mathbb R}bbR < psi, Wpsi>, dx$ on $H^1(mathbb R;bbR; mathbb C^m)$,bbC^m)$, then has only isolated negative eigenvalues, each of finite multiplicity, with possible accumulation only at zero.  Write $lambda_j > 0$ for the absolute values of the negative eigenvalues of $H_W$, repeated according to multiplicity.  The inequality under study is

$$sum_j lambda_j^gamma le C_gamma int_{mathbb R}bbR tr(W(x)^p), dx, qquad C_gamma = ((gamma - 1/2)/(gamma + 1/2))^{gamma - 1/2} (Gamma(gamma+1))/(sqrtpi,Gamma(gamma + 3/2)).$$

The constant is independent of $m$.  It is sharp.  With $r = (gamma - 1/2)^{-1} > 1$ and $w(x) = (r+1)sech^2(rx)$, the diagonal potential $W(x) = diag(w(x), 0, ..., 0)$ has exactly one negative eigenvalue, namely $-1$, and equality holds in the inequality.

The one-bound-state case, $lambda_1^gamma le C_gamma int tr(W^p)$, was computed by Keller for scalar potentials in 1961 and appeared again in Lieb and Thirring's 1975 proof that the kinetic energy of a fermion gas is dominated by its density, which settles the stability of matter.  The 1976 contribution of Lieb and Thirring to a volume in honor of Valentine Bargmann extended the scalar inequality to every $gamma ge 1/2$ with the semiclassical constant and conjectured that the one-bound-state constant remains sharp when all negative eigenvalues are summed.

In one dimension the endpoints were treated first.  At $gamma = 1/2$, Calogero and Cohn had already found the sharp one-state bound; Weidl proved finiteness of the moment, and Hundertmark, Lieb and Thomas obtained the constant $1/2$ for scalar potentials, which Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl extended to operator-valued potentials.  At $gamma = 3/2$, Laptev and Weidl proved the sharp constant for matrix potentials from the matrix Buslaev-Faddeev-Zakharov trace formulae, and Benguria and Loss found an alternative proof by Riccati factorization; Aizenman-Lieb integration covers every $gamma > 3/2$.  In the open interval, Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl proved a bound with twice the semiclassical constant, Dolbeault, Laptev and Loss sharpened the constant at $gamma = 1$ using orthonormal trial functions, and Read and Schulz obtained the sharp value $4/(3sqrt3pi)$ at $gamma = 1$ for operator-valued potentials.  The remaining question in the interval $1/2 < gamma < 3/2$ is whether summing all negative eigenvalues raises the constant above the one-bound-state value; the answer is negative in every case.

The proof has three parts.  An integrated trace comparison $tr(NV) ge tr(N^q)$ for resolvent differences of Hermitian pairs is the only matrix-specific step; the scalar theory has rank one at that point.  A smooth field $M_delta$ on $Sym_n$ supplies the endpoint difference $sum_i int_{-k_i}^{k_i} (k_i^2 - s^2)^sigma, ds$ for $K = diag(k_1, ..., k_n)$.  A penalized path from $K$ to $-K$ then yields the sharp constant as the penalty and the smoothing vanish.

Throughout, put $sigma = gamma - 1/2 in (0, 1)$, $p = 1 + sigma$, $q = 1 + 1/sigma$, and $beta = sigma - 1/2$, so that $p^{-1} + q^{-1} = 1$ and $beta + 1/2 = sigma$.  The Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a matrix is $|A|_{HS} = (tr(A^* A))^{1/2}$, and $|A|_{op}$ denotes the operator norm.  The space $Sym_n$ consists of the real symmetric $n times n$ matrices, with inner product $tr(BH)$.  Complex inner products are linear in the second variable.  The constant

$$c_sigma^{-1} = int_0^∞ t^beta (t^{-1/2} - (t+1)^{-1/2}),dt$$

is finite, because the integrand is $O(t^{beta-1/2})$ at zero and $O(t^{beta-3/2})$ at infinity.  The substitution $t mapsto yt$ gives

$$c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta (t^{-1/2} - (t+y)^{-1/2}),dt = y^sigma$$

for every $y ge 0$.

Two computational facts are used repeatedly.  The first establishes the form domain and isolates the finiteness of the negative spectrum that the definition of $sum_j lambda_j^gamma$ requires.

Lemma.  Let $m ge 1$, let $p > 1$, and let $W : mathbb RbbR to mathbb C^bbC^{mtimes m}$ be measurable, Hermitian, positive semidefinite, and satisfy $int_{mathbb R}bbRtr(W^p) < ∞$.  The form $h_W$ is closed and bounded below on $H^1(mathbb R;bbR; mathbb C^m)$.bbC^m)$.  Multiplication by $W^{1/2}$ is compact from $H^1(mathbb R;bbR; mathbb C^m)$bbC^m)$ to $L^2(mathbb R;bbR; mathbb C^m)$,bbC^m)$, and $H_W$ has only isolated negative eigenvalues of finite multiplicity.

Proof.  Put $w = |W|_{op}$.  Since $w^p le tr(W^p)$, $w in L^p(mathbb R)$.bbR)$.  The Sobolev estimate

$$|psi|_∞^2 le 2 |psi|_2 |psi'|_2$$

holds for vector-valued functions, by applying the scalar estimate to $x mapsto |psi(x)|$.  Holder's inequality and interpolation then give, for any $ε > 0$,

$$int_{mathbb R}bbR<psi, Wpsi>, dx le |w|_p |psi|_{2p/(p-1)}^2 le 2^{1/p} |w|_p |psi'|_2^{1/p} |psi|_2^{2 - 1/p} le ε |psi'|_2^2 + C_ε |psi|_2^2,$$

by Young's inequality.  A shifted form norm is thus equivalent to the $H^1$ norm, so $h_W$ is closed and bounded below.

Compactness: for $R, L > 0$ put $T_{R,L}psi = 1_{{|x| le R, w(x) le L}} W^{1/2}psi$.  Restriction to $[-R, R]$ and the compact embedding $H^1([-R, R]; mathbb C^m)bbC^m) subset L^2([-R, R]; mathbb C^m)$bbC^m)$ show that $T_{R,L}$ is compact, and

$$|(T - T_{R,L})psi|_2^2 le C |w, 1_{{|x| > R} cup {w > L}}|_p |psi|_{H^1}^2$$

tends to zero uniformly, so $T$ is compact.

Isolated spectrum: suppose the spectral subspace of $H_W$ on $(-∞, -a]$ were infinite dimensional for some $a > 0$.  An orthonormal sequence $(psi_j)$ in it lies in the form domain with uniformly bounded shifted form norm, hence in $H^1(mathbb R;bbR; mathbb C^m)$bbC^m)$ with uniformly bounded norm.  A weakly convergent subsequence in $H^1$ has $H^1$-weak limit zero, because the original sequence is $L^2$-orthonormal.  Compactness of $T$ would then send $|Tpsi_j|_2 to 0$.  But $-a ge h_W[psi_j] = |psi_j'|_2^2 - |Tpsi_j|_2^2 ge -|Tpsi_j|_2^2$, a contradiction.  So each such spectral subspace is finite dimensional.

The second ingredient isolates the matrix step.  Fix $n ge 1$ and two Hermitian matrices $B, V in mathbb C^bbC^{ntimes n}$.  Suppose

$$X_s = (sI - B)^2 + V ge b I qquad (s in mathbb R)$$bbR)$$

for some $b > 0$.  Set

$$Y_s = (sI - B)^2, qquad R(t) = pi^{-1}int_{mathbb R}bbR(X_s + tI)^{-1}, ds, qquad P(t) = t^{-1/2} I - R(t), qquad N = c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta P(t), dt.$$

Diagonalizing $B$ gives $pi^{-1}int (Y_s + tI)^{-1}, ds = t^{-1/2}I$.  From $X_s + tI ge bI$ and quadratic growth, the difference of resolvents is $O((s^2 + t)^{-2})$ uniformly, so $|P(t)|_{op} = O(t^{-1/2})$ at zero and $O(t^{-3/2})$ at infinity; the matrix $N$ is thus well defined and Hermitian.

Theorem (integrated trace comparison).  With this notation, if $N ge 0$ then

$$tr(NV) ge tr(N^q).$$

When $V = v > 0$ and $B = 0$, $R(t) = (t+v)^{-1/2}$ and $N = v^sigma$, so both sides equal $v^q$.  The proof rewrites the difference $tr(NV) - tr(N^q)$ as an integral of nonnegative terms.  Solutions of a constant-coefficient differential equation on the two half-lines will express $R(t)$ in a form that separates into commuting pieces and a positive remainder.

Half-line solutions.  Fix $t > 0$ and put $nabla = ∂_x - iB$.  Consider the differential equation

$$(-nabla^2 + V + tI)psi = 0, qquad psi in H^1(mathbb R;bbR; mathbb C^n).$$bbC^n).$$

Lemma.  For every $v in mathbb C^n$bbC^n$ the equation above has a unique $H^1$ solution on each half-line $x > 0$ and $x < 0$ with boundary value $v$ at zero.  There exist Hermitian matrices $S = S(t)$ and $T = T(t)$, independent of $v$, such that

$$nablapsi(0+) = -Sv, qquad nablapsi(0-) = Tv,$$

and

$$V + tI = S^2 + i[B,S] = T^2 - i[B,T], qquad (S + T)/(2) = R(t)^{-1}.$$

Every eigenvalue of $B + iS$ has strictly positive imaginary part.

Proof.  Define the sesquilinear form

$$a_t(phi, psi) = int(< nablaphi, nablapsi> + < phi, (V+tI)psi>) dx$$

on the trace-zero subspace of $H^1$ on each half-line.  The Fourier symbol of $a_t$ on the whole line is $X_s + tI$.  The lower bound $X_s + tI ge bI$ combined with $X_s = s^2 I - 2sB + B^2 + V$ produces $c_t > 0$ with $X_s + tI ge c_t(1 + s^2)I$; the same bound holds on each half-line by zero extension.  Coercivity follows, and Riesz on the trace-zero space produces a unique solution for any boundary value.  The equation forces $psi'' in L^2$, so $psi$ and $psi'$ have boundary traces and both tend to zero at the infinite end of each half-line.

Define $S$ and $T$ by $nablapsi(0+) = -Sv$ and $nablapsi(0-) = Tv$.  Integration by parts gives $a_t(psi_v, psi_w) = < v, Sw>$ on the right and $a_t(psi_v, psi_w) = < v, Tw>$ on the left.  Since $a_t$ is Hermitian, both matrices are Hermitian.  Translation invariance and uniqueness identify the derivative relations

$$nablapsi = -Spsi quad (x > 0), qquad nablapsi = Tpsi quad (x < 0).$$

Substituting the first into the equation and evaluating at zero for arbitrary $v$ gives $V + tI = S^2 + i[B, S]$; the second similarly gives $V + tI = T^2 - i[B, T]$.

Glue the two solutions with a common boundary value $v$ at zero.  The resulting function is continuous, its derivative has jump $-(S+T)v$, and it satisfies

$$(-nabla^2 + V + tI)psi = delta_0 (S+T)v$$

as a distribution.  Using the Fourier transform with forward kernel $e^{-isx}$ and evaluating the inverse transform at zero gives

$$v = (1)/(2pi)int_{mathbb R}bbR(X_s + tI)^{-1}, ds, (S+T)v = (1)/(2)R(t)(S+T)v,$$

so $(S+T)/2 = R(t)^{-1}$ since $R(t) > 0$.

For the last claim, $nablapsi = -Spsi$ gives $psi' = i(B+iS)psi$ on $(0, ∞)$.  If $(B+iS)w = zw$ with $w ne 0$, uniqueness for this first-order initial value problem gives $psi(x) = e^{izx}w$.  The function is in $L^2(0, ∞; mathbb C^n)$bbC^n)$ only when $Im z > 0$.

The resolvent decomposition.  Put

$$L = (S+T)/(2) = R(t)^{-1}, qquad Z = (S-T)/(2).$$

Both are Hermitian and $L > 0$.  Adding and subtracting the two forms of $V + tI$,

$$V + tI = L^2 + Z^2 + i[B,Z], qquad LZ + ZL + i[B,L] = 0.$$

In an eigenbasis of $L$ the second identity has diagonal entries $2L_{jj}Z_{jj} = 0$, so $tr Z = 0$.  Multiplying the second identity on both sides by $R = L^{-1}$ gives $i[R, B] = -(ZR + RZ)$; cyclicity of the trace yields $itr(R[B, Z]) = -2tr(RZ^2)$, and therefore

$$tr(R(V+tI)) = tr L - tr(RZ^2).$$

The quantity $tr(RZ^2) = tr(Z R Z)$ is nonnegative.

The scalar integrals $F$ and $G$.  Introduce

$$F(t) = (tr V)/(sqrt t) - pi^{-1}int_{mathbb R}bbRlog(det(X_s + tI))/(det(Y_s + tI)), ds.$$

The determinants are positive.  Factoring with $(Y_s + tI)^{1/2}$ shows the logarithm is $O(|s|^{-2})$ at infinity, so the integral converges absolutely.  Its derivative in $t$ is $tr(t^{-1/2}I - R(t))$ and has an integrable bound locally uniform in $t$; hence $F$ is continuously differentiable on $(0, ∞)$.

The first Riccati identity factors

$$X_s + tI = (sI - B + iS)(sI - B - iS).$$

Let $u_j + i v_j$, $1 le j le n$, be the eigenvalues of $B + iS$.  From the last part of the half-line lemma, $v_j > 0$.  Taking determinants gives

$$det(X_s + tI) = prod_{j=1}^n ((s - u_j)^2 + v_j^2).$$

The denominator has the same form, with centres the eigenvalues of $B$ and widths all equal to $sqrt t$.  Symmetric-cutoff integrations move the centres to zero without changing the infinite integral; the scalar identity

$$int_{mathbb R}bbRlog(s^2 + v^2)/(s^2 + w^2), ds = 2pi(v - w) qquad (v, w > 0)$$

then gives

$$F(t) = (tr V)/(sqrt t) - 2tr(L - sqrt t, I).$$

Set

$$G(t) = tr(L - 2sqrt t, I + tR(t)).$$

Each scalar eigenvalue $r > 0$ of $R(t)$ contributes $r^{-1} - 2sqrt t + tr = (1 - sqrt t, r)^2 / r ge 0$, so $G(t) ge 0$.  Taking traces in $V + tI = L^2 + Z^2 + i[B, Z]$ and substituting the formula for $F$ gives

$$F(t) = (1)/(sqrt t)tr((L - sqrt t, I)^2 + Z^2) ge 0.$$

Comparing the two expressions for $F$ with $tr(P(t)V)$,

$$tr(P(t)V) = F(t) + G(t) + tr(R(t)Z^2).$$

The bounds already proved place both $F$ and $G$ at $O(t^{-1/2})$ near zero and $O(t^{-3/2})$ near infinity; both are integrable against $t^beta, dt$, and $t^{beta+1} F(t) to 0$ at both endpoints.  Differentiating the definition of $F$ and simplifying gives $tF'(t) = -F(t)/2 - G(t)$, and integrating the derivative of $t^{beta+1}F(t)$ over a compact subinterval then passing to the endpoints gives $int t^beta G(t), dt = sigma int t^beta F(t), dt$.  Consequently

$$tr(NV) = q c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta G(t), dt + c_sigma int_0^∞ t^betatr(R(t)Z^2), dt.$$

Comparison with an external square.  Fix a positive semidefinite matrix $D$ and put

$$F_0(t, D) = (tr D)/(sqrt t) - 2tr((tI + D)^{1/2} - sqrt t, I).$$

For a scalar eigenvalue $d$ of $D$ the contribution to $F_0$ is $int_0^d (t^{-1/2} - (t+y)^{-1/2}), dy ge 0$, so by Tonelli and the identity $c_sigmaint t^beta (t^{-1/2} - (t+y)^{-1/2}), dt = y^sigma$,

$$c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta F_0(t, D), dt = p^{-1} tr(D^p).$$

The Hilbert-Schmidt square

$$mathcal D_D(t)calD_D(t) = |R^{-1/2} - R^{1/2}(tI + D)^{1/2}|_{HS}^2 = tr(R^{-1} + R(tI+D) - 2(tI+D)^{1/2})$$

is nonnegative and equals $G(t) - tr(P(t)D) + F_0(t, D)$.  Each of the three terms is integrable against $t^beta, dt$, so

$$c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta G(t), dt = tr(ND) - p^{-1}tr(D^p) + c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta mathcal D_D(t)calD_D(t), dt.$$

Assume $N ge 0$ and choose $D = N^{1/sigma}$.  Since $p/sigma = q$, $tr(ND) - p^{-1}tr(D^p) = q^{-1}tr(N^q)$.  Combining with the weighted identity for $F$ and $G$ gives the deficit formula

$$tr(NV) - tr(N^q) = c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta(tr(R(t)Z(t)^2) + q,mathcal D_calD_{N^{1/sigma}}(t)), dt.$$

Both integrands are nonnegative, so $tr(NV) ge tr(N^q)$.  If $B$ and $V$ commute both integrals vanish, recovering the scalar equality; the two nonnegative terms in the deficit carry the discrepancy in the noncommuting case.

The trace comparison just proved is the matrix-specific step.  The remaining ingredients follow the finite-interval method used for the scalar case, with every rank-one comparison replaced by the comparison at positive values of a regularized matrix field.

The field.  Fix a positive diagonal matrix $K = diag(k_1, ..., k_n)$ and put $kappa = max_i k_i$.  For each $delta > 0$ the argument requires a field $M_delta : Sym_n to Sym_n$ with three properties.  First, $M_delta$ is locally Lipschitz and has a $C^1$ primitive $J_delta$ with $dJ_delta(B)[H] = -tr(M_delta(B)H)$ for all $B, H in Sym_n$.  Second, $M_delta$ is equivariant under $B mapsto SBS$ for any diagonal sign matrix $S$, and its action on the standard basis vector $e_i$ is diagonal whenever $B$ is diagonal.  Third, whenever $M_delta(B) ge 0$,

$$tr(M_delta(B)(K^2 - B^2)) ge tr(M_delta(B)^q) - E_delta, qquad E_delta = n(delta kappa^{2sigma} + delta^sigma(kappa^2 + delta)),$$

and $E_delta to 0$ as $delta ↓ 0$ for fixed $K$.

Construction.  For $y ge 0$ set

$$f_delta(y) = (c_sigma)/(pi)int_0^∞ t^beta((1)/(t + delta) - (1)/(t + delta + y)) dt.$$

For $y < 0$ put $f_delta(y) = f_delta'(0)y$, the tangent-line extension at zero.  Differentiation under the integral and the substitution $t = (y + delta)u$ give

$$f_delta'(y) = a_sigma (y + delta)^{beta - 1}, qquad a_sigma = (c_sigma)/(pi)int_0^∞ (u^beta)/((1 + u)^2), du > 0 qquad (y ge 0).$$

Both integrals converge because $-1/2 < beta < 1/2$.  Hence $f_delta$ is $C^1$, strictly increasing, and concave on $mathbb R$,bbR$, and the tangent extension brings every real symmetric matrix, including matrices with negative eigenvalues, into the domain of $f_delta$.

For $s in mathbb R$bbR$ and $B in Sym_n$ put

$$Q_s(B) = s^2 I - 2s B + K^2, qquad M_delta(B) = lim_{r to ∞}int_{-r}^r (f_delta(Q_s(B)) - f_delta(s^2) I), ds.$$

The symmetric cutoff cancels the leading odd-in-$s$ term.

Lemma (fields and primitives).  The limit defining $M_delta(B)$ exists locally uniformly on $Sym_n$, and $M_delta$ is locally Lipschitz.  There is a $C^1$ function $J_delta : Sym_n to mathbb R$,bbR$, normalized by $J_delta(0) = 0$, with

$$dJ_delta(B)[H] = -tr(M_delta(B)H) qquad (B, H in Sym_n).$$

Proof.  Two facts about Hilbert-Schmidt norms are used.  If $h$ is scalar Lipschitz on an interval containing the spectra of Hermitian matrices $X$, $Y$, then

$$|h(X) - h(Y)|_{HS} le Lip(h), |X - Y|_{HS}.$$

Indeed, in eigenbases $(e_i)$ of $X$ and $(v_j)$ of $Y$ the $(i,j)$ entries of the two matrix differences are $(h(lambda_i) - h(nu_j))< e_i, v_j>$ and $(lambda_i - nu_j)< e_i, v_j>$; the Lipschitz bound, followed by summation of squared moduli, proves the inequality.

Restrict $B$ to a bounded set.  For large $|s|$ the spectrum of $E_s(B) = -2s B + K^2$ lies in $[-a|s|, a|s|]$ with $a$ independent of $B$.  On this interval put

$$h_s(y) = f_delta(s^2 + y) - f_delta(s^2) - f_delta'(s^2)y.$$

By the derivative formula, $sup_{|y| le a|s|} |h_s'(y)| = O(|s|^{2beta - 3})$.  Since $h_s(0) = 0$ and $|E_s(B)|_{HS} = O(|s|)$, the Hilbert-Schmidt Lipschitz calculus gives

$$f_delta(Q_s(B)) - f_delta(s^2)I = -2s f_delta'(s^2)B + T_s(B),$$

with $|T_s(B)|_{HS} = O(|s|^{2beta - 2})$ and $|T_s(B_1) - T_s(B_2)|_{HS} le C|s|^{2beta - 2}|B_1 - B_2|_{HS}$ uniformly on the chosen bounded set.  The linear term integrates to zero on $[-r, r]$, and $2beta - 2 = 2sigma - 3 < -1$ makes both remainder bounds integrable at infinity.  Local Lipschitz continuity on bounded $s$-intervals is immediate.  Convergence and regularity follow.

To construct the primitive, choose $g_delta$ with $g_delta' = f_delta$.  The trace differentiation identity

$$d(tr g_delta(X))[H] = tr(f_delta(X) H)$$

follows first for polynomials by cyclicity of the trace, then for $g_delta$ by $C^1$ polynomial approximation on compact intervals containing the relevant spectra.  For $s ne 0$ the function

$$B mapsto (tr(g_delta(Q_s(B)) - g_delta(K^2)))/(2s) + f_delta(s^2)tr B$$

has differential $-tr((f_delta(Q_s(B)) - f_delta(s^2)I)H)$; at $s = 0$ the field is constant and the primitive is linear.  Hence the path integral of the negative field around every piecewise smooth closed curve vanishes at every finite cutoff, and local uniform convergence passes the identity to $M_delta$.  Path integration on the vector space $Sym_n$ produces $J_delta$ with the required relation, and continuity of $M_delta$ gives $J_delta in C^1$.

Scalar shifts and matrix directions.  The scalar companion of the field is

$$mu_delta(z) = int_{mathbb R}bbR(f_delta(s^2 + z) - f_delta(s^2)), ds, qquad z in mathbb R.$$bbR.$$

The integral converges absolutely, and on bounded sets of $z$ its integrand and its Lipschitz constant in $z$ are $O(|s|^{2beta - 2})$ at infinity; so $mu_delta$ is locally Lipschitz.  Strict increase of $f_delta$ makes $mu_delta$ strictly increasing, and $mu_delta(0) = 0$.  For $z ge 0$, Tonelli's theorem and $pi^{-1}int_{mathbb R}bbR(s^2 + a)^{-1}, ds = a^{-1/2}$ give

$$mu_delta(z) = c_sigma int_0^∞ t^beta((t + delta)^{-1/2} - (t + delta + z)^{-1/2}) dt = (z + delta)^sigma - delta^sigma.$$

The last equality is the difference of $y^sigma$ at $y = delta + z$ and $y = delta$.

Field properties.  A translation in the integration variable extends the previous formula for $mu_delta$ to all $z$.  For fixed $z$ and $a$, put $F_z(s) = f_delta(s^2 + z)$.  This function is even, and $F_z'(s) = O(|s|^{2beta - 1}) to 0$.  For $a ge 0$, pairing the intervals $[r, r+a]$ and $[r-a, r]$ gives

$$|int_{-r}^r (F_z(s-a) - F_z(s)), ds| le |a|^2 sup_{r - |a| le u le r + |a|} |F_z'(u)| to 0 qquad (r to ∞),$$

and therefore

$$lim_{r to ∞}int_{-r}^r (f_delta((s-a)^2 + z) - f_delta(s^2)) ds = mu_delta(z).$$

Lemma (field properties).  For every $B in Sym_n$ and every unit vector $e in mathbb R^n$,bbR^n$,

$$e^{mathrm T}rmT M_delta(B) e le mu_delta(e^{mathrm T}rmT K^2 e - (e^{mathrm T}rmT B e)^2).$$

If $B e_i = y e_i$ for a standard basis vector $e_i$ and $y in mathbb R$,bbR$, then

$$M_delta(B) e_i = mu_delta(k_i^2 - y^2) e_i.$$

If $S$ is a diagonal matrix with entries in ${1, -1}$, then $M_delta(S B S) = S M_delta(B) S$.  Finally,

$$J_delta(-K) - J_delta(K) = sum_{i=1}^n int_{-k_i}^{k_i} mu_delta(k_i^2 - s^2), ds.$$

Proof.  Scalar concavity, applied in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $Q_s(B)$, gives

$$e^{mathrm T}rmT f_delta(Q_s(B)) e le f_delta(e^{mathrm T}rmT Q_s(B) e).$$

With $a = e^{mathrm T}rmT B e$ and $z = e^{mathrm T}rmT K^2 e - a^2$ the right side reads $f_delta((s - a)^2 + z)$.  Subtracting $f_delta(s^2)$, integrating over $[-r, r]$, and applying the shift identity gives the directional bound.

If $B e_i = y e_i$, then $e_i$ is an eigenvector of $Q_s(B)$ with eigenvalue $(s - y)^2 + k_i^2 - y^2$, and functional calculus together with the shift identity gives the diagonal action.

For equivariance, $S K^2 S = K^2$ gives $Q_s(SBS) = S Q_s(B) S$, and conjugating inside the definition of $M_delta$ proves it.

On the diagonal subspace, the coordinate formula and the primitive identity give

$$dJ_delta(diag(b_1, ..., b_n)) = -sum_{i=1}^n mu_delta(k_i^2 - b_i^2), db_i,$$

and integrating from $(k_1, ..., k_n)$ to $(-k_1, ..., -k_n)$ within this subspace proves the endpoint formula.

Comparison at positive field values.  Matrix Riccati factorization also underlies the commutation approach of Benguria and Loss to matrix Schrodinger inequalities.  The trace engine provides the comparison when the field value is positive.

Proposition.  For every $B in Sym_n$ with $M_delta(B) ge 0$,

$$tr(M_delta(B)(K^2 - B^2)) ge tr(M_delta(B)^q) - E_delta.$$

Proof.  Write $M = M_delta(B)$.  By the Jensen bound and strict increase of $mu_delta$, positivity of $M$ implies $e^{mathrm T}rmT K^2 e ge (e^{mathrm T}rmT B e)^2$ for every unit vector $e$; consequently

$$e^{mathrm T}rmT Q_s(B) e = (s - e^{mathrm T}rmT B e)^2 + e^{mathrm T}rmT K^2 e - (e^{mathrm T}rmT B e)^2 ge 0.$$
