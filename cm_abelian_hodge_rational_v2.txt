


Exercise: The headline. What does this paper prove?
For a smooth projective complex variety X, the rational Hodge conjecture says every rational cohomology class of type (p,p) is a rational combination of classes of complex subvarieties of codimension p. It is open in general. This paper proves it completely for one large class of X: abelian varieties with complex multiplication, and all their finite products and powers, in every codimension. Concretely, if A is a complex abelian variety whose endomorphism algebra is large enough (contains a commutative semisimple Q-algebra of dimension 2·dim A; this is what "CM" means and is made precise below), then the cycle class map from codimension-p cycles, tensored with Q, onto the (p,p) classes is surjective. No hypothesis on dimension or codimension is imposed.
Original paper: "The rational Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties", Section 1, Theorem 1.1 (nicknamed (Main Theorem)).


Exercise: The Hodge conjecture. What exactly does it ask?
Fix a smooth projective complex variety X of complex dimension n, and an integer p with 0 ≤ p ≤ n. Inside the complex cohomology H^{2p}(X, C) there is a rational subspace H^{2p}(X, Q), and inside that a subspace of classes of Hodge type (p,p) (defined below). Meanwhile each complex subvariety Z ⊂ X of codimension p has a fundamental cohomology class cl(Z) ∈ H^{2p}(X, Q), and these classes are automatically of type (p,p). The Hodge conjecture says: every rational (p,p) class is a finite rational linear combination of such cl(Z). Equivalently, the map cl : CH^p(X) ⊗ Q -> H^{2p}(X, Q) ∩ H^{p,p}(X) is surjective, where CH^p(X) is the group of codimension-p cycles modulo rational equivalence. The case p = 1 is the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem and is a theorem; the cases p ≥ 2 are the hard part and are open for general X.
Hodge 1952, "The topological invariants of algebraic varieties": https://www.mathunion.org/fileadmin/ICM/Proceedings/ICM1950.1/ICM1950.1.ocr.pdf


Exercise: Cohomology. What is H^k(X), in words?
For a nice space X, H^k(X) is an abelian group that measures the k-dimensional holes of X. H^0 counts connected components; H^1 counts independent loops; H^2 counts enclosed surfaces (a hollow sphere has a cavity, so a nonzero H^2); and so on. When X is a compact manifold, each H^k(X, Q) = H^k(X) ⊗ Q is a finite-dimensional Q-vector space. There is a product, the cup product ∪ : H^a × H^b -> H^{a+b}, making H^*(X) = ⊕_k H^k(X) into a graded ring; the class of a point in the top degree H^{2n}(X) lets you integrate: for compact X of real dimension 2n, pairing a class in H^{2n}(X) with the fundamental class [X] gives a number, written ∫_X. For smooth complex projective X one computes the same groups from differential forms (de Rham's theorem), which is how complex analysis enters.
Milne, "Lectures on Étale Cohomology" / standard de Rham references; the paper uses Betti (= singular) cohomology with Q-coefficients throughout.


Exercise: Hodge decomposition and (p,p) classes. What is "type (p,p)"?
When X is a compact Kähler manifold of complex dimension n (all smooth complex projective varieties are), the complex cohomology splits:
  H^k(X, C) = ⊕_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(X),
where H^{p,q}(X) consists of classes representable by differential forms with p copies of dz_i and q copies of dz̄_j (holomorphic degree p, antiholomorphic degree q). The two numbers p and q are the Hodge type. Facts: complex conjugation swaps H^{p,q} and H^{q,p}; the integer k = p+q is the weight, fixed by k. A class of type (p,p) is one sitting in H^{p,p}, so its weight is 2p, and it is fixed by conjugation. These (p,p) classes are exactly the "Hodge classes": the ones the Hodge conjecture wants cycles for. The diagonal condition p = q is what makes a class geometric-looking; the (p,p) subspace of H^{2p}(X, Q) is written Hdg^{2p}(X).
Deligne 1982, "Hodge cycles on abelian varieties", Sections 1-2: https://www.jmilne.org/math/Documents/Deligne82.pdf


Exercise: Algebraic cycles and the cycle class map. Why are cl(Z) automatically (p,p)?
A codimension-p cycle on X is a finite formal Z-combination of closed complex subvarieties of codimension p. Poincaré duality sends each such subvariety Z (smooth for intuition) to a cohomology class cl(Z) ∈ H^{2p}(X, Z). The key point: a complex subvariety has a compatible complex structure, so on it the holomorphic and antiholomorphic directions pair up, and its fundamental class is killed by everything that is not (p,p). Thus cl(Z) always lies in H^{2p}(X, Q) ∩ H^{p,p}(X). So cycle classes are automatically Hodge classes; the Hodge conjecture asks for the converse. A class is called algebraic if it is a Q-linear combination of cycle classes; the set of these is the image of cl, written Z^{2p}(X) for the part of degree 2p (later also denoted the rational cycle span). Two cycles give the same class after "clearing denominators", which is why Q-coefficients are the natural home.
Original paper, Section 2, "Scalar coefficients and degree-one maps", defines Hdg^{2p}(X) and Z^{2p}(X).


Exercise: Rational coefficients. Why ⊗ Q, and why is "scalar descent" needed?
Cycles live over Z and cohomology classes over Q, but the constructions in the proof naturally produce coefficients in M = the algebraic closure of Q inside C (or in C itself). To get a statement over Q one needs to descend: if a class t with algebraic coefficients has every Galois conjugate σt lying in the same rational subspace P, then t lies in (P ∩ Q-vector-space) ⊗ M. This is the content of "Lemma (Scalar Descent)". The mechanism: for a Galois extension K of Q containing the coefficients, the trace Tr_{K/Q}(a·t) is rational for each a ∈ K, and a trace-dual basis of K recovers t from finitely many such rational vectors. For maps, "having Hodge type (p,q)" is a complex-linear condition, so the same descent applies inside a Hom space of Hodge structures.
Original paper, Section 2, Lemma 2.2 (Scalar Descent).


Exercise: Abelian variety. What is it, and what is special about its cohomology?
A complex abelian variety of dimension g is a complex torus A = C^g / Λ (Λ ≅ Z^{2g} a lattice) that can be embedded in projective space; equivalently, it carries a positive-definite Hermitian form whose imaginary part takes integer values on Λ (a polarization, or Riemann form). Because it is a torus, A is an algebraic group with commutative group law. Everything about its cohomology factors through degree one: H^1(A, Z) = Hom(Λ, Z) is the dual lattice, of rank 2g (so H^1(A, Q) is a 2g-dimensional Q-vector space), and the full cohomology is the exterior algebra
  H^*(A, Q) = Λ^* H^1(A, Q),
with the cup product being the exterior (wedge) product. So the entire Hodge theory of A is governed by one weight-one Hodge structure H^1(A, Q), and H^p is the p-th exterior power. Hodge types on A are read off from a basis of H^1 split into holomorphic (1,0) and antiholomorphic (0,1) parts.
Milne, "Abelian Varieties" (1986, revised 2022), Sections 1-2: https://www.jmilne.org/math/xnotes/AVs.html


Exercise: Complex multiplication (CM). What is a CM abelian variety?
An abelian variety A is of CM type, or simply CM, if End(A) ⊗ Q (its endomorphism algebra) contains a commutative semisimple Q-algebra E of Q-dimension 2·dim A = 2g. Because H^1(A, Q) has dimension 2g and E acts faithfully on it, this forces E to be exactly the largest possible commutative algebra, namely a product of CM fields, each acting on H^1 as a "half" of the field. The basic example is an elliptic curve (g=1) with an order in an imaginary quadratic field such as Q(i) or Q(√-3): then E = Q(i) or Q(√-3) has Q-dimension 2 = 2g, so it is CM. Finite products and powers of CM varieties are again CM. The point of CM is that, over a Galois CM field E, H^1 splits over the algebraic closure into one-dimensional "embedding lines", one for each embedding of E into C, and the Hodge type is just a choice of which lines are (1,0). That makes the Hodge classes combinatorially visible, which is what makes the conjecture tractable here.
Original paper, Section 2, Lemma 2.3 (Toric CM characterization), originally Pohlmann 1968.


Exercise: The three consequences. Besides the headline, what else follows, and in what fields?
Three more long-open statements fall out, because the Hodge conjecture for CM varieties feeds into known "if Hodge for CM, then ..." theorems of Milne. (1) The generalized Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties: any rational Hodge substructure W of H^k(A) of "level" r (meaning W(r) is effective) is supported on a closed algebraic subset of codimension at least r. (2) The Tate conjecture for all abelian varieties over finite fields, not just CM ones: Galois-invariant l-adic classes are algebraic, and numerical equals l-adic homological equivalence. (3) The Hodge standard conjecture for abelian varieties in every characteristic: the intersection form on primitive algebraic classes is positive definite. Each is stated and proved as a separate corollary in Section 8 of the paper. The leaps (2) and (3) go through Milne's comparison theorems, which need exactly the CM Hodge input supplied here.
Original paper, Section 8 (corollaries 8.2, 8.5, 8.6), Milne 1999 and Milne 2002.


Exercise: Lefschetz (1,1). Why is codimension one free?
The Lefschetz (1,1) theorem says: for a smooth projective complex variety X, the cycle class map cl : CH^1(X) ⊗ Q -> Hdg^2(X) = H^2(X,Q) ∩ H^{1,1}(X) is an isomorphism. In words, every rational (1,1) class is the class of a rational combination of divisors (codimension-one subvarieties). Divisors correspond to line bundles, and the (1,1) condition is exactly the "curvature is of type (1,1)" condition that makes a line bundle algebraic (Kodaira). So p = 1 is a theorem, and all the difficulty sits at p ≥ 2. In the CM proof, codimension one and zero are the base cases; the induction/reduction handles p ≥ 2.
Original paper, Section 2, after Lemma 2.5; standard.

Exercise: Weil classes. Why can't every Hodge class be a product of divisors?
If every Hodge class were a polynomial in divisor classes, the Hodge conjecture would be easy. It is not: there are Hodge classes not generated by divisors. The cleanest examples are Weil classes. Take an abelian variety A of dimension 2n with an action of an imaginary quadratic field K, such that the two embeddings of K occur with equal multiplicity in the (1,0) part of H^1(A). Then the "determinant" piece Λ^{2n}_K of the degree-2n cohomology supplies a 2-dimensional rational space of (n,n) classes, the Weil classes. Their type is forced by the multiplicity condition, but their algebraicity needs a geometric construction, and they can fail to lie in the divisor algebra: this happens already on abelian fourfolds (Moonen-Zarhin). So Weil classes are genuine test cases for the Hodge conjecture, and the CM proof must handle them. Recent work of Markman proves algebraicity of certain Weil classes on sixfolds and deduces Hodge for all fourfolds.
Original paper, Section 1, "Hodge classes on CM abelian varieties"; Deligne 1982, Prop. 4.4; Moonen-Zarhin 1999, Thm 0.1; Markman 2025.


Exercise: Galois CM field, embedding lines, and the Hodge structure U(v). What are the basic building blocks?
Fix a Galois CM field E: a totally imaginary quadratic extension E = F(√-d) of a totally real field F, with E/Q Galois. Let c ∈ Gal(E/Q) be complex conjugation (central, since E/Q is Galois). Write G_E = Gal(E/Q) and s = [F:Q], so [E:Q] = 2s. Every CM abelian variety we care about can be arranged so its H^1 lives over one such E ("one E contains any finite collection of CM fields and their conjugates"). Over the algebraic closure, the Q-vector space E splits into one-dimensional "embedding lines": for each embedding λ : E -> C, there is a line E_λ = {u : a·u = λ(a)·u for all a ∈ E}. An "odd sign function" is a map v : G_E -> {1,-1} with v(cλ) = -v(λ). Given v, define a weight-one Hodge structure U(v) on the Q-vector space E by declaring the line U(v)_λ to have Hodge type (1,0) if v(λ) = 1 and (0,1) if v(λ) = -1. The oddness condition is exactly what makes this compatible with the real structure; without it, conjugation would not swap types correctly.
Original paper, Section 2, "Rank-one CM structures".


Exercise: The variety B(v). Why is U(v) the cohomology of an abelian variety?
The Hodge structure U(v) is polarizable: one can choose an element ξ ∈ E with c(ξ) = -ξ and signs tuned to v (weak approximation supplies it), giving an alternating form ψ_v(x,y) = Tr_{E/Q}(ξ·x·c(y)) that is positive on the (1,0) part. A polarizable weight-one Hodge structure is, by the Riemann criterion, exactly the H^1 of an abelian variety. So there is a CM abelian variety B(v) of dimension s = [F:Q] with
  U(v) = H^1(B(v), Q).
This works for every odd sign function, including imprimitive "CM types"; no principal polarization is required. The upshot: odd sign functions are the same as CM abelian varieties built from the same field E, and tensor products of their H^1's describe cohomology of products of such varieties.
Original paper, Section 2, Equation (2.4) and the Riemann-criterion construction; standard CM theory.

Exercise: The four-factor relation. What single algebraic statement drives everything?
Two odd sign functions can be added pointwise (values in {1,-1} ⊂ Z). The crucial elementary relation is
  v1 + v2 = v3 + v4  (as integer-valued functions on G_E).
When it holds, look at the four-factor tensor line
  L = U(v1)_1 ⊗ U(v2)_1 ⊗ U(v3)_c ⊗ U(v4)_c
      ⊂ H^4( B(v1) × B(v2) × B(v3) × B(v4), Q-closure ).
Here the subscript "1" means take the line labeled by the identity embedding, and "c" means the line labeled by complex conjugation. The relation forces this line to have type (2,2) in every Galois conjugate (the four signs sum to v1+v2-v3-v4 = 0). That makes it a candidate Hodge class, but a candidate is not a cycle. "Theorem (Four-Factor Switch)" asserts: whenever v1+v2 = v3+v4, the line L is algebraic (lies in the Q-closure-scalar extension of the rational cycle span). This is the sole hard statement; everything else is bookkeeping around it.
Original paper, Section 2, Theorem 2.9 (Four-Factor Switch).

Exercise: From the switch to the Main Theorem. How does one tensor line control all Hodge classes?
Take a Hodge class h on a CM abelian variety A. Over the Galois CM field E, H^1(A) splits into embedding lines, so H^{2p}(A) splits into "monomial lines" indexed by ordered tuples of embeddings (wedge products of embedding lines). Each monomial line sits in a single Hodge type, so a rational Hodge class spreads evenly across the Galois orbit of each monomial line it touches. Fix a supported monomial; its 2p factors have odd complete-type functions z1,...,z_{2p}, and the fact that the type is (p,p) reads as sum_i z_i = 0 (balanced). Set x_i = z_i for i = 1..p and y_i = -z_{p+i} for i = 1..p; then sum x_i = sum y_i. By a "line transfer" lemma, the original monomial reduces to the algebraicity of the balanced product
  L_{x,y} = (⊗_{i=1}^p U(x_i)_1) ⊗ (⊗_{i=1}^p U(y_i)_c).
Now one connects the list x to the list y by a sequence of "two-row switches": at each step, two entries a,b are replaced by a',b' with a+b = a'+b', exactly the Four-Factor Switch hypothesis. The switch supplies the needed algebraic four-factor tensor; unchanged slots are paired as U(v)_1 ⊗ U(v)_c, an automatically algebraic (1,1) two-factor piece. Composing consecutive tensors is done with the "transition composition" formula (cup, multiply by ample divisor powers, push forward). Iterating proves L_{x,y} algebraic, hence every monomial algebraic, hence h algebraic.
Original paper, Section 2, Proposition 2.10 (switch reduction) and its proof.


Exercise: Degree-one Hodge maps. How does a cohomology class "come from" an abelian variety?
Let A, B be abelian varieties. The first fact is Riemann's: every rational Hodge map H^1(B,Q) -> H^1(A,Q) is, up to a positive rational multiple, the pullback f^* of an algebraic homomorphism f : A -> B (equivalently, on homology, a map of lattices respecting the complex structure). The second fact: if S is any smooth projective variety and h : H^1(B,Q) -> H^1(S,Q) is a rational Hodge map, then there are a positive integer m and an actual morphism f : S -> B with f^* = m·h on H^1(B,Q). One proves this by passing to the Albanese variety of S and clearing denominators. So in degree one, "Hodge map" = "morphism, up to rational multiple"; the class h(α) is really (1/m)·f^*(α). This is "Lemma (Weight-One Maps)".
Original paper, Section 2, Lemma 2.4 (Weight-One Maps), from Milne-Shimura.

Exercise: The surface criterion. What cheaper statement would imply the Four-Factor Switch?
Suppose you can find a single smooth connected projective complex surface S, four rational Hodge maps h_i : U(v_i) -> H^1(S,Q) (i = 1,2,3,4), and nonzero vectors α_i in the required lines U(v_i)_{λ_i} with λ = (1,1,c,c), such that the top-degree period is nonzero:
  ∫_S h1(α1) ∪ h2(α2) ∪ h3(α3) ∪ h4(α4) ≠ 0.
Then "Proposition (Surface Criterion)" says the Four-Factor Switch line L is algebraic. So the hard tensor statement is replaced by a search for four one-forms on one surface whose cup product integrates to something nonzero. The geometry of the proof from here on is entirely about producing these four one-forms.
Original paper, Section 3, Proposition 3.1 (Surface Criterion).

Exercise: Why does a nonzero surface period give the algebraic tensor line? (proof sketch)
By Weight-One Maps, replace each h_i by (1/m_i) f_i^* for a morphism f_i : S -> B(v_i), and put A = prod_i B(v_i), n = dim A = 4s, f = (f_1,...,f_4) : S -> A. Push forward the fundamental class: z = f_*[S] is a codimension-(n-2) cycle on A, Z = cl(z). The projection formula gives ∫_A Z ∪ t_α = k, a nonzero algebraic number, where t_α = α1⊗α2⊗α3⊗α4. So Z is a nonzero functional on the switch line, but a functional on a line is not a vector on it. To convert, for each i take a second copy B(v_i)' with a vector β_i on the conjugate line; then D_i = α_i ⊗ β_i has type (1,1) in every Galois conjugate and hence is algebraic by Lefschetz (1,1). Multiply these four divisor kernels into K = prod_i q_i^* D_i on A × A'. A sign bookkeeping (six swaps) gives K = t_α ⊗ t_β, so integrating over A:
  p'_*( p^* Z ∪ K ) = k · t_β,
which is algebraic (k ≠ 0 algebraic). Dividing by k shows the line generated by t_β is algebraic; ordinary complex conjugation moves (c,c,1,1) to (1,1,c,c), i.e. to L. Done.
Original paper, Section 3, proof of Proposition 3.1 (Eq. 3.4).

Exercise: The surface criterion for complex spans. Why do complex classes suffice?
The theta construction below produces classes η_i with complex (transcendental) coefficients, not algebraic ones. "Corollary (Surface-Span)" relaxes the hypothesis: it is enough that each η_i lie in the complex span of {h(U(v_i)_{λ_i}) : h a rational Hodge map}, and that ∫_S η1∪η2∪η3∪η4 ≠ 0. Then expanding the nonzero integral into a finite sum of products of rational-map images, at least one term is nonzero, and that term is in the Surface Criterion's shape. So we never need algebraic-coefficient vectors — only membership in a complex span of rational Hodge images. This is why the arithmetic work in Sections 5-6 is phrased as "the theta class lies in the complex span of images of U(v)_1".
Original paper, Section 3, Corollary 3.2 (Surface Criterion for Complex Spans).


Exercise: The ball and SU(V). Where do the theta forms live?
Let E be a Galois CM field, F = E^+, s = [F:Q] >= 2. Choose an E/F-Hermitian space V of E-dimension 3 that has signature (2,1) at the distinguished embedding μ = 1 and is positive definite at every other real place of F. Let G = SU(V). The space D = { negative lines L in V_μ } is the complex 2-ball. Because V is definite everywhere else, G is F-anisotropic; arithmetic subgroups are cocompact lattices, so for a deep principal congruence level Γ, the quotient X = Γ  D is a smooth connected compact Kähler surface. This X is the surface S of the surface criterion (up to passing to deep enough level and to a projective model). Everything is built on X.
Original paper, Section 4, "The ball and the sign data".

Exercise: Multiplicity spaces and positive Lagrangians. How does a sign function become geometry?
For a sign function d_i(τ) = v_i(τ^{-1}), take an E/F-Hermitian line W_i with a form chosen so that the "positive Lagrangian" F_i ⊂ V ⊗ W_i over D is holomorphic and positive. Concretely, F_i is a half-dimensional subbundle of the (complexified) symplectic bundle whose fiber at the active place is L ⊗ (W_i)_μ plus the annihilator on the conjugate side, and at each compact place is the full summand selected by the sign d_i(τ). This F_i is isotropic, half-dimensional, and positive. Its determinant bundle is L^{2n} up to a constant line (n = dim W_i), and its half-determinant lives in a line you can identify with Ω^1_D times a constant. All of this exists over the rationals; the signs d_i encode the arithmetic.
Original paper, Section 4, "Positive Lagrangians and differential bundles", Lemma 4.2.

Exercise: Theta series and Taylor tensors. Where do one-forms and two-forms come from?
On the Siegel domain covering D, form the standard theta series
  θ_φ(Z, z) = sum_{x ∈ F^a} φ(x) exp( πi Σ x_τ^t Z_τ x_τ + 2πi Σ x_τ^t z_τ ),
with φ a finite-adelic Schwartz input. Completing it by a Gaussian in z cancels the quadratic exponential in the transformation law and yields clean Taylor coefficients T_{k,φ}(Z) in z^{⊗k}. Weil's rational covariance gives a tensor transformation law T_{k,φ}(gZ) = j(g,Z)·Sym^k(J)·T_{k,φ}(Z), valued in (half-det F)^{⊗} Sym^k F. The first coefficient T_{1,φ} is holomorphic and, projected to the right summand, gives a holomorphic one-form Θ_i(φ) on D. The second coefficient T_{2,φ}, projected to Sym^2 of the conjugate Lagrangian and then put through the double-alternating map alt_2, gives a holomorphic (2,0)-form Ξ_W(φ) on D whenever the multiplicity is a rank-two W = W1 ⊕ W2.
Original paper, Section 4, "Rational theta covariance" (Lemma 4.3), and "Rank-two tensors, wedges".

Exercise: The one-forms span cotangent fibers. Can four of them be chosen to be nonzero together?
Yes. "Proposition (Theta One-Forms)" says: as the finite input φ varies, the values of Θ_i(φ) at any fixed point of D span the whole cotangent space Ω^1_D at that point. The proof uses very thin inputs φ_k supported on a single lattice point x_0 as k -> infinity; their gradients converge to a single nonzero vector 2πi·(x_0) times an exponential, and ranging x_0 spans the fiber. Therefore finitely many actual inputs already give a basis of the fiber, and, applying the unitary splitting lemma, all of them descend at one common principal level. So we can pick two nonzero one-forms with a nonzero wedge at a point.
Original paper, Section 4, Proposition 4.5 (Theta One-Forms).

Exercise: Wedges as finite sums. Why is the two-form a sum of one-form wedges?
Take W = W1 ⊕ W2 (rank two), so a product input φ = Σ_r φ_{1,r} ⊗ φ_{2,r} in adapted coordinates. Completed theta series multiply, so the total degree-two Taylor coefficient is T_2^{(1)}T_0^{(2)} + T_1^{(1)}T_1^{(2)} + T_0^{(1)}T_2^{(2)}. Applying alt_2 kills the two "unmixed" terms (both factors sit in the same one-dimensional line), leaving the mixed term, which is exactly Σ_r Θ_1(φ_{1,r}) ∧ Θ_2(φ_{2,r}). So Ξ_W(φ) is a finite sum of wedges of the one-forms, holomorphic at every rational orthogonal decomposition. And by choosing inputs whose one-forms have a nonzero wedge, Ξ_W(φ) is nonzero for suitable φ. This is "Proposition (Rank-Two Tensor)" parts (i)-(ii).
Original paper, Section 4, Proposition 4.6 (Rank-Two Tensors), Eq. (4.7).

Exercise: Fixed-data variation. What is held constant while the sign pairs are compared?
Keep fixed: the rational W, the symplectic space and the SU(V)-action, the global Weil and unitary splitting choices, the finite action, a single total finite input φ, and the compact quotient X. Vary only the way W decomposes at the compact places, t in a parameter space T: choose decompositions W_τ = W_τ^+ ⊕ W_τ^- with dimensions and signs matching d1,d2. At the active place keep the total Lagrangian unchanged (it depends only on W_μ, not the decomposition). "Proposition (Rank-Two Tensor)(iii)" then says: the resulting form Ξ_W(φ;t) is smooth in t in the C^∞ topology on the fixed compact X, and the same principal level works throughout a neighborhood.
Original paper, Section 4, Proposition 4.6(iii), "Rank-two tensors, wedges, and fixed-data variation".

Exercise: The mixed period. How is the nonzero surface period produced?
Suppose d1 + d2 = d3 + d4, all positive at μ. Start with a decomposition for d1,d2 giving a nonzero holomorphic two-form ξ = Ξ_W(φ;t_0). Because the sign pairs agree, weak approximation produces a rational orthogonal decomposition W = W3 ⊕ W4 with signs d3,d4 whose compact-place Lagrangians are arbitrarily close to those at t_0, while the active Lagrangian stays exactly the same. For a close rational choice t', the form ξ' = Ξ_W(φ;t') is again holomorphic on X, with the SAME total finite input φ, and continuity of the integral plus positivity of ξ ∧ ξ̄ give:
  ∫_X ξ ∧ ξ̄' ≠ 0.
Now factor the two endpoints into finitely many one-form inputs; on a finite cover π : X' -> X all individual one-forms descend, and the covering formula gives ∫_{X'} π^*ξ ∧ π^*ξ̄' = deg(π)·∫_X ξ ∧ ξ̄' ≠ 0. In the expansion, at least one pair of individual wedge terms has nonzero mixed period. Those four one-forms are exactly the data the Surface Criterion needs. This is "Corollary (Theta Switch Continuity)".
Original paper, Section 4, Corollary 4.8, Eq. (4.9) and (4.11).

Exercise: What is proven so far, and what is still missing?
The theta construction supplies the nonzero mixed period on a compact ball quotient X'. But the Surface Criterion (in its complex-span form) requires that each of the four one-forms lie in the complex span of images of U(v_i) under rational Hodge maps. The theta forms are only known to be complex (transcendental) cohomology classes; nothing yet says which rational CM pieces they come from. That identification is the arithmetic input of Sections 5-6, and it is the technical heart of the paper's second half.
Original paper, transition at end of Section 4.


Exercise: Making the ball quotient projective. Why is the surface actually algebraic?
A compact Kähler surface need not be algebraic. To get a projective X, the paper realizes it as a component of a moduli space. Fix E, V, the ball D, a lattice Λ, and the Lie ranks r_μ = 2, r_c = 1, and {r_τ, r_{cτ}} = {0,3} at each other conjugate pair. Consider the moduli scheme S of tuples (B, λ, ι_E, η_N): B an abelian variety of dimension 3s, λ a polarization of fixed type, ι_E : O_E -> End(B) a unital action whose Rosati adjoint is c, η_N a full ordered basis of N-torsion, and each Lie summand of the given rank. "Lemma (Reference Moduli)" proves S is of finite type, smooth, and projective over a number field K_0; its connected complex components are compact ball quotients; and the component from Λ is exactly ΓD for the deep principal level Γ. So the algebraic-geometry machinery of Section 5 can be applied to X = S_0.
Original paper, Section 5a, Lemma 5.2 (Reference Moduli).

Exercise: Good models and ordinary points. Why does the argument specialize to finite fields?
Spread S, the universal abelian scheme, and the Albanese maps a_j : S_j -> A_j = Alb(S_j) over the ring of integers of a finite extension K of K_0, after removing finitely many primes. "Lemma (Good Ordinary Points)" gives: smooth proper models with geometrically connected fibers, good abelian models, and the property that differences a_j(x) - a_j(y) generate each A_j. Then at any rational prime q that is completely split in K, the ordinary locus of the universal abelian scheme is dense in each geometric component of the special fiber. The proof uses integral idempotents from rational orthogonal projectors to exhibit one explicitly ordinary point, then openness of ordinariness and irreducibility. This density is what lets the finite-field computation below control the characteristic-zero endomorphism.
Original paper, Section 5a, Lemma 5.3 (Good Ordinary Models).

Exercise: Algebraic Hecke operators. What is a Hecke operator, in concrete terms?
At the split prime q, the local similitude group is G_q = (prod_{j} GL_3(Q_q)) × Q_q^×, and its integral subgroup K_q has volume 1. The spherical Hecke algebra is spanned by double-coset characteristic functions, each specified by a polarization multiplier exponent h and, on each block j, an unordered triple (a_{j,1}, a_{j,2}, a_{j,3}) of elementary exponents. A single positive Hecke operator (0 ≤ a ≤ h) is geometrically: pick a subgroup H ⊂ B[q^h] with those elementary divisors, maximal isotropic (so the q^h-scaled form descends), take the quotient f:B -> B/H, and require f^∨ λ' f = q^h λ. This quotient is an algebraic construction in families. "Lemma (Hecke on Albanese)" proves each such operator gives an integral endomorphism of A_S = prod_j A_j, defined over K, whose pullback on H^1 is the sum of pullbacks over its branches (all translated to the fixed basepoint). Negative-exponent positions are defined by clearing a fixed finite-order central power, so the full rational spherical algebra acts.
Original paper, Section 5a, Lemma 5.4 (Hecke on Albanese), Eq. (5.1)-(5.2).

Exercise: From Hecke operators to Frobenius. What is the cubic identity?
Let T_i be the Hecke operator with i active exponents equal to 1 (and multiplier exponent 0), for i = 1,2,3, and let C_0 be the central multiplier-by-q position. Define D_i = C_0^i T_i; these are integral endomorphisms of A_S. Let Φ be the q-power Frobenius endomorphism of the good reduction. "Proposition (Albanese Congruence)" proves, in the integral endomorphism ring:
  Φ^3 - D_1 Φ^2 + q D_2 Φ - q^3 D_3 = 0.
The proof counts quotient isogenies at an ordinary point. The active q-divisible group splits canonically as G_w = M_0 ⊕ E_0 (multiplicative of height 1, étale of height 2). Three operations, X_0 (kernel M_0[q], multiplier q), Y_0 (kernel an étale line ℓ, multiplier q), Z_0 (kernel E_0[q], multiplier q^2), have explicitly counted specializations, giving multiset identities D_1 : X_0 + qY_0, D_2 : X_0 Y_0 + q^2 Z_0, D_3 : X_0 Z_0. Identifying X_0 with Frobenius and checking it commutes with Y_0, Z_0 makes the operator above vanish on Albanese differences; density of ordinary points makes it vanish identically.
Original paper, Section 5b, Proposition 5.5 (Albanese Congruence), Eq. (5.3) and (5.5)-(5.6).


Exercise: The active Hecke character of a theta class. What is its shape?
A theta one-form is an eigenvector for the active subalgebra H_w^0 (supported on multiplier-0, nonactive-trivial positions). "Lemma (Active Hecke Theta)" shows the geometric Hecke action on theta inputs is the INVERSE spherical action; so the eigenvalue data is the inverse Satake parameter. Computing it takes two steps: (1) the input φ is fixed by SL_3(Z_q), so only radial Schwartz functions b_j = 1_{q^j Z_q^3} occur, and the dilation D_q b_j = q^{3/2} b_{j+1} acts cyclically; (2) fixing the other finite places, a rational scalar r with valuations ±M at w and its conjugate makes the class an eigenvector with D_q^M = ω. "Proposition (Theta Satake)" then proves every active character of a theta class has projective Satake parameter
  {q^{1/2}, q^{-1/2}, e},
with e = e_0^{-1} for a root e_0^M = ω, e algebraic, all complex conjugates of modulus 1, and for every σ in Gal(Q-bar/Q):
  ν_ι(σ e) = -(3/2)·d((σ|_E)^{-1}) = -(3/2)·v(σ|_E).
So the compact-place signs v are exactly encoded in the q-adic valuations of the Satake parameter. A scalar conjugate has the same shape with third entry ±σe.
Original paper, Section 6, Lemma 6.1 and Proposition 6.3 (Theta Satake), Eq. (6.3) and (6.4).

Exercise: The full character and the central relation. How is the common Satake scalar pinned?
A full numerical Hecke character χ extending the active η has GL_3 parameter some scalar multiple b·(q^{1/2}, q^{-1/2}, e'). Let D_3 = C_0^3 T_3 be the central determinant operator. "Lemma (Central Finite Order)" shows a positive power of C_0 and of T_3 is the identity on A_S, hence the character value of D_3 (which is b^3 e') is a root of unity. This forces b algebraic with all complex conjugates of modulus 1, so the Satake entries stay in the right shape.
Original paper, Section 6, Lemma 6.4 (Central Finite Order) and Eq. (6.5).

Exercise: Frobenius slopes. How is the Hodge type read off p-adically?
Fix a full character χ on H^1(A_S). Its Hecke numerology at q, combined with the cubic Frobenius identity, gives: every Frobenius eigenvalue is a root of X^3 - q e_1(α) X^2 + q^2 e_2(α) X - q^3 e_3(α) = prod_{j=1}^3 (X - q α_j), where (α_1,α_2,α_3) = b(q^{1/2}, q^{-1/2}, e'). The three candidates for a root are
  b q^{3/2},  b q^{1/2},  q b e'.
Weil's theorem for abelian varieties over a finite field says every Frobenius eigenvalue has complex absolute value q^{1/2}. Since |b| = |e'| = 1, the three absolute values are q^{3/2}, q^{1/2}, q; so only b q^{1/2} survives. On the whole generalized eigenspace there is exactly one Frobenius slope, and it is
  t_η = ν_ι(b q^{1/2}) = 1/2 - (1/3)·ν_ι(e'),
which is 0 or 1 by Theta Satake. This is "Proposition (Active Hodge Types)", first half.
Original paper, Section 6, Proposition 6.5 (Active Hodge Types), Eq. (6.6)-(6.8).

Exercise: From a slope {0,1} to a pure Hodge type. Why is one slope enough?
The Hecke projectors p_χ are algebraic-coefficient polynomials in extending algebraic endomorphisms, so they preserve the de Rham (Hodge) filtration and commute with crystalline Frobenius. "Lemma (Filtered Hecke Summands)" states: for a weakly admissible filtered-Frobenius module whose filtration has only indices 0 and 1, any filtration-preserving Frobenius-commuting idempotent whose image has all-slopes-0-or-1 has image's F^1 either 0 or the whole image, according to the slope (0 or 1). Equality of Hodge and Newton numbers makes it work. The caveat is essential: a slope projector that does NOT preserve the filtration can fail (give a diagonal example). So the fact that p_χ comes from an actual endomorphism is doing real work here. Applying this on H^1(A_S) at the fixed prime identifies the Hodge type of each full generalized summand: slope 1 = holomorphic (1,0), slope 0 = antiholomorphic (0,1). This is the second half of Proposition (Active Hodge Types).
Original paper, Section 6, Lemma 6.6 (Filtered Hecke Summands) and Proposition 6.5.

Exercise: The sign matching. How is the type tied to v(g)?
Recall from Theta Satake that ν_ι(σe) = -(3/2)·v(σ|_E). Put g = σ|_E. Then
  t_{ση} = 1/2 - (1/3)·ν_ι(σ e) = 1/2 + (1/2)·v(g) = (1 + v(g))/2.
So if v(g) = 1 the whole active space for ση is holomorphic (1,0); if v(g) = -1 it is antiholomorphic (0,1). This holds for EVERY vector in the space, not just the theta vectors, because the statement is about the whole generalized eigenspace. This is "Equation (CM sign-to-Hodge-type)", the technical bridge from a q-adic slope back to a complex Hodge type.
Original paper, Section 6, Eq. (6.9) (cm-sign-to-hodge-type).

Exercise: The extraction theorem. How does the theta class get a rational CM source?
"Theorem (Degree-One CM Extraction)": given the sign v and a finite collection of theta inputs, there is a deep principal ball quotient S_0 on which every associated theta one-form class lies in
  span_C { f(U(v)_1) : f in Hom_Hdg(U(v), H^1(S_0,Q)) }.
Proof: extend the theta class by zero on other components; it is holomorphic; decompose it into active generalized spaces. For each active η and its parameter e, Theta Satake plus Active Hodge Types give the sign-to-Hodge equation for every Galois conjugate. Now pick a nonzero x in an active space and a nonzero u in U(v)_1, and define a Q-bar-linear map h:U(v) -> H^1(S) by h(u) = x, h = 0 on every other embedding line. Every scalar conjugate of h is a Hodge map (source line type matches target line type). Scalar Descent then writes h as a Q-bar-linear combination of rational Hodge maps. Taking complex spans covers the whole active component, including transcendental coefficients. Restricting to S_0 gives the cited span, and deeper levels persist by pullback. So each theta one-form IS a complex combination of images of the selected CM line.
Original paper, Section 6, Theorem 6.1 (Degree-One CM Extraction), Eq. (6.1) and (6.9).

Exercise: The finite-family corollary. Can all four families be put on ONE surface?
Yes. "Corollary (Finite-Family Extraction)" states: given any finite list of sign functions v_1,...,v_m (each positive at μ) and finite theta input families for them, and any prescribed torsion-free principal base quotient X_base, there is a common deeper principal cover Y -> X_base (smooth, connected, projective) on which EVERY theta class from EVERY family lies in its prescribed U(v_j)_1-span. The maps are rational Hodge maps; the conclusion persists under further pullback. Proof: apply Degree-One CM Extraction family by family, take a common deeper level that is also contained in X_base, and use the persistence clause. The auxiliary primes for the different v_j may be different — nothing forces them to agree, and the corollary does not require them to.
Original paper, Section 6, Corollary 6.2 (Finite-Family Extraction).


Exercise: Normalization. Why may we assume all v_i(1) = 1 and s >= 2?
"Lemma (Switch Normalization)" reduces the Four-Factor Switch to this case. If v1 and v2 are nowhere simultaneously positive, oddness forces v2 = -v1 and v4 = -v3, and the four slots split into two all-conjugate (1,1) pairs, automatically algebraic. Otherwise some g has v1(g) = v2(g) = 1; the relation makes all four signs positive there. Translating by a scalar automorphism extending g turns the statement into one about the right-translates v_i^g(τ) = v_i(τg), which are odd with v_i^g(1) = 1, and the line transfer lemma carries the normalized statement back. The case s = 1 has only one odd sign function positive at 1, so the tensor is a product of ordinary pairs. Hence it suffices to work with s >= 2 and v_i(1) = 1 for all four.
Original paper, Section 2, Lemma 2.11 (Switch Normalization).

Exercise: Assembly. How do the four theta forms land on one projective surface?
Work in the normalized case, with d_i(τ) = v_i(τ^{-1}), so d1 + d2 = d3 + d4 and all positive at μ. Choose the rank-three Hermitian space V, ball D, and the rational orthogonal decomposition W = W1 ⊕ W2 for d1,d2. Rank-Two Tensor (ii) supplies a total finite input φ with Ξ_W(φ) nonzero; Corollary (Theta Switch Continuity) supplies a second decomposition for d3,d4 with a nonzero mixed period on a common quotient X'. On X' the two endpoint forms expand, with the SAME φ, as
  ξ1 = Σ_a c_a Θ_1(φ_{1a}) ∧ Θ_2(φ_{2a}),   ξ2 = Σ_b c'_b Θ_3(φ_{3b}) ∧ Θ_4(φ_{4b}),
and ∫_{X'} ξ1 ∧ ξ2-bar ≠ 0. These are four FINITE families of individual theta inputs. Apply Corollary (Finite-Family Extraction) to them with base X': get a common smooth connected projective principal cover π : Y -> X' on which all four U(v_i)_1-span conclusions hold. Pull everything back.
Original paper, Section 7 (assembly), first half.

Exercise: Finding one nonzero cup term. Why is one term enough, and what are the four classes?
On Y, write ϑ_{ia} for the pulled-back theta cohomology classes. The mixed period on Y is a finite sum,
  ∫_Y π^*ξ1 ∧ π^*ξ2-bar = Σ_{a,b} c_a c'_b 〈 ϑ_{1a} ∪ ϑ_{2a} ∪ ϑ_{3b}-bar ∪ ϑ_{4b}-bar , [Y] 〉.
Since the total is nonzero, at least one term (a,b) is nonzero. Keep its four classes ϑ_1,ϑ_2,ϑ_3,ϑ_4 (all holomorphic one-forms on Y). By Finite-Family Extraction each ϑ_i lies in the complex span of {h(U(v_i)_1)}. Because the h are rational Hodge maps, they commute with scalar complex conjugation, which sends U(v_i)_1 to U(v_i)_c; so the conjugates ϑ_3-bar, ϑ_4-bar lie in the complex span of {h(U(v_i)_c)}. Therefore the four classes ϑ_1, ϑ_2, ϑ_3-bar, ϑ_4-bar satisfy Corollary (Surface-Span) on the single projective surface Y, with source labels (1,1,c,c) and the nonzero period just selected. Hence the normalized switch line is algebraic.
Original paper, Section 7, Eq. (7.1).

Exercise: Finishing the Main Theorem. How does the Four-Factor Switch give the Hodge conjecture?
The Four-Factor Switch is now proved for every Galois CM field (normalization + assembly). Section 2's reduction, "Proposition (Switch Reduction)", then turns it into the Main Theorem: every rational Hodge class on a CM abelian variety lies in the rational cycle span. The switch connects monomial lines by two-row moves, unchanged slots are automatic (1,1) pieces, the transition-composition formula composes them, and final scalar descent lands back on the original variety A. Finite products and powers of CM varieties are themselves CM, so they are covered too. That is "Theorem (Main Theorem)".
Original paper, Section 7 (assembly), final step and Proposition 2.10.

Exercise: Generalized Hodge. How is "a Hodge substructure is supported on a codimension-r subset" obtained?
"The Generalized Hodge Conjecture" (Grothendieck) predicts: a rational Hodge substructure W ⊂ H^k(X,Q) with W(r) effective lies in the coniveau filtration N^r, i.e. W is killed by removing a closed algebraic subset of codimension at least r. For CM A it is "Corollary (Generalized Hodge for CM)". Sketch: reduce to W simple. Let r_0 be the smallest Hodge index in W. "Lemma (Effective Realization)" builds an auxiliary CM abelian variety B (possibly a point) and a surjective rational Hodge map f : H^n(B,Q)(-r_0) -> W, by writing W as a line over a CM field E and decomposing its type into n weight-one CM types. Composing with W ⊂ H^k(A,Q) and using Poincaré duality, f corresponds to a rational Hodge class of type (b+r_0, b+r_0) on B × A. Since B × A is CM, the Main Theorem represents this exact class by a rational cycle Γ of codimension b + r_0. The projection of Γ to A is a closed Z of codimension ≥ r_0 ≥ r, and functoriality makes W restrict to zero on A  Z.
Original paper, Section 8, Lemma 8.1 (Effective Realization) and Corollary 8.2 (Generalized Hodge for CM).

Exercise: Tate conjecture. How does a complex Hodge statement imply a finite-field l-adic one?
"The Tate Conjecture for abelian varieties over finite fields" is "Corollary (Tate)". The bridge is Milne's theorem (1999, Thm 7.1): if the Hodge conjecture holds for all CM abelian varieties, then (a) on any abelian variety A over F_q, numerical and l-adic homological equivalence of rational cycles coincide, and (b) the geometric l-adic Tate classes (fixed over some finite extension) are spanned by cycle classes. Milne's hypothesis is exactly the Main Theorem. One then descends a finite-field-of-definition statement to F_q itself: a class fixed by the full Galois group, written as cycles over the finite extension F_{q^m}, descends by averaging over Gal(F_{q^m}/F_q) and dividing by m (legitimate in Q_l). The auxiliary CM field in Milne's theorem is chosen to contain a real subfield of degree > 2 that is inert at the residue. Conclusion: the Tate conjecture holds for EVERY abelian variety over a finite field, not only CM ones.
Original paper, Section 8, Corollary 8.5 (Tate), after Milne 1999, correcting the auxiliary field as in the addenda.

Exercise: Hodge standard conjecture. What is positivity of the intersection form, and why does it follow?
"The Hodge Standard Conjecture" says: for an abelian variety A of dimension g over an algebraically closed field, with a rational ample divisor class h, the form on primitive algebraic classes x, y in codimension r ≤ g/2,
  (x, y) ↦ (-1)^r · deg(x·y·h^{g-2r}),
is positive definite once extended to R. "Corollary (Hodge Standard)" gives it in EVERY characteristic, plus equality of numerical and l-adic homological equivalence. The proof is Milne (2002, Thm 3.3), whose hypothesis is again exactly the Main Theorem: positivity follows from polarizations of CM Hodge structures, transferred to arbitrary abelian varieties. The "rational ample class" and the reduction from mh (a hyperplane section, via Bertini) to h are bookkeeping. So Hodge theory for CM varieties settles the standard conjectures for all abelian varieties.
Original paper, Section 8, Corollary 8.6 (Hodge Standard), after Milne 2002.

Exercise: Specialization. Does this say anything about abelian varieties that are not CM?
One more consequence, "Remark (Algebraic Specialization)": if A/Q-bar has good reduction A_0 at a p-adic place, then every rational Hodge class on A specializes to the class of one rational algebraic cycle on A_0, simultaneously in all prime-to-p realizations and in crystalline cohomology (after extending coefficients). This uses the CM transport of the companion paper, not the Main Theorem directly: transport writes the specialization as a pullback of a specialized CM Hodge class, main theorem algebraizes the CM class, and Chow pullback delivers the cycle. It asserts algebraicity on the special fiber; it does NOT solve the Hodge conjecture for general complex abelian varieties.
Original paper, Section 8, Remark 8.7 (Algebraic Specialization).

Exercise: Big picture. What is the one-sentence summary of the whole proof?
Convert "every CM Hodge class is algebraic" into "one four-factor tensor line is algebraic" (Section 2); convert that into "four one-forms on one surface have a nonzero integral" (Section 3); build those four one-forms from theta series on a compact ball quotient (Section 4); prove p-adically, via Hecke operators, a Frobenius cubic identity, and the Satake parameter, that each one-form is a complex combination of images of the right CM line (Sections 5-6); glue onto one projective surface and read off the switch, hence the Main Theorem (Section 7); harvest the generalized Hodge, Tate, and Hodge-standard consequences (Section 8). The single hard input is the arithmetic identification of the theta one-forms' CM sources, which is where Frobenius slopes become Hodge types.
