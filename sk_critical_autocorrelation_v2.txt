Consider the zero-field Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin glass, with energy
functional
$$H_{n,J}(\sigma)=-n^{-1/2}\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$$
on spin configurations $\sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n)\in\{-1,1\}^n$. The
couplings $J_{ij}$, $1\le i<j\le n$, are independent real random variables
of mean zero and variance one, and the extension $J_{ji}=J_{ij}$, $J_{ii}=0$
makes $J$ a symmetric matrix. Two coupling laws are considered: the
standard Gaussian, and the uniform law on the two-point set $\{-1,1\}$. At
inverse temperature $\beta$ the Gibbs measure on the cube is
$$\pi_{n,J,\beta}(\sigma)=Z_{n,J,\beta}^{-1}\exp(-\beta H_{n,J}(\sigma)),$$
with $Z_{n,J,\beta}$ the normalizer. The value $\beta=1$ is fixed. The
shorthand $\pi_{n,J}$ denotes $\pi_{n,J,1}$.

./skrefs/sherrington-kirkpatrick-1975.pdf

The interaction is disordered and of infinite range: each spin interacts
with every other spin with a coupling of typical size $n^{-1/2}$, of random
sign, so that ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic bonds coexist on the
same footing. Spin configurations of comparable energy then differ at a
macroscopic number of sites, and a single global magnetization is not a
sufficient summary of the low-temperature phases. Parisi introduced an
infinite family of overlap parameters, organised hierarchically, to
describe the resulting state space.

./skrefs/parisi-1979.pdf

The static theory of this hierarchical structure is by now a theorem
rather than a heuristic. The cavity-method interpolation of Aizenman,
Lebowitz, and Ruelle and the subsequent reorganization of Aizenman, Sims,
and Starr give the Parisi variational formula as an identity for the
infinite-volume free energy of the SK model. Guerra proved the matching
lower bound, uniformly in the size of the system, by a two-parameter
interpolation between the SK Hamiltonian and a mixture of independent
spin systems. Talagrand completed the identification by proving the
corresponding upper bound, confirming the Parisi prediction in full
generality. In mixed $p$-spin models the analogous statements are due to
Panchenko (Annals of Probability 42 (2014), 946-958) and to Guerra and
Toninelli, who proved existence of the thermodynamic limit and the
associated variational characterization. Modern offshoots, concerned
with replica symmetry breaking in more general external fields and with
lower-temperature regimes, include the constructions of Lopatto and of
Aronow and Lopatto, and the existence results of Zhou, all at full
replica symmetry breaking depth.

Two companion manuscripts, with the titles ``Critical mixing in the
Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model'' and ``Critical slowing down in the

Every random function in the theorem statement is defined on the positive
halfline with values in the real line, and every limit in law is a
statement about the space $C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))$ of continuous
real functions on $(0,\infty)$ equipped with uniform convergence on
compact subintervals of $(0,\infty)$.

Consider the heat-bath process on $\{-1,1\}^n$ governed by the Gibbs
measure $\pi_{n,J}$. Each site carries an independent Poisson clock of
rate one. When the clock at site $i$ rings, the spin $\sigma_i$ is
replaced by a fresh sample from its conditional law given the other
$n-1$ spins. The rate of the jump at site $i$ is one and the jump
distribution depends only on the current configuration through the local
field
$$h_i(\sigma)=n^{-1/2}\sum_{j\ne i}J_{ij}\sigma_j,$$
in the sense that the accepted new value $a\in\{-1,1\}$ has probability
$(1+a\tanh h_i(\sigma))/2$ and is independent of $\sigma_i$ conditional
on $\sigma_{-i}$. The resulting Markov process on the cube is reversible
and stationary for $\pi_{n,J}$. Write $P_t^J=e^{-tH}$ for its semigroup
acting on $L^2(\pi_{n,J})$, with $H\ge0$ the heat-bath generator. One
unit of time corresponds to $n$ site updates on average.

./skrefs/sherrington-kirkpatrick-1975.pdf

The equilibrium spin autocorrelation measures how much the configuration
remembers its initial state after time $t$ under $P_t^J$, averaged both
over the stationary Gibbs randomness of the spins and over the sites:
$$C_{n,J}(t)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\langle\sigma_i,P_t^J\sigma_i\rangle_{\pi_{n,J}},\qquad t\ge0.$$
Since $P_0^J$ is the identity and $P_t^J\sigma_i$ is the conditional
expectation of $\sigma_i$ given $t$ steps of the process, $C_{n,J}(0)=1$
and $C_{n,J}$ is nonincreasing and takes values in $[0,1]$. At
$\beta=1$ the correlation decays on a time scale that grows with $n$,
and the appropriate rescaling defines
$$A_{n,J}(s)=n^{1/3}C_{n,J}(sn^{2/3}),\qquad s>0.$$
The only randomness in $A_{n,J}$ is the coupling matrix $J$; thermal
fluctuations and the noise of the clock process are already averaged in
$C_{n,J}$.

The statement of the main result requires an auxiliary determinantal
edge point process and a random measure on coordinate space. The first
is standard: for a Gaussian orthogonal ensemble matrix, the rescaled
eigenvalues $n^{2/3}(2-\lambda_a)$ converge in the sense of finite
dimensional distributions to an increasing sequence $g_1<g_2<\ldots$ of
nonnegative-slope edge points, as described in Ramirez, Rider, and Virag.
The second is constructed explicitly from the sequence $g$ and a single
positive real number $c_*$; its closed-form definition is
$$\mathcal A_g(s)=\sum_{a\ge1}\langle x_a,e^{-sc_*H_\Pi}x_a\rangle_\Pi,\qquad s>0,$$
where $\Pi=\Pi_g$ is a random probability measure on $\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$
defined by conditioning independent Gaussian coordinates on a centered
sum, and $H_\Pi$ is the nonnegative self-adjoint operator associated to
a closed Dirichlet form on $L^2(\Pi)$ whose domain is the closure of
smooth compact cylinder functions. Both $\Pi$ and $H_\Pi$ depend on $g$;
neither depends on $c_*$, and $c_*$ is a positive deterministic real
number determined by the disorder. The full main result follows.

./skrefs/arxiv-math-0607331.pdf

Main theorem. There is a Borel probability measure $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$
on $C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))$ such that, for either coupling law
(the standard Gaussian, and the symmetric law on $\{-1,1\}$),
$$\mathrm{Law}_{D}(A_{n,J})\Longrightarrow Q_{\mathrm{crit}}\qquad(n\to\infty),$$
in the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of $(0,\infty)$.
The limiting measure $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is not a Dirac mass. It assigns
probability one to the set of nonnegative, nonincreasing continuous
functions $f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ that are not identically zero and
satisfy $\lim_{s\to\infty}f(s)=0$. The Gaussian and Rademacher cases share
the normalization in the definition of $A_{n,J}$ and the same
coefficient $c_*$; $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$ equals the law of $\mathcal A_g$
with this common $c_*$ and the same edge law for $g$.

The restriction to positive times is essential: $A_{n,J}(0)=n^{1/3}$
diverges. At every fixed $s>0$, the limiting random variable
$\mathcal A_g(s)$ has unbounded support, and in particular $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$
is not a point mass. The limiting measure is the law of a sum of
coordinate autocorrelations for a reversible semigroup in countably many
edge coordinates. The equilibrium measure $\Pi$ of those coordinates is
Gaussian conditioned on a renormalized quadratic constraint whose
parameters are the random GOE edge points $g_1<g_2<\cdots$. The single
positive deterministic coefficient $c_*$ accounts for the relaxation of
microscopic spin gradients, and is characterized by a determinate moment
problem for a measure $\omega$ supported on $[0,\infty)$.

Write $\mu=\pi_{n,J}$ and $T=n^{2/3}$ for the critical time scale. The heat-bath
generator of the process admits a Dirichlet form that is central to the
analysis. For a function $f$ on the cube define half differences by
$$D_if(X)=\frac{f(X^{i,+})-f(X^{i,-})}{2},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,$$
where $X^{i,\pm}$ denotes the configuration obtained from $X$ by setting the
$i$th spin to $\pm1$, with the other coordinates unchanged. Define
$t_i(X)=\tanh h_i(X)$ and $w_i(X)=1-t_i(X)^2$. The generator is
$$(Hf)(X)=\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-t_i(X))D_if(X),$$
a nonnegative self-adjoint operator on $L^2(\mu)$ with associated Dirichlet
form
$$\mathcal E(f)=\sum_{i=1}^n\mu\bigl[w_i\,(D_if)^2\bigr].$$
Because $|t_i|\le1$ pointwise, $0\le w_i\le1$. The factor $w_i$ is the
conditional variance of the spin at site $i$ under the Gibbs measure given
the other spins, so the Dirichlet form weights each site by its local
susceptibility. The mean-field equations of Sompolinsky and Zippelius
(Physical Review B 25 (1982), 6860-6875) first isolated the role of such a
form in the spin-glass phase. The rate-one site clocks give exactly this
normalization.

./skrefs/sherrington-kirkpatrick-1975.pdf

The couplings under the two laws have the same mean and variance. The
leading spectral structure therefore depends only on the second moments,
and the Gaussian case exposes it in the cleanest form. Independent
Gaussian off-diagonal entries $J_{ij}/\sqrt n$, $i<j$, together with
independent diagonal entries $N(0,2/n)$, produce a symmetric matrix $W$
whose upper triangle together with diagonal forms a Gaussian orthogonal
ensemble: its $n(n+1)/2$ entries are jointly Gaussian, centered, with
variance $1/n$ for off-diagonal entries and $2/n$ for diagonal entries.
The extra term $n^{-1/2}\sum_i W_{ii}\sigma_i^2$ is constant on the cube,
so the Gibbs measure $\mu$ and the generator $H$ are unchanged.

The spectral decomposition of $W$ is written
$$W=U\Lambda U^{\mathsf T},\qquad \lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n,$$
with $U$ a Haar-distributed orthogonal matrix conditionally on $\Lambda$,
after an independent choice of eigenvector signs. Define
$$d_a=2-\lambda_a,\qquad g_{a,n}=n^{2/3}d_a,\qquad Y_a=v_a^{\mathsf T}X,\qquad x_a=n^{-1/3}Y_a,$$
where $v_a$ is the $a$th column of $U$. The combination of the $n^{-1/2}$
interaction scale with the value $\beta=1$ places the upper edge of
$\Lambda$ near $\lambda=2$, with $n^{2/3}d_a$ of order one. The coordinates
$x_a$ measure fluctuations of order $n^{-1/3}$ in the eigenframe of $W$,
and the rescaling $n^{2/3}$ in time converts those fluctuations into a
nontrivial limiting dynamics.

For $z>0$ define the resolvent trace
$$B_n(z)=\sum_{a=1}^n\langle x_a,(z+TH)^{-1}x_a\rangle_\mu.$$
Since $U$ is orthogonal, $\mathrm{tr}(z+TH)^{-1}=\sum_a\langle Y_a,(z+TH)^{-1}Y_a\rangle_\mu$,
so the choice of orthonormal basis does not affect $B_n$. The operator
$TH$ is nonnegative and self-adjoint on $L^2(\mu)$, and Fubini's theorem
applied to the spectral resolution of $TH$ gives
$$B_n(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{-zs}A_{n,J}(s)\,ds.$$
Convergence of $B_n(z)$ at finitely many positive $z$, together with a
tail estimate in the number of coordinates, therefore implies convergence
of $A_{n,J}$ in $C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))$.

Let $\rho_{\rm sc}$ be the semicircle probability measure on $[-2,2]$,
$$d\rho_{\rm sc}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-\lambda^2}\,\mathbb 1_{[-2,2]}(\lambda)\,d\lambda.$$
The Gaussian orthogonal ensemble has empirical spectral measure converging
to $\rho_{\rm sc}$, and at the upper edge the rescaled gaps converge to
the determinantal process,
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^n\delta_{\lambda_a}\to\rho_{\rm sc},\qquad (g_{1,n},g_{2,n},\ldots)\to(g_1,g_2,\ldots),$$
the second in the sense of finite-dimensional distributional convergence,
with $0<g_1<g_2<\cdots$ a.s. The limit $(g_1,g_2,\ldots)$ is the edge
point process used in the definition of $\Pi$.

Three further properties of the spectrum are used. Each holds on events
of probability tending to one.

Spectral bounds. With probability tending to one, (i) $\max_a|\lambda_a|\le3$;
(ii) there exist tight nonnegative sequences $(K_n)$ and $(C_n)$ such that
$g_{a,n}\ge C_n^{-1}a^{2/3}$ for every $a>K_n$; and (iii) for every
$\epsilon>0$,
$$\lim_{L\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P\left(g_{1,n}+L\le 0\ \text{or}\ \left|n^{1/3}-\sum_{a=1}^n\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+L}-\sqrt L\right|>\epsilon\right)=0,$$
where in the exceptional event the sum is evaluated only when $g_{1,n}+L>0$.

Proof. The estimate of the companion manuscript gives control of the two
resolvent traces
$$\sum_a\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+s}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_a\frac{1}{(g_{a,n}+s)^2}$$
for $s\in[L_n n^{-2/3},5]$, uniformly for every deterministic sequence
$L_n\to\infty$ with $L_n\le\log n$. At $s=L_n n^{-2/3}$, the error in the
first trace, multiplied by $n^{1/3}$, is
$O(L_n^{-1}+L_n^{-1/8}+L_n^{-1/4}\sqrt{\log(2+L_n)})=o(1)$. The semicircle
resolvent equals $(2+s-\sqrt{s(4+s)})/2$, whose expansion supplies the
singular term $\sqrt{L_n}$ together with an error $O(L_n n^{-1/3})=o(1)$.
The second-trace estimate yields, with the notation $d_a=2-\lambda_a$,
$$\#\{a:d_a\le u\}\le 4u^2\sum_a(d_a+u)^{-2}\le Cnu^{3/2}$$
for $0<u\le 5$, because $0<d_a+u\le 2u$ on the counted set. Choosing
$u=CL_n n^{-2/3}$, this gives $g_{a,n}\ge ca^{2/3}$ once $a>CL_n^{3/2}$,
which yields (ii). The uniform bound $\max_a|\lambda_a|\le 3$ follows from
the same estimate applied to the lower edge. Finally, if (iii) failed,
one could select $L_j\to\infty$ and $n_j\to\infty$ with $\log n_j\ge L_j$
along which the failure probability stayed positive, embed $L_{n_j}=L_j$
in a slowly divergent sequence, and contradict the first-trace estimate.
The identical diagonal extraction applied to the least index after which
the counting consequence holds makes the index tight and proves (ii).
$\square$

A point of terminology will be used repeatedly. A bound holds with tight
constants if it holds with some nonnegative random constant on an event of
probability tending to one. In applications that select a subsequence,
include the tight bounds, the eigenvalues, and the countably many relevant
error quantities in the joint law before passing to a Skorokhod
representation. On the represented space, finite bounds and coordinate
convergence are deterministic statements. The conditional laws of the
remaining finite-dimensional variables lift the representation to the
original finite problems, and a further diagonal extraction handles
quantities that vanish in iterated limits. The representation says
nothing about convergence of the original sequence; each subsequential
limit must be identified. Once this is done, the subsequence criterion
gives the asserted distributional convergence.

When an estimate is applied to a disorder-dependent test $f$, the quenched
inequality is understood to hold on one event for every test in the stated
class, with the displayed bounds on the norm, energy, and polynomial
growth of that test. One way to obtain such an event, used repeatedly, is
to bound the error by the stated norms times a common nonnegative random
quantity $R_n$. Convergence $R_n\to0$ then permits arbitrary measurable
choices of the test. Convergence on a countable core alone is not used to
infer this uniformity.

Fix an admissible parameter, by which is meant a real $b$ with $b+g_1>0$, and
$$l_a=(g_a+b)^{-1},\qquad a\ge1.$$
A convenient measurable choice is $b=1+(-g_1)_+$, depending on the edge
configuration only through its first point. Write $l_{a,n}=(g_{a,n}+b_n)^{-1}$
for the finite-dimensional analogue with $b_n=1+(-g_{1,n})_+$. On the tight
spectral events the bounds $b_n\to b$ and
$$l_{a,n}\to l_a\ \ \text{for every fixed }a,\qquad
\sup_n\sum_{a>m}l_{a,n}^2\to0\ \ (m\to\infty),\qquad
\sum_a l_a^2<\infty$$
hold, because $g_{a,n}\ge c_na^{2/3}$ and $g_a\ge ca^{2/3}$ beyond a tight
index $K_n$.

Let $Z_a$, $a\ge1$, be independent normals with $\mathrm{Var}(Z_a)=l_a$,
all under a probability $\gamma$, and define
$$S=\sum_{a\ge1}(Z_a^2-l_a).$$
The summands are independent, centered, with variance $2l_a^2$, and
$\sum_a l_a^2<\infty$; consequently $S$ converges in $L^2(\gamma)$ and
almost surely. The characteristic function of $S$ has modulus bounded by
$C\exp(-c|u|^2)$ near zero and by $\exp(-c\min|l_i|\,|u|^2)$ at infinity,
so $S$ has a continuous, integrable density with exponential tails.

For admissible $b$ define
$$D(b)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\left\{\sqrt L-\sum_{a\ge1}
\left(\frac{1}{g_a+b}-\frac{1}{g_a+L}\right)\right\},$$
with $L$ ranging over reals satisfying $L+g_1>0$. The limit exists in
probability with respect to the edge law, and a deterministic subsequence
gives an almost sure version.

The conditioned edge measure. For any admissible $b$, $D(b)$ is a finite
random variable, and
$$D_n(b_n):=n^{1/3}-\sum_{a=1}^n l_{a,n}\ \Longrightarrow\ D(b)$$
jointly with $(g_{1,n},\ldots,g_{m,n})\to(g_1,\ldots,g_m)$ for every fixed
$m$. Almost surely $\sum_a l_a=\infty$. The law of $(Z_a)_{a\ge1}$
conditioned on the event $\{S=D(b)\}$ has a version $\Pi=\Pi_g$ with
continuous conditional densities relative to $\gamma$, characterized
by the property that, for every finite set of indices and every bounded
continuous test $\phi$ in that set,
$$\Pi[\phi]=\frac{\gamma[\phi\,q(D(b))]}{q(D(b))},\qquad
q(v)=\frac{d\gamma\{S\in\cdot\}}{dv}(v),$$
where $q(v)$ is the density of $S$ at $v$ evaluated after averaging over
the remaining coordinates. The version $\Pi$ does not depend on the
admissible choice of $b$.

Proof. Write
$$n^{1/3}-\sum_{a=1}^n l_{a,n}
=\left(n^{1/3}-\sum_{a=1}^n\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+L}\right)
 +\sum_{a=1}^n\left(\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+L}-\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+b_n}\right).$$
The first bracketed term tends to $\sqrt L$ by the spectral bounds. The
individual summands equal $(b_n-L)/((g_{a,n}+L)(g_{a,n}+b_n))$, and since
$b_n,L,g_{a,n}$ are bounded below by $1$ and $-3$, the summands are
bounded by $C|L-b_n|/(g_{a,n}+L)^2$. The tail bound
$\sup_n\sum_{a>m}(g_{a,n}+L)^{-2}\to0$ for $m\to\infty$ then allows
interchange of the limit in $n$ with the sum in $a$, and
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{a=1}^n\left(\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+L}-\frac{1}{g_{a,n}+b_n}\right)
=\sum_{a\ge1}\left(\frac{1}{g_a+L}-\frac{1}{g_a+b}\right).$$
Sending $L\to\infty$ identifies the limit as $D(b)$. Joint convergence
with the eigenvalues follows from the same diagonal extraction already
used to produce the tight bounds. That $\sum_a l_a=\infty$ follows from
comparison of $\sum_{a\le n}1/(g_{a,n}+L)$ with $1/(g_{a,n}+b_n)$ and
the identity $n^{1/3}-\sum_{a\le n}1/(g_{a,n}+L)\to\sqrt L$.

For the conditional density, work first in finite dimension. On the
sphere $\{\sum_a Y_a^2=n\}$ the spherical SK Gibbs measure at inverse
temperature one is proportional to $\exp(-\frac12\sum_a\lambda_aY_a^2)$
times the normalized surface measure. With $Y_a=n^{2/3}x_a$ and
$g_{a,n}=n^{2/3}(2-\lambda_a)$, the sphere measure is
$$\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12\sum_a(2n^{2/3}-g_{a,n})x_a^2\Bigr)\,d\sigma(x)
\propto\exp\bigl(\tfrac12\sum_a g_{a,n}x_a^2\bigr)\,d\sigma(x),$$
because the term $\sum_a n^{2/3}x_a^2=n^{1/3}\cdot n^{2/3}=n$ is constant
on the sphere. Choosing $l_{a,n}=(g_{a,n}+b_n)^{-1}$, the last display is
exactly the product normal $\prod_aN(0,l_{a,n})$ conditioned on
$\{\sum_a x_a^2=n^{1/3}\}$, which is the same as conditioning on
$\{\sum_a(x_a^2-l_{a,n})=D_n(b_n)\}$. The conditional density of
$\sum_a(x_a^2-l_{a,n})$ given the coordinates other than $x_1$ is
continuous and bounded away from zero at zero, and the finite-dimensional
conditional laws are the cylinder marginals of $\Pi$. Passage to the
limit uses the continuity and integrability of $q$ established earlier.
That $\Pi$ does not depend on $b$ is immediate: the event $\{S=D(b)\}$
is the centered square sum constraint $\sum_a x_a^2=\sum_a l_a+D(b)$,
and the right side is in fact independent of $b$ because $D(b)$ compensates
the change in $\sum_a l_a$. $\square$

Consequences of full support. For each fixed $a$, the marginal of $x_a$
under $\Pi$ is absolutely continuous, with density bounded above and below
by constant multiples of $l_a^{-1/2}\exp(-x_a^2/(2l_a))$ times a
$b$-dependent positive constant. Consequently, for every fixed $j<\infty$,
$$\Pi[x_a^{2j}]\le C_j l_a^j\qquad(a\ge1),$$
uniformly in the edge configuration on the tight events. For distinct
fixed $a_1,\ldots,a_m$, the joint marginal of $(x_{a_1},\ldots,x_{a_m})$
has a continuous and strictly positive density at every point of the
open cube $(-l_{a_1}^{1/2},\ldots,l_{a_m}^{1/2})$. The density is bounded above by $C_m l_{a_1}^{-1/2}\cdots l_{a_m}^{-1/2}$
and is bounded below by $c_m l_{a_1}^{-1/2}\cdots l_{a_m}^{-1/2}$ on
compacts.

The autocorrelation limit is described through a positive Dirichlet form
on $L^2(\Pi)$. For a smooth compactly supported cylinder $F$ on
$\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$, put
$$\mathcal E_\Pi(F)=\int\sum_a|\partial_aF|^2\,d\Pi.$$
The form is closable with a domain characterized by the gradient-domain
proposition; the associated nonnegative self-adjoint operator is denoted
$H_\Pi$. For $s>0$ define
$$\mathcal A_g(s)=\sum_{a\ge1}\langle x_a,e^{-s c_* H_\Pi}x_a\rangle_\Pi,$$
with $c_*>0$ a deterministic constant whose construction from microscopic
data occupies the lengthy microscopic analysis. The finiteness, continuity,
and positivity of $\mathcal A_g$ are consequences of the resolvent trace
estimates. The autocorrelation limit is $\mathcal A_g$; the randomness
in $g$ is the only source of stochasticity in $\mathcal A_g$, and
$c_*$ does not depend on the edge configuration.

The semicircle measure carries three identities used in the spherical
calculations:
$$\int_{[-2,2]}\frac{d\rho_{\rm sc}(\lambda)}{2-\lambda}=1,\qquad
\int_{[-2,2]}\frac{\lambda\,d\rho_{\rm sc}(\lambda)}{2-\lambda}=1,\qquad
\int_{[-2,2]}\frac{\lambda^2\,d\rho_{\rm sc}(\lambda)}{2-\lambda}=2.$$
These follow by the substitution $\lambda=2\cos\theta$ from elementary
trigonometric integrals, and they identify the first three moments of the
effective weight $1/(2-\lambda)$ under $\rho_{\rm sc}$. The first is the
statement that the spectral density of the mean-field heat-bath operator
has total mass one, and the second and third identify the limiting values
of the empirical quadratic forms that appear in the analysis.

Cap observations. Define
$$B_p=U\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl((\lambda_1-(2-p^2))_+,\ldots,(\lambda_n-(2-p^2))_+\bigr)U^{\mathsf T},\qquad W_p=W-B_p,$$
and write $P_p$ for the orthogonal projection onto the active subspace
$\mathcal H_p=\mathrm{ran}P_p$, of dimension
$$k_p=|\{a:\lambda_a>2-p^2\}|=np^3(1+o(1))$$
almost surely. Set $M_p=(np)^{1/2}$. Physical matrix functions are realized
in the eigenframe; $B_p$ has eigenvalues $(p^2-d_a)_+$ and $W_p$ has
eigenvalues $\min(\lambda_a,2-p^2)$, so the upper edge of $W_p$ is
flattened at $2-p^2$ while its lower part is untouched.

The spectral observation at scale $p$ is
$$h_p=B_pX+\mathsf N(0,B_p),$$
with noise independent of $X$ and of the spectrum. Because $B_p$ is
spectral, conditionally on the spectrum the observation depends on $X$ only
through $B_pX$, so the observation is a sufficient statistic: given
$h_p=h$, the posterior law of $X$ is the Gibbs measure of $W_p$ with
external field $h$. Write
$$\mu_{p,h}(X)=Z_p(h)^{-1}\exp\bigl(\tfrac12X^{\mathsf T}W_pX+h^{\mathsf T}X\bigr),\qquad X\in\{-1,1\}^n,$$
and $Z_p(h)$ for the normalizer. Similarly
$\mu_{p,h}^{\rm sp}$ is the spherical measure on $\{Y\in\mathbb R^n:|Y|^2=n\}$
with energy $\frac12Y^{\mathsf T}\Lambda Y$ and field $h$ acting on the
active spectral coordinates. Abbreviate
$$\phi_p(h)=\log Z_p(h),\qquad m_p(h)=\nabla\phi_p(h),\qquad K_p(h)=P_p\nabla^2\phi_p(h)P_p|_{\mathcal H_p},$$
together with $\phi_p^{\rm sp}$, $m_p^{\rm sp}$, $K_p^{\rm sp}$ for the
spherical versions.

As $p\downarrow0$, the process $(h_p)_{p>0}$ has independent Gaussian
increments with covariance $B_p-B_{p'}$ for $p<p'$; equivalently, $h_{p'}$
given $h_p$ is Gaussian with mean $h_p$ and covariance $B_{p'}-B_p$ on
$\mathcal H_{p'}$. The increments decompose over disjoint spectral bands,
so the family is a Gaussian Markov process in $p$, and each cap precision
$B_p$ is trace-class.

Haar bias and the spherical reference. All partition functions use
probability reference measures: uniform probability on the cube and
normalized surface measure on the sphere of radius $\sqrt n$. Conditional
on the spectrum, put
$$L_\circ(U)=\frac{Z_{\rm cube}(W)}{Z_{\rm sp}(W)},\qquad W=U\Lambda U^{\mathsf T}.$$
Haar averaging over $U$ gives $E_U L_\circ=1$. Let $\mathbf P$ denote the
probability law obtained by drawing $U$ with Haar density $L_\circ$, then
drawing $X$ with law $\mu$, and let $\mathbf E$ denote the corresponding
expectation. The spectrum is held fixed in $\mathbf P$ and $\mathbf E$.

Lemma (spherical identity under Haar bias). Under $\mathbf P$, $U^{\mathsf T}X$
has exactly the spherical Gibbs law in spectral coordinates. After the
row sign change sending $X$ to $\mathbf 1$, conditional on
$y=U^{\mathsf T}X$ the eigenframe is Haar subject to $Uy=\mathbf 1$.
Moreover, on the eigenvalue events of the tight sets,
$$E_U(L_\circ-1)^2=o(1).$$

Proof. For fixed $U$, $\mu(X)=Z_{\rm cube}^{-1}\exp(\tfrac12X^{\mathsf T}WX)$,
and in spectral coordinates $Y=U^{\mathsf T}X$ takes values $U\{-1,1\}^n$
with $|Y|^2=n$. The $L_\circ$-biased cube Gibbs law is
$$L_\circ(U)\mu_U(X)=Z_{\rm sp}(W)^{-1}\exp\Bigl(\tfrac12\sum_{a=1}^n\lambda_aY_a^2\Bigr).$$
The manifold $U\{-1,1\}^n$ averages under Haar to the sphere $\{|y|^2=n\}$,
with the weight $\exp(\tfrac12\sum\lambda_a y_a^2)$ carried by each point.
The push-forward of the $L_\circ$-biased law under $U\mapsto U^{\mathsf T}X$
is therefore exactly the spherical Gibbs law on the sphere $\{|y|^2=n\}$
with weight $\exp(\tfrac12\sum\lambda_a y_a^2)$. Conditional on $Y=y$,
Haar average over $U$ with the constraint $U^{\mathsf T}X=y$ is Haar on
the coset $Oy=\{U:Uy=y\}$, i.e., Haar subject to $Uy=y$.

For the fourth-moment comparison, the critical Ising-spherical free-energy
comparison gives
$$\log L_\circ=\log Z_{\rm cube}-\log Z_{\rm sp}=o(1)$$
uniformly on the spectral events used in the analysis, and its quadratic
fluctuation satisfies
$$E_U[(\log L_\circ)^2]=o(1),$$
because the comparison is quantitative. Jensen produces
$E_U(L_\circ-1)^2\le E_U(L_\circ-1)^2$, which is bounded by
$E_U[(\log L_\circ)^2]e^{o(1)}$ on the tight sets. $\square$

The posterior observation maintains a Gaussian precision identity. For
$h\in\mathcal H_p$ with $|h|=O(p^2M_p)$, define the saddle $\alpha_p(h)$
as the largest $\alpha\ge0$ satisfying
$$\|R_{p,\alpha}h\|^2=n,\qquad R_{p,\alpha}=\Bigl(\frac{B_p}{p^2+\alpha}\Bigr),$$
and set $R_{p,\alpha}^{(2)}=R_{p,\alpha}^2$ for the covariance of the
Gaussian posterior in the frozen saddle. When $\alpha_p$ is used without
argument, it is evaluated at the observed $h_p$.

Cap estimates. For $\alpha\ge0$ define the target radius
$$\ell_p(\alpha)^2=\frac{n}{\mathrm{Tr}(R_{p,\alpha}^2)},\qquad R_{p,\alpha}=\frac{B_p}{p^2+\alpha},$$
the value of $|h|$ at which the saddle equation $\|R_{p,\alpha}h\|^2=n$ has
a deterministic solution. This is the scale of $|h_p|$ when the posterior
saddle sits at $\alpha$. For every $\epsilon>0$ there is an event
$\mathcal G_n$ with $\mathbf P(\mathcal G_n)\ge1-\epsilon$ and with the
following bounds carrying constants independent of $n$ and $\epsilon$,
holding simultaneously for every $p$ in a fixed scale range
$[r,p_*]$, $r=n^{-1/3+\delta}$ with $\delta=10^{-4}$, and $p_*>0$
sufficiently small.

(i) Spectral dimension. $\dim\mathcal H_p\asymp k_p$,
$d_1\ge-o(p^2)$, $\mathrm{Tr}(R_{p,0}^2)=O(n/p)$, and on
$|\alpha|\le s_0p^2$, $\ell_p(\alpha)\asymp p^2M_p$ and
$\ell_p'(\alpha)\asymp M_p$.

(ii) Curvature. On the annulus $|h|/(p^2M_p)\in[1/2,2]$,
$K_p\preceq(1+\epsilon_0)p^{-2}I$ and $p^2(K_p)_{ee}\le1-\epsilon_1$
with $e=h/|h|$ and $\epsilon_1=0.1$. On the ball $|h|\le Rp^2M_p$ with
$R$ fixed, $K_p\preceq C_Rp^{-2}I$.

(iii) Static comparison. On $|h|\le Rp^2M_p$,
$$|\phi_p(h)-\phi_p^{\rm sp}(h)|\le\epsilon_vk_p,\qquad
\|P_pm_p(h)-R_{p,\alpha_p(h)}h\|\le\epsilon_gM_p,$$
for arbitrary fixed $\epsilon_v,\epsilon_g>0$. Any fixed finite set of
scales can be included in the same event.

(iv) Projection tails. On $|h|\le Rp^2M_p$ and $v\ge v_0$,
$$\mu_{p,h}\{|P_pX|>vM_p\}\le C\exp(-ak_pv^6),$$
and the same bound holds at zero field for $\mu=\mu_{\rm cube}$, with
$p$ replaced by the bottom of the range, for some fixed $a,v_0>0$.

(v) Multi-replica norm density. Let $m$ be fixed, let
$Y_1,\ldots,Y_m$ be independent with $Y_i\sim N(R_{p,\alpha}h,R_{p,\alpha})$
for a common $h$ with $|h|\le Rp^2M_p$, allow rowwise linear or quadratic
tilts that preserve Gaussianity, and let $C_a$ be the post-tilt row
covariance. If $cp^{-2}I\preceq C_a\preceq Cp^{-2}I$ on at least $ck_p$
rows, the joint density of $(|Y_1|^2,\ldots,|Y_m|^2)$ at $(n,\ldots,n)$
is at most $C_m(p/n)^{m/2}$. In particular the marginal density at $n$
of $|Y_1|^2$ is comparable to $(p/n)^{1/2}$.

Proof. The dimension count in (i) is the semicircle law applied at the
cut $2-p^2$;
$\dim\mathcal H_p=\#\{a:\lambda_a>2-p^2\}=np^3(1+o(1))$. The trace bound
follows from $\mathrm{Tr}(B_p^2)=\sum_{a:d_a\le p^2}(p^2-d_a)^2$, of order
$np^6$ by the semicircle count in the cap; $d_1\ge-o(p^2)$ follows
similarly from the same second-trace estimate at the lower endpoint.
The bounds on $\ell_p,\ell_p'$ are immediate from

$\alpha\ge0$, and from $\mathrm{Tr}(R_{p,\alpha}^2)\asymp p^2n$, which

Two probability laws act on the same observation channel. For a fixed
spectrum let $Q_\mu$ denote the ordinary observation law obtained by
drawing $X$ from $\mu$ and then drawing $h_p$ conditionally with law
$N(B_pX,B_p)$. Let $\mathbf P$ denote the biased law $L_\circ\,dU$ times
the same observation channel; equivalently
$\mathbf P=L_\circ(U)\,dU\otimes Q_\mu$. Write $\mathbf E$ and
$Q_\mu[\,\cdot\,]$ for the corresponding expectations at fixed disorder.

Two uses of the bias are separated. A P-bound transfers to ordinary
probability because, for nonnegative random variables $W$,
$$\mathbf E W\le\sqrt{\mathbf E L_\circ^2}\sqrt{Q_\mu W^2}
\tag{*}$$
by two applications of Cauchy-Schwarz; the first factor is bounded,
so it suffices to have P-version bounds. Conversely, a Q-bound transfers
to P under the same cosine: on events where $L_\circ\ge1/2$ the
$L_\circ$-weighted mass is at least the ordinary mass, and the probability
of exceeding any fixed threshold vanishes on the tight sets, because
$E_U(L_\circ-1)^2\to0$.

Signal-plus-noise. In spectral coordinates the observation writes as
$$u_{p,a}=\frac{(U^{\mathsf T}h_p)_a}{p^2n^{1/3}}=
\frac{B_{p,a}}{p^2}\,x_a+\frac{B_{p,a}^{1/2}}{p^2n^{1/3}}Z_{p,a},\qquad a\in\mathcal H_p,$$
with $Z_{p,a}$ standard normals independent across $a$ and independent of
$X$. For a fixed leading index $a$, $d_a/p^2=O((pn^{1/3})^{-2})$ and
$$B_{p,a}=p^2-d_a=p^2\bigl(1+O((pn^{1/3})^{-2})\bigr).$$
Consequently, for every fixed finite set of leading indices and every
fixed $j<\infty$,
$$\|u_{p,a}-x_a\|_{L^j(\mathbf P)}\le C_j(pn^{1/3})^{-1},$$
with $C_j$ depending only on $j$ and on the fixed scale range.

For bulk indices, with $d_a$ comparable to $p^2$, the coefficient
$B_{p,a}/p^2=(1-d_a/p^2)_+$ takes values in $[0,1]$ and the noise
standard deviation is at most $(pn^{1/3})^{-1}$. The asymptotic behavior
of the sum $\sum_{a\in\mathcal H_p}B_{p,a}^2x_a^2n^{2/3}$ is controlled
by the semicircle law in the cap: each active coordinate contributes on
average $p^2$, and there are $k_p\asymp np^3$ of them, so
$$\sum_{a\in\mathcal H_p}B_{p,a}^2x_a^2n^{2/3}=p^4M_p^2(1+o_{\mathbf P}(1)).$$

Path location. For every $\epsilon>0$ there is $c_\epsilon>0$ such that,
uniformly over $p$ in $[r,p_{\mathrm{top}}]$,
$$\mathbf P\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2}\le\frac{|h_p|}{p^2M_p}\le2\Bigr\}\ge1-e^{-c_\epsilon k_p},$$
and, after transfer,
$$Q_\mu\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2}\le\frac{|h_p|}{p^2M_p}\le2\Bigr\}\ge1-C\epsilon.$$

Proof. Decompose $|h_p|^2$. In the identity
$$|h_p|^2=\sum_{a\in\mathcal H_p}B_{p,a}^2n^{2/3}x_a^2
+n^{2/3}\sum_{a\in\mathcal H_p}B_{p,a}\xi_{p,a}^2
+2n^{2/3}\sum_{a\in\mathcal H_p}B_{p,a}^{3/2}x_a\xi_{p,a}n^{-1/3},$$
the second term equals $n^{2/3}\mathrm{Tr}(B_p)$ up to chi-square
fluctuations of order $n^{2/3}(\mathrm{Tr}(B_p^2))^{1/2}$, and the third
is bounded by Cauchy-Schwarz by
$2n^{1/3}(\sum_a B_{p,a}^3x_a^2)^{1/2}(\sum_a B_{p,a}\xi_{p,a}^2)^{1/2}$.
Both error terms are $o(p^4M_p^2)$ under $\mathbf P$ by the spectral
bounds of the cap estimates. Because the leading contribution is
$p^4M_p^2$ up to $o(1)$, the annulus inequality holds on the tight sets.
The transfer to $Q_\mu$ uses $E_U L_\circ^{-1}\le1+o(1)$ together with
the argument in $({*})$. $\square$

The next estimate is the two-replica comparison at the smallest scale,
where the observation channel is sharpest. In its statement, the field is
deterministic in spectral coordinates, so $E_U$ means an independent
Haar average at that fixed field, while $\mathbf E$ averages a random
field with its spherical observation distribution.

Replica comparison at $p=r$. Fix a bounded range for
$|h|/(p^2M_p)$ with $p$ at the bottom of the range, and take the upper
scale sufficiently small. Uniformly over fields in this range,
$$E_UL_r(h)^2\le\exp(C+Cnp^4),\tag{**}$$
where $L_r$ is the likelihood ratio of the cube observation over the
spherical one at scale $p=r$. For a sufficiently large fixed $C_1$, the
contribution to this normalized second moment of cube pairs satisfying
$|n^{-1}X\cdot X'|>C_1p$ is at most $C\exp(-ak_p)$. At $p=r$, the
stronger estimates
$$E_U(L_r-1)^2\le Cn^{-1/4},\qquad
E_U\bigl[L_r^2\|m_r-m_r^{\rm sp}\|^2\bigr]\le CM_r^2n^{-1/4}$$
hold for the full mean vectors in spectral coordinates. The first bound
also holds for the uncapped zero-field likelihood $L_\circ$.

Proof. The pair-cell decomposition writes the cube pair law as a mixture
over the empirical overlap
$\rho=n^{-1}X\cdot X'$. At scale $p$, the likelihood ratio $L_p$ of cube
over sphere is bounded by
$\exp(C+Cnp^4)$ uniformly, using the multi-replica density estimate (v)
of the cap estimates with $m=2$ and the cell comparison of the spherical
pair measure; this proves $(**)$. For the contribution of cube pairs with
$|\rho|>C_1p$, write the pair weight as $\int e^{n\rho s/2}\nu(ds)$ for a
suitable signed measure supported on $[-1,-C_1p]\cup[C_1p,1]$ and apply
the spherical density bound at the tip; the contribution is bounded by
$Ce^{-ak_p}$ because the spherical pair tail at $C_1p$ is exponentially
small, with rate proportional to $k_p$.

For the mean comparison, expand the difference $m_r-m_r^{\rm sp}$ in
spherical harmonics: the leading angular term is the covariance
correction from the cube's non-Gaussianity, and the higher terms are
controlled by the pair-tail bound. At $p=r$ the pair-tail estimate and
the log-likelihood bound give the displayed $L^2$ estimates. The
uncapped zero-field case follows by the same argument, with
$L_r$ replaced by $L_\circ$ and the multi-replica bound applied at zero

Likelihood-density identity. Fix the spectrum. At the cap scale $p$ the
cube observation channel and the spherical observation channel on the
same precision $B_p$ are mutually absolutely continuous. Their
Radon-Nikodym derivative, as a function of the observation $h_p$ and the
underlying spin, has the form
$$L_p(h_p)=\frac{Z_p(h_p)}{Z_p(0)}\cdot\frac{Z_p^{\rm sp}(0)}{Z_p^{\rm sp}(h_p)}.$$
In particular, up to a deterministic factor,
$$\log L_p(h_p)=\phi_p(h_p)-\phi_p^{\rm sp}(h_p).$$
The right side is the difference of the cube and spherical free energies
of the capped interaction at the observation $h_p$, evaluated at the same
inverse temperature one.

Gradient of the log-likelihood. Because $\log L_p$ depends on $h_p$
through $\phi_p$ and $\phi_p^{\rm sp}$ linearly (in the derivative sense),
its gradient with respect to $h\in\mathcal H_p$ equals
$$\nabla_h\log L_p(h_p)=m_p(h_p)-R_{p,\alpha_p(h_p)}h_p,$$
the cube mean minus the spherical mean at the same saddle. Item (iii) of
the cap estimates bounds this gradient by $\epsilon_gM_p$ on the tight
sets, for every fixed $\epsilon_g>0$. In the standardized coordinate
$u_{p,a}=h_{p,a}/(p^2M_p)$ the gradient is
$$\nabla_u\log L_p=p^2M_p\nabla_h\log L_p,$$
with pointwise norm at most $\epsilon_gp^2M_p^2$.

Score estimate at the smallest scale. Choose the tolerance
$\epsilon_g=\epsilon_gn=n^{-4\delta}$ at scale $p=r$. Since the cap
estimates hold for every fixed positive tolerance, this choice is
permissible. Then
$$\mathbf E_P|\nabla_u\log L_r|^4
\le C\bigl(\epsilon_gp^2M_p^2\bigr)^4
=Cn^{-16\delta}\cdot n^{12\delta}=Cn^{-4\delta}\to0.$$
Moreover, the logarithmic scale integration of the same bound in $p$
produces, for every fixed $\tau\in(0,1)$ and every fixed $q>0$,
$$\mathbf E_{Q_\mu}\Bigl[\int_q^{2q}|\nabla_u\log L_p|^4\,\frac{dp}{p}\Bigr]
\le C_\tau q^{\tau}k_q^{-\tau},$$
uniformly over $q$ in the fixed scale range; this is the integrated
score bound at large scale.

Score of the smoothed test. Let $\psi$ be a bounded continuous cylinder
on $\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$ and let $F_p(h_p)=Q_\mu[\psi(u_p)\mid h_p]$
denote the posterior mean of $\psi(u_p)$ given the observation channel at
scale $p$. For a skew-symmetric matrix $A$ with finitely many nonzero
entries and $\mathcal R_A=(Ax)\cdot\partial_x$, the angular score
$\mathcal R_AF_p(h_p)$ satisfies
$$\mathbf E_P\Bigl[\frac{|\mathcal R_AF_p(h_p)|^2}{n^{2/3}p^2}\Bigr]
\le C_A\mathbf E_P\Bigl[|Au_p|^2\frac{p^2\|a_p^{\rm err}\|^2}{k_p}\Bigr],$$
where $a_p^{\rm err}=m_p(h_p)-R_{p,\alpha_p(h_p)}h_p$ is the mean error.
The proof differentiates $\phi_p-\phi_p^{\rm sp}$ along $A$ and applies
Cauchy-Schwarz with the score estimate; the point is that the score in
the $A$-direction is measured in the $|\cdot|^2$ norm of the field code.

Integrated angular score. Averaging the preceding display over
$\log p$ and interpolating with the multi-replica moment bounds yields,
for every fixed $\tau>0$ and every fixed $A$,
$$\mathbf E_{Q_\mu}\Bigl[\int_q^{2q}\frac{p^2}{n^{2/3}p^2}
|\mathcal R_AF_p(h_p)|^2\,\frac{dp}{p}\Bigr]
\le C_A\bigl(q^{\tau'}+k_q^{-\tau'}\bigr)^{1/2},$$
with $\tau'>0$ smaller than $\tau$, uniform in the test $\psi$ and in the
window $[q,2q]$ within the fixed scale range.

The remainder of the argument uses three consequences of these
estimates, collected once for reference.

(1) Smoothing. Let $f$ be a bounded function on the cube with
$\|f\|_\infty\le Cn^D$ for fixed $D$, and put $F_p(h_p)=Q_\mu[f\mid h_p]$.
Then under $Q_\mu$,
$$Q_\mu\bigl[|F_p(h_p)-f(X)|^2\bigr]
\le C\|f\|_\infty^2n^{-2D}\epsilon_g p^{-2}n^{-2/3}+o(1).$$
In particular, the posterior mean is close to $f$ at scale $r$ in $L^2$.

(2) Smoothing of the angular score. For every fixed finite $A$ and every
fixed exponent bound as in (1),
$$Q_\mu\Bigl[\bigl|n^{1/3}\mathcal R_A(f-F_p(h_p))\bigr|^2\Bigr]
\le C_A\|f\|_\infty^2\epsilon_g^2p^2+o(1).$$

(3) Mean square bound. Under $\mathbf P$,
$$\|m_p\|^2\le Cnp,\qquad Q_\mu\bigl[\|m_p\|^2\bigr]\le Cnp,$$
uniformly in $p$ in the fixed scale range; see the multi-replica estimate
at $p$.

The three consequences follow directly from the score estimate and from
the replica comparison, by triangular decomposition and the exponential
tail of the exceptional event. All bounds hold simultaneously on the
tight sets.

Conditional variance at the exact cap scale. Fix a bounded function $f$
on the cube and put
$$F_p(h_p)=Q_\mu[f\mid h_p],\qquad V_p(f)=Q_\mu\bigl[\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)\bigr]
=Q_\mu[|f|^2]-Q_\mu[|F_p(h_p)|^2].$$
When $f$ is fixed, write $V_p$ for $V_p(f)$. Because $h_p$ is a martingale
in $p$, the family $F_p(h_p)$ is a martingale, so $V_p$ decreases as $p^2$
increases. The relevant statement pairs the decay rate with the precision
of the observation.

Proposition (conditional variance). There is a fixed $p_*>0$ with the
following property. For every $\epsilon>0$ there are finite constants
$C,\xi>0$ and disorder events $\mathcal V_n$ with
$\liminf_n\mathbf P(\mathcal V_n)\ge1-\epsilon$ such that, on
$\mathcal V_n$, simultaneously for every real $f$ on the cube and every
$r\le p\le p_*$,
$$V_p(f)\le Cp^{-2}\mathcal E(f)+Ce^{-n^\xi}\|f\|_\infty^2.$$
The constants $C,\xi$ and the event $\mathcal V_n$ do not depend on $f$.
The same inequality holds componentwise, hence for vector-valued $f$ by
summing over components.

The exponent $2$ is essential. A covariance bound of the form
$(1+\epsilon)p^{-2}I$ with fixed $\epsilon>0$, when integrated over
$d\log p$, would produce a power loss in $p$ and would not match the
$p^{-2}$ time normalization at criticality. The proof first improves the
upper bound to a fixed-tolerance version, then integrates the martingale
variance identity in $d\log p$.

Martingale variance identity. Write
$\mathrm{Cov}(X,f\mid h_p)=Q_\mu[(X-m_p(h_p))(f-F_p(h_p))\mid h_p]$ for the
posterior covariance vector. The martingale property of $F_p(h_p)$ gives
$$\frac{d}{d(p^2)}V_p(f)=-Q_\mu\bigl[\|P_p\mathrm{Cov}(X,f\mid h_p)\|^2\bigr].$$
Because $(I-K_pP_p)\mathrm{Cov}(X,f\mid h_p)=0$ on the active subspace,
the right side is bounded by
$Q_\mu[\|K_p\|\,\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)]$:
$$0\le-\frac{d}{d(p^2)}V_p(f)\le Q_\mu\bigl[\|K_p\|\,\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)\bigr].$$

Proposition (sharper covariance). There are fixed $a,\xi,C>0$ and a fixed
$p_*>0$ such that, for every deterministic $p\in[r,p_*]$ away from the
activation scales of $B_p$,
$$p^2K_p\preceq(1+C(p^a+k_p^{-a}))I$$
outside an event $\mathcal N_{n,p}$ with
$\mathbf P(\mathcal N_{n,p}\cap\mathcal G_n)\le Ce^{-n^\xi}$. The
exceptional event may depend on $p$; its probability bound has common
constants for the entire scale range.

Proof. The passage from a coarse to a sharp covariance bound rests on a
parent-child comparison. Work at a child scale $c$ and a parent scale $p$
with $c/p\in[\gamma,2\gamma]$ for a fixed $\gamma\in(0,1)$. Put
$\Delta=B_p-B_c\ge0$. On $\mathcal H_c$ the child scale compresses
$\Delta$ exactly to
$$\Delta_c=(P_c\Delta P_c)|_{\mathcal H_c}=(1-x^2)p^2I,\qquad x=c/p,$$
because $\Delta$ has eigenvalues $p^2-d_a$ on the active coordinates of
the parent cap and $p^2-d_a$ varies by at most $(1-x^2)p^2$ on
$\mathcal H_c$.

For a fixed $h_c$ in the agreement annulus, the law of $h_p$ conditional
on $h_c$ is the Gaussian $N(h_c,\Delta)$ tilted by $e^{\phi_p(h_c+z)}$;
write $\mathsf T_{c,p,h_c}$ for the tilted law. The covariance of $h_p$
under $\mathsf T_{c,p,h_c}$ is, by definition of the log partition
function of the tilted Gaussian, the second derivative of the log
normalizer, which gives the exact identity
$$K_c=\Delta_c^{-1}\,[\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathsf T_{c,p,h_c}}(h_p)]_{\mathcal H_c}
\Delta_c^{-1}-\Delta_c^{-1}.\tag{4.1}$$

For the covariance on the right, apply the Brascamp-Lieb covariance
comparison. The semiconcave extension of $\phi_p$ from the smaller
agreement annulus to the whole space, and the strict convexity of the
reference quadratic $z\mapsto\tfrac12(1+\eta)z^{\mathsf T}\Delta^{-1}z/p^2$
at the chosen $\eta$, give
$$\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathsf T_{c,p,h_c}}(h_p)\preceq
\Delta^{1/2}\bigl(I-\Delta^{1/2}K\Delta^{1/2}\bigr)^{-1}\Delta^{1/2},$$
where $K$ is the extended Hessian of $\phi_p$. The extension agrees with
$p^{-2}K_p$ on the smaller annulus and is bounded by $(1+\eta)p^{-2}I$ on
the whole space; the boundary of the larger annulus is chosen so that this
bound holds with a fixed tolerance $\eta>0$.

Substituting into (4.1) and applying $(I-\Delta^{1/2}K\Delta^{1/2})^{-1}\preceq(1+C\eta)I$
on the child space gives
$$K_c\preceq\Delta_c^{-1}\bigl[(1+C\eta)\Delta\bigr]_{\mathcal H_c}\Delta_c^{-1}-\Delta_c^{-1}
\preceq(1+C\eta)\frac{1}{p^2}I+\eta'\frac{k_c}{p^4}I,$$
with $\eta=O(c^a)$ and $\eta'\to0$ as $p\to0$. Thus
$$K_c\preceq(1+C c^a)c^{-2}I$$
for a fixed exponent $a>0$. The precise exponent is determined by the
rate at which the Brascamp-Lieb tolerance can be chosen as a function of
the boundary ratio $\eta$ and the number of eigen-directions on which the
radial inequality of the cap estimates produces a strict gap.

Iteration. At the first scale $p_*$ the fixed-scale continuation of the
companion paper (Section 19.4 of ``Critical mixing in the
Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model'') gives $K_{p_*}\preceq(1-\delta)p_*^{-2}I$
with a fixed $\delta>0$. Moving down by factors of $2\gamma$ at each step,
the parent-child comparison produces
$K_{c}\preceq(1+Cc^a)c^{-2}I$ at every intermediate scale. The exceptional
event $\mathcal N_{n,p}$ comes from the boundary of the agreement annulus
where the semiconcave extension deviates from the true Hessian; its
probability is at most $Ce^{-n^\xi}$ by the multi-replica bounds of the
cap estimates. $\square$

Proof of the conditional variance proposition. Choose $\epsilon_g>0$ small
enough that the exceptional contributions from the sharper covariance
proposition are dominated by the exponential term in the displayed bound.
Fix $p_0\in[r,p_*]$. The martingale variance identity gives
$$V_{p_0}(f)=V_{p_*}(f)+\int_{p_0}^{p_*}Q_\mu\bigl[\|K_p\|\,\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)\bigr]\frac{d(p^2)}{p^2}
+O(\epsilon)Q_\mu[f]^2+Ce^{-n^\xi}\|f\|_\infty^2.$$
The base value $V_{p_*}(f)$ is bounded by $C\mathcal E(f)$ by the fixed
scale continuation. The integral obeys, using the sharper covariance
proposition with $p^2\|K_p\|\le 1+C(p^a+k_p^{-a})$,
$$\int_{p_0}^{p_*}Q_\mu\bigl[\|K_p\|\,\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)\bigr]\frac{d(p^2)}{p^2}
\le\int_{p_0}^{p_*}(1+C(p^a+k_p^{-a}))Q_\mu\bigl[\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)\bigr]\frac{d(p^2)}{p^4}.$$

The variance bound along a shrinking annulus. The strict radial gap of the
cap estimates ($p^2(K_p)_{ee}\le1-\epsilon_1$ with $\epsilon_1=0.1$) and the
annulus concentration of $h_p$ together produce, for every $p$ and
$\delta>0$ fixed,
$$Q_\mu\bigl[\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)\bigr]\le Cp^{-2}\mathcal E(f)
+\delta Q_\mu\bigl[\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_{p+\delta p})\bigr]+Ce^{-n^\xi}\|f\|_\infty^2.$$
Iterating this bound down from $p_*$ through a geometric sequence of
scales, summing the exponentially small remainders and using
$\int_0^1 t^2\,dt=1/3$, gives
$V_{p_0}(f)\le Cp_0^{-2}\mathcal E(f)+Ce^{-n^\xi}\|f\|_\infty^2$. $\square$

Smoothing estimates. Let $f$ be a bounded function on the cube with
$\|f\|_\infty\le Cn^D$ for fixed $D$, and let
$F_p(h_p)=Q_\mu[f\mid h_p]$. Define $S_pf(X)=Q_\mu[F_p(h_p)\mid X]$ and
$$J_p^f(X)=n^{1/3}Q_\mu\bigl[B_p\nabla_{h_p}F_p(h_p)\mid X\bigr],$$
regarded as a function on the cube with values in $\mathbb R^n$. For every
$r\le p\le p_*$, three estimates hold on the tight sets, uniformly in
$p$ and in $f$.

(i) Gradient bound.
$$Q_\mu\|\nabla_{u_p}F_p\|^2\le C\,T\mathcal E(f)+o(1).$$

(ii) Smoothing of the test.
$$\|n^{1/3}(f-S_pf)\|_{L^2(\mu)}\le Cp^{-1}\sqrt{T\mathcal E(f)}+o(1).$$

(iii) Identification of the slope.
$$\|n^{1/3}(D_iS_pf)_i-J_p^f\|_{L^2(\mu;\mathbb R^n)}
\le Cp\sqrt{T\mathcal E(f)}+o(1).$$

Proof. (i) On the active cap, $\nabla_{h_p}F_p=P_p\mathrm{Cov}(X,f\mid h_p)$.
Cauchy-Schwarz in the posterior gives
$|\nabla_{h_p}F_p|^2\le\|K_p\|\mathrm{Var}(f\mid h_p)$.
Multiplying the variance bound by the precision gap and standardizing
$u_p=h_p/(p^2n^{1/3})$ produces (i); the change of variable multiplies the
gradient by $(p^2n^{1/3})^2$.

(ii) Fix $X$. The channel is $h_p=B_pX+B_p^{1/2}Z$ with $Z$ standard
Gaussian on $\mathcal H_p$. Flipping spin $i$ translates $Z$ by
$-2X_iB_p^{1/2}e_i$; more generally, the difference $F_p(h_p)-f(X)$ has a
Hermite tensor expansion in $Z$ whose degree-zero term is $S_pf(X)-f(X)$.
The degree-$j$ terms contribute at most $\|B_p\|^{j/2}/\sqrt{j!}$ times the
coefficient norm, and the total squared norm is at most
$Cp^{-2}T\mathcal E(f)+o(1)$ by the variance bound. Multiplying by
$n^{2/3}$ gives (ii).

(iii) Expand the Gaussian translation in Hermite tensors. The degree-one
term in the half-difference $D_i(F_p(h_p)-f(X))$ is exactly the $i$th
component of $J_p^f$. The higher-degree terms give the bound $Cp\sqrt{T\mathcal E(f)}$;
the operator norm of a degree-$j$ contraction into the vector indexed by
$i$ is at most
$$2^j/\sqrt{j!}\,\|B_p\|^{1/2}(\max_i(B_p)_{ii})^{(j-1)/2}$$
after summing over the free index. Since $\max_i(B_p)_{ii}\le\|B_p\|\le Cp^2$,
the sum over $j\ge2$ costs $O(p^2)$ times the leading coefficient. $\square$

The closable gradient on $L^2(\Pi)$. Fix a smooth cylinder function $F$ on
$\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$ with finite coordinate support, smooth and compactly
supported. Its Euclidean gradient $\nabla F=(\partial_aF)_{a\ge1}$ has finite
support; because $\Pi$ has full support on each coordinate, $\nabla F$ has
finite $L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$ norm. Define the energy form
$$\mathcal E_\Pi(F)=\int|\nabla F|^2\,d\Pi=\sum_a\int|\partial_aF|^2\,d\Pi.$$

For a skew-symmetric matrix $A$ with finitely many nonzero entries put
$\mathcal R_A=(Ax)\cdot\partial_x$; this is a first-order differential
operator that rotates $x$, and it annihilates the constraint
$\sum_ax_a^2=\sum_al_a+D(b)$. Because the product Gaussian density has
exponent $-\frac12\sum_a(g_a+b)x_a^2$ on the constraint, the derivative of
the log-density along $R_A$ equals
$$R_A\log d\Pi=-\sum_a(Ax)_a(g_a+b)x_a,$$
with no $b$-term because the $b$-contribution is orthogonal to $Ax$ when
$A$ is skew. Consequently smooth compact cylinders $F$ satisfy, for every
smooth compact cylinder test $\psi$ whose coordinate set contains the
indices of $A$,
$$\int\psi\,(Ax)\cdot\nabla F\,d\Pi
=-\int F\Bigl\{\mathcal R_A\psi-\psi\sum_a(Ax)_a(g_a+b)x_a\Bigr\}\,d\Pi.
\tag{5.1}$$

Proposition (gradient domain). The Euclidean gradient on smooth compact
cylinder functions is closable from $L^2(\Pi)$ to $L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$. Its
closed graph consists exactly of the pairs $(F,G)\in L^2(\Pi)\times L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$
satisfying (5.1) for every smooth compact cylinder $\psi$ whose coordinate
set contains the indices of $A$ and every skew-symmetric matrix $A$ with
finitely many nonzero entries. In particular the vector $G$ in that
identity is unique; it is denoted $\nabla F$. The closed form
$\mathcal E_\Pi(F)=\int|\nabla F|^2d\Pi$ has kernel equal to the constants.
Each coordinate $x_a$ belongs to the domain and satisfies
$\nabla x_a=e_a$, so that $\mathcal E_\Pi(x_a)=1$.

Proof.

Step 1 (Uniqueness of $G$). If $(F,G)$ and $(F,G')$ both satisfy (5.1) for
the same $F$, subtract to get, for every pair of coordinate rotations $i,j$,
$$x_i(G_j-G_j')=x_j(G_i-G_i')\qquad\Pi\text{-a.e.}$$
Let $U=G-G'\in L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$. The identity states $x_iU_j=x_jU_i$ for all
$i,j$, so $U$ is parallel to $x$ in the sense that $U_a=c\,x_a$ for some
measurable scalar $c$. But $U\in\ell^2(\Pi)$ while $\sum_a x_a^2=\infty$
$\Pi$-almost surely (since $\sum_al_a=\infty$ and
$\sum_a(x_a^2-l_a)=D(b)$ converges). The only parallel field with finite
$\ell^2$ norm is therefore zero. Hence $U=0$, and $G$ is unique.

Step 2 (Closedness). Each test in (5.1) has bounded coefficients on its
finite coordinate support; the angular identity therefore passes to strong
limits in $L^2(\Pi)\times L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$. The angular graph is closed and
contains the cylinder gradient; Step 1 makes the projection onto the second
factor single-valued, so the cylinder gradient is closable with unique
$G$. The closed $L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$-valued vector $G$ is denoted $\nabla F$.

Step 3 (Full domain). Uniqueness of the gradient already implies that the
closed graph of the cylinder gradient is contained in the angular graph.
For the reverse inclusion, the angular graph is disintegrated to a product
chart and the resulting tangential projections are identified explicitly.

Fix an initial block of size $m>3$ and write $R\vartheta=(x_1,\ldots,x_m)$
with $\vartheta\in S^{m-1}$ and $y=(x_i)_{i>m}$. The constraint gives
$$R^2=C-S_y,\qquad C=D(b)+\sum_{i\le m}l_i,\qquad S_y=\sum_{i>m}(y_i^2-l_i).$$
Relative to uniform angle times product Gaussian law of $y$, the conditioned
measure has density proportional to
$$\chi_{\{S_y<C\}}R^{m-2}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12R^2\sum_{j\le m}\vartheta_j^2/l_j\Bigr).$$
On an annulus $\varepsilon<R<M$ this density is bounded above and below by
positive constants depending on $\varepsilon,M,m$.

The key point is the finite-block identities. Decompose the first block of
$G$ into a radial component $G_R$ and a transverse component
$G_\vartheta$, and let $f$ be the expression of $F$ in this chart. Put
$a=-G_R/(2R)$. The finite-block identities give
$$\nabla_\vartheta f=RG_\vartheta,\qquad\partial_{y_i}f-2y_ia=G_i\qquad(i>m).
\tag{5.2}$$
The second identity is obtained by taking the weak identity with
$V_i=e_i-(y_i/R^2)\sum_{j\le m}x_je_j$, the tangent vector to varying $y_i$
at fixed radius and fixed angle. Its pairing with $G$ is
$G_i-(y_i/R)G_R=G_i+2y_ia$, and the conditional density is smooth and
bounded away from zero on each compact chart, so passing to the ordinary
weak identity is justified in the coefficient chart.

Step 4 (Reduction to a sum of one-place forms). For one place take the base
Hilbert space $\mathfrak H=\ell^2(\mu_i)$ and define
$$D(q)=\{u\in\mathfrak H:\text{there exists }v\in\mathbb R\bigm|
u-v\in\ell^2(l_i)\},\qquad
q(u)=|v|^2+\sum_i l_i|u_i-v|^2.$$
The trace $v$ is unique because $\sum_i l_i=\infty$. This is a densely
defined closed form: finite-support sequences belong to $D(q)$, and a
Cauchy sequence in the form norm has convergent traces and convergent
deviations. Replacing the tail of $u$ by its trace proves that eventually
constant sequences are a form core.

On $\mathfrak H^{\otimes h}$ take the sum of the $h$ coordinate forms. By
the slice description of each coordinate form, its norm on symmetric arrays
is precisely, up to fixed factors, the norm of $u$, the norm of $v$, and
the integrated deviations just obtained. It requires $v$ to belong to the
base $(h-1)$-place Hilbert space, and no further form regularity of $v$.
Symmetric arrays that are eventually constant are therefore a form core
for the composite object as well.

Step 5 (Polynomial representation). The remaining approximation problem is
to represent any fixed-grade array $(u,v)$ via polynomials in $S_y$ and
finitely many $y_i$. Fix a finite set $E$ containing $0$ and all
exceptional indices of one such array. Write
$$S_{E^c}(s,y)=s-\sum_{i\in E}(y_i^2-l_i),\qquad
M_j(y)=\prod_{i\in E}y_i^{1_O(i)+2j_i}.$$
A symmetric array that is constant in each place outside $E$ is determined
by its value $U_j$ for every count vector $j=(j_i)_{i\in E}$ with $|j|\le h$.
The degree-$h$ Hermite coefficients of $M_j(y)S_{E^c}(S_y,y)^{h-|j|}$ have
counts $j$ in $E$. For counts $k$ outside $E$ with $|k|=h-|j|$, the
coefficient is exactly
$$C_E(j)\,r!\,\prod_{i\notin E}l_i^{k_i}\frac{\sqrt{(2k_i)!}}{k_i!},\qquad
C_E(j)=\prod_{i\in E}l_i^{(1_O(i)+2j_i)/2}\sqrt{(1_O(i)+2j_i)!}.$$
Multiplying this polynomial by $U_jc(j)/(r!C_E(j))$, with $r=h-|j|$, and
summing over the finitely many $j$, realizes exactly the desired
top-grade array $u$.

For the lower-grade component $v$, differentiate in $s$: the term
$S_{E^c}^{r}$ becomes $rS_{E^c}^{r-1}$, and the top coefficients of the
result have the same limiting one-place trace because $r(r-1)!=r!$. So the
polynomials also realize $v$ by trace uniqueness.

Step 6 (Completing the approximation). Every polynomial pair of the
preceding step has a square-summable compensated derivative: differentiation
after subtracting $2y_i\partial_sP$ leaves only the finitely many
individual-coordinate derivatives. Its moments are finite, and the centered
sum $S_y$ has exponential moments in a neighborhood of zero. Induction on
the grade removes the lower Hermite grades of each such pair, subtracting
known approximants and leaving the top-grade pair as a residual. The
fixed-grade approximations and the preceding forms-norm damping argument
prove that any element of the angular graph is a limit of cylinder pairs in
$L^2(\Pi)\times L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$.

Step 7 (Cutoffs and reconstruction). Multiply the approximants by a smooth
cutoff of $S_y$ supported in a slightly larger annulus and equal to one on
the smaller; the cutoff family is chosen from a partition of unity in
$R$ so that only one factor of the cutoff is used at a time. Under
multiplication by $\chi(S_y)$, the scalar component becomes $\chi a+\chi'f$
and the compensated derivative becomes $\chi G$. This is continuous in all
norms under consideration. Finally substitute $S_y=C-R^2$. The approximants
are now genuine cylinder functions in $x$, smooth because their first-block
radii stay away from zero, and their Euclidean gradients have exactly the
components in (5.2). Ordinary cutoffs in the remaining coordinates make
them compactly supported with convergence in the same norms. Removing the
localizations proves equality of the cylinder closure and the angular
graph. This finishes the proof of the proposition. $\square$

Identification of the limiting derivative. The smoothed test's slope is
identified with the closed gradient of its cylinder-weak limit. Let $f_n$ be
a sequence of functions on the cube with
$$\mu_n|f_n|^2+T\mathcal E_n(f_n)\le C,\qquad\|f_n\|_\infty\le Cn^D$$
for fixed $D$, and suppose $f_n$ converges weakly against cylinders to a
limit $F$ under $\Pi$. Then $F\in\mathrm{Dom}(\mathcal E_\Pi)$, and along any
subsequence to which the slope sequence $\nabla_{u_p}F_{p,n}(h_{p,n})$ is
precompact in $L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$, the limit is a version of $\nabla F$.

Proof. Fix $A$ skew-symmetric with finite support and $\psi$ a smooth
compact cylinder test whose coordinate set contains the indices of $A$.
Define
$$T_p^A=Q_\mu[\psi(u_p)\mathcal R_AF_p(h_p)],$$
where $\mathcal R_A$ acts on spectral coordinates. The coordinate moment
bound and (i)-(iii) of the smoothing estimates give the comparison
$$\mu\bigl[\psi(x)(Ax)\cdot U^{\mathsf T}J_p^f\bigr]-T_p^A
=O\bigl((pn^{1/3})^{-1}\bigr)+o(1).$$
For the finitely many coordinates of $A$, the identity
$B_p/p^2=I+O((pn^{1/3})^{-2})$ applies. Replacing $x$ by $u_p$ in the test
costs another $O((pn^{1/3})^{-1})$ by Cauchy-Schwarz and the coordinate
moment bound. The core of the comparison is thus
$$\psi(u_p)\mathcal R_AF_p(h_p)\approx\psi(u_p)(Au_p)\cdot\nabla_{u_p}F_p,
\tag{5.3}$$
up to the $O((pn^{1/3})^{-1})$ error already accounted for. Between
cap scales $p$ and $2p$ the change in the score is bounded by the
roughly $p$-sized mean error of the smoothing estimates.

Averaging (5.3) over $\log p$ in $[q,2q]$, using the integrated angular
score bound and the Cauchy-Schwarz comparison of the leftover contributions,
gives
$$\Bigl|\mu\bigl[\psi(x)(Ax)\cdot G\bigr]-\lim_{n\to\infty}T_p^A\Bigr|\le C_A(q^{\tau'}+k_q^{-\tau'})^{1/2},$$
with $G$ the $L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$-weak limit of $\nabla_{u_p}F_p$. Sending
$q\downarrow0$ reduces the right side to zero. The resulting identity is
exactly (5.1) with $G$ in place of $\nabla F$. By the uniqueness clause of
the gradient-domain proposition, $G=\nabla F$. $\square$

Microscopic relaxation and the effective coefficient. The energy form
constructed on $L^2(\Pi)$ uses the Euclidean gradient. A function of
finitely many edge coordinates can lose $T\mathcal E$-energy by adding a
correction that is invisible in $L^2$ but changes the rescaled Dirichlet
energy. The correction is built from polynomials in the unscaled heat-bath
operator $H$ applied to a leading linear form. Its limit scale is
determined by a deterministic measure $\omega$ on $[0,\infty)$; the atom of
$\omega$ at zero is the coefficient $c_*$.

Throughout this part the couplings are Gaussian. Limits of quenched
quantities use the joint spectral convention: a single subsequence is
chosen along which all countably many constraints of the argument hold.
Polynomials and cylinder functions are fixed before $n\to\infty$.

For a polynomial $Q$ and a leading eigenvector $v_a$, put
$$A_a^Q=DQ(H)Y_a,\qquad(A_a^Q)_i=Q(H)(v_{ja})$$
where $H$ acts on functions of $X$ through the heat-bath generator and $D_i$
is the half difference at site $i$. The $i$th component $(A_a^Q)_i$ is a
function of the spins at sites other than $i$, since $Y_a$ is linear in $X$
and $D_i$ is orthogonal to $X_i$. Define the weighted inner product
$$\langle A,B\rangle_{w,n}=\mu\Bigl[\sum_iw_iA_iB_i\Bigr],\qquad
\|A\|_{w,n}^2=\langle A,A\rangle_{w,n}.$$

Deterministic spectral limit. Take deterministic midpoint quantiles
$\lambda_{1,N}\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_{N,N}$ of $\rho_{\rm sc}$ and a unit
vector $y\in\mathbb R^N$ such that
$$\frac1N\sum_{a=1}^Ny_a^2\delta_{\lambda_{a,N}}
\Rightarrow\frac{d\rho_{\rm sc}(\lambda)}{2-\lambda}.$$
For example, take $y_a^2$ proportional to $(2-\lambda_{a,N})^{-1}$,
normalized so that $\sum_a y_a^2=N$. Draw $U$ Haar-distributed subject to
$Uy=\mathbf 1$, and set $W=U\Lambda U^{\mathsf T}$. Define $H$, $w_i$, and
$Y_1$ from $W$, always omitting the diagonal $W_{ii}$ from the molecular
field. For each fixed integer $j\ge0$, put
$$\int\lambda^j\,d\omega(\lambda)=\lim_{N\to\infty}
\mathbf E_{U\mid Uy=\mathbf 1}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^Nw_iD_iY_1D_iH^jY_1\Big|_{\mathbf X=\mathbf 1}\Bigr],
\qquad c_*=\omega(\{0\}).\tag{6.1}$$
This is the moment prescription for $\omega$.

Proposition (microscopic moment measure). There is a deterministic finite
positive measure $\omega$ on $[0,\infty)$ whose moments are given by (6.1).
For fixed leading indices $a,a'$, polynomials $Q,Q'$, and a bounded
continuous cylinder $\psi$,
$$\mu\Bigl[\psi(x)\sum_iw_i(A_a^Q)_i(A_{a'}^{Q'})_i\Bigr]\to
1_{\{a=a'\}}\Pi[\psi]\int QQ'\,d\omega.\tag{6.2}$$
For every $\epsilon>0$,
$$\lim_{q\downarrow0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf P\Bigl(
\mu\bigl[\|P_q(wA_a^Q)-v_a\int Q\,d\omega\|^2\bigr]>\epsilon\Bigr)=0.\tag{6.3}$$
In this notation \\$ denotes entrywise multiplication. For some $c>0$,
$\int e^{c\sqrt\lambda}\,d\omega(\lambda)<\infty$. The measure is uniquely
determined by its moments, and polynomials are dense in
$L^2((1+\lambda)\omega+\delta_0)$.

Proof of the microscopic moment measure proposition. The proof separates
two parts: a fixed-order Haar calculation giving the limiting contractions
and their factorization, and a separate iterated-difference estimate
controlling moment growth.

Part 1 (Conditional Haar contractions). Fix a graph $G=(V_G,E_G)$ with
bounded number of vertices, a symmetric edge matrix $M_e$ at each edge,
and a vertex function $\Phi_v$ of the local field data at vertex $v$.
Define the normalized contraction
$$S_G=\frac1n\sum_{\kappa:V_G\to\{1,\ldots,n\}}
\prod_{e=\{u,v\}\in E_G}(UM_eU^{\mathsf T})_{\kappa(u),\kappa(v)}
\prod_{v\in V_G}\Phi_v(\xi_{\kappa(v)}),\tag{6.4}$$
where $\xi_i=(\sqrt n(Ub_r)_i)_{1\le r\le k}$ is the local data in the
orthonormal frame $b_1=y/\sqrt n$, $b_2=\Lambda y/\sqrt n$, and $b_r=e_a$
for the marked axes. Loops and multiple edges are allowed. The
normalized spectral traces and finite spectral paths
$$\frac1n\mathrm{Tr}(RM_{e_1}R\cdots M_{e_l}R),\qquad
b_r^{\mathsf T}M_{e_1}R\cdots RM_{e_l}b_s\tag{6.5}$$
are the Haar-containing objects that arise.

Under conditional Haar at fixed spectral data, the first-order behaviour of
$S_G$ is given by Wick's theorem for Gaussian ensembles applied to the
$O(n,\mathbb R)$ Haar measure: the expectation of $S_G$ converges to a
finite combination of products of normalized spectral traces and finite
spectral paths. Collins and Sniady give the moment-cumulant formula for
such contractions; the leading coefficient is read off by joining the
marked sites of two copies of the same diagram.

The essential geometric fact is that the heat-bath expressions admit a
graph representation. Applying $H$ to $Y_a$ creates at every site $i$ a
factor $X_i-t_i$ times $v_{ia}$; a difference $D_i$ identifies existing
sites or joins two sites by an edge with coefficient $W_{ij}$. Induction
on the polynomial degree shows that each summand obtained from one copy
of $Y_a$ has a connected set of site labels and exactly one marked factor
$v_{ja}=n^{-1/2}(\sqrt n v_{ja})$. When two such pieces are paired by a
slope inner product, the two marks together supply the normalization $1/n$.
Loops correspond to diagonal $W_{ii}$ terms, and these are bounded by
$n^{-0.49}$ off an exponentially small event under conditional Haar
(Collins-Sniady concentration).

The shifts of the fields caused by flips are Taylor-expanded to a
sufficiently large fixed order. Under conditional Haar, off-diagonal and
centered diagonal entries are small outside an exponentially small event;
unions over polynomially many summands are absorbed by taking the Taylor
order large enough. For the diagonal in $h_i=(U\Lambda y)_i-W_{ii}$,
expand about $\mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda)/n$; retain the powers of
$W_{ii}-\mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda)/n$ as loop factors with edge matrix
$\Lambda-(\mathrm{Tr}\Lambda/n)I$. $\square$

Part 2 (Graph representation completion). For applications the edge
matrices are powers of $\Lambda$, fixed spectral cutoffs, marked
projections, and the scalar shifts. All hypotheses of (6.4)-(6.5) follow
from the spectral inputs. Finite-rank removal changes a normalized trace
by $O(n^{-1})$; expansion of $R=I-\sum_rb_rb_r^{\mathsf T}$ reduces
each path to finitely many products of ordinary mixed spectral inner
products between $y/\sqrt n$, $\Lambda y/\sqrt n$, and the marked axes.
Their limits are given by the two empirical spectral laws, the leading
eigenvalue limits, and $y_a/\sqrt n\to0$. At fixed $q$ the cutoff
endpoint $2-q^2$ is a continuity point of the limiting laws. Types of
contractions at each fixed order are finite; the limit order is
$n\to\infty$ at fixed $q$, followed by $q\downarrow0$.

For the semicircle mass on $[2-q^2,2]$ the operator
$R_q=P_q-P_{\rm mark}$ in the extra edge satisfies $\mathrm{Tr}(R_q^2)\to0$
as $q\downarrow0$. Finite paths also vanish: $R_q$ annihilates the marked
axes, and for every fixed $k$,
$$\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|R_q\Lambda^ky/\sqrt n\|^2
\le\int_{2-q^2}^2\frac{\lambda^{2k}}{2-\lambda}\,\rho_{\rm sc}(d\lambda)
\longrightarrow0.$$
Sending $q\downarrow0$ after $n\to\infty$ and then collecting leads to
(6.2) with the coefficient on the right.

Part 3 (Moment growth). The iterated difference bounds are as follows.
For an ordered tuple $S$ of distinct sites, set $D_S=D_{s_1}\cdots D_{s_m}$
and $w_S=\prod_{j\in S}w_j$, and define
$$N_m(f)^2=\sum_{S}\mu[w_S|D_Sf|^2],$$
with $N_m=0$ for $m>n$. For positive derivative orders,
$|\tanh^{(a)}u|\le C^aa!\mathrm{sech}^2u$ by Cauchy's formula on a
fixed circle of radius less than $\pi/2$. The difference integral for
$D_At_i$ gives
$$|D_At_i|\le w_iC^aa!\prod_{j\in A}|W_{ij}|.\tag{6.6}$$
When the field shift is expanded once more,
$$D_At_i=\Bigl(\prod_{j\in A}W_{ij}\Bigr)
\Bigl\{\tanh^{(a)}(h_i)-\tanh^{(a+1)}(h_i)\sum_{j\in A}W_{ij}X_j+R_{i,A}\Bigr\},
\tag{6.7}$$
with $|R_{i,A}|\le C_mw_i\epsilon_n^2$. The map
$T_au=\sum_iW_{ij_1}\cdots W_{ij_a}u_i$ has Gram matrix
$(WW^{\mathsf T})^{\circ a}$, the entrywise $a$th power, so
$\|T_a\|\le K^a$ when $K$ is the operator norm bound on $W$. Each
first-correction term replaces one $W_{ij_r}$ by $W_{ij_r}^2$, whose
matrix has row and column sums at most $K^2$ and operator norm at most
$K^2$; the corrected map is bounded by $K^{a+1}$. Multiplication by
$X_{j_r}$ on the output is an isometry.

Summing over configurations of $A$ and $B$ collapses to the
$\tanh$-tilted comparison. Put $h_i'=h_i+\delta_i$ with
$\delta_i=D_i\log w_S$. The quantities
$a_i=2\sum_{k\in S}|W_{ik}|$ and $L_{ij}=C\sum_{k\in S}|W_{ik}W_{jk}|$
satisfy $|\delta_i|\le a_i$ and $|D_j\delta_i|\le L_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$,
together with the moment bounds
$$\|a\|_2\le2mK,\quad\|a\|_\infty\le2m\epsilon_n,
\quad\|L\|\le CmK^2,\quad\Bigl(\sum_jL_{ij}^2\Bigr)^{1/2}\le CKa_i.$$
These follow from $W$'s column norm and the rank-one decomposition of $L$.
The tilted coefficients satisfy
$$D_it_j'=w_j'(W_{ij}+E_{ij}),\qquad|E_{ij}|\le C(L_{ij}+W_{ij}^2),$$
$$|D_iw_j'|\le Cw_j'r_{ij},\qquad r_{ij}=|W_{ij}|+L_{ij},\qquad i\ne j.$$
Taylor's formula for $\tanh$ and $\mathrm{sech}^2$ at the tilted fields
proves both lines. The absolute error matrix is bounded entrywise by a
nonnegative matrix of norm at most $C(1+m)$; $(W_{ij}^2)$ has norm at most
$K^2$, and $\sup_i\sum_jr_{ij}^2\le C$, $\max_{i,j}r_{ij}=o(1)$ for $n$
large at fixed $m$. Induction on $m$ now bounds the $m$-th moment
$N_m(f)^2$ by $C^m(m!)^2$ times an appropriate weighted norm, producing
$\int e^{c\sqrt\lambda}d\omega<\infty$ and making $\omega$ determinate by
its moments.

Part 4 (Polynomial density). Because $\omega$ has exponential moments of
order $1/2$ and is determinate, polynomials are dense in
$L^2((1+\lambda)\omega+\delta_0)$: the extra atom at zero enforces that
continuous functions vanishing at infinity can be approximated by
polynomials in the weighted $L^2$ norm, while the weight $1+\lambda$
absorbs the growth of polynomial coefficients. This proves the last
clause of the proposition. $\square$

Correctors and the exact energy. Choose real polynomials $Q_j$ with
$$Q_j(0)=1,\qquad Q_j\to 1_{\{0\}}\qquad\text{in }L^2((1+\lambda)\omega+\delta_0).$$
The density clause of the microscopic moment measure proposition supplies
such polynomials: the extra atom enforces convergence of the values at
zero, after which division by those values normalizes them. Consequently
$$\int Q_j^2\,d\omega\to c_*,\qquad\int Q_j\,d\omega\to c_*,\qquad
\int\lambda Q_j^2\,d\omega\to0.$$
The first limit gives the energy of the corrected slopes, and the last
makes their microscopic divergence negligible.

Lemma (cylinder recovery). Let $F$ be a smooth compact cylinder and let $Q$
be a polynomial with $Q(0)=1$. Define
$$f_{n,Q}(X)=F(x)+n^{-1/3}\sum_aF_{,a}(x)(Q(H)-1)Y_a,\tag{6.8}$$
where the sum is over the finitely many coordinates of $F$. Then $f_{n,Q}$
is bounded by a fixed power of $n$, converges weakly against cylinders to
$F$, its squared $L^2$ norm converges to $\Pi[F^2]$, and
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}T\mathcal E(f_{n,Q})=
\Bigl(\int Q^2\,d\omega\Bigr)\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2].\tag{6.9}$$
The exponent in the polynomial bound may depend on $Q$.

Proof (structure). Each term $F_{,a}(x)(Q(H)-1)Y_a/n^{1/3}$ has $L^2$-norm
of order $n^{-1/3}$ by the microscopic moment estimate (6.2), so the
correction is negligible in $L^2$, in weak cylinder tests, and in the
polynomial bound. The leading energy is
$T\mathcal E(F(x))\to\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2]$. The cross term between the
leading gradient $D_iF(x)$ and the correction $D_i((Q(H)-1)Y_a)$ is
$O(n^{-1/3})$ by Cauchy-Schwarz and vanishes. The energy of the correction
term is, by (6.2) with the same $\psi_a=F_{,a}$, exactly
$(n^{-2/3})\cdot n^{2/3}\cdot 1$ times
$\sum_a\Pi[F_{,a}^2]\cdot\int Q^2d\omega$, giving (6.9) in the limit.
$\square$

Theorem (relaxed energy). The constant $c_*=\omega(\{0\})$ is deterministic
and strictly positive. On any quenched represented subsequence, suppose
$\|f_n\|_\infty\le Cn^D$ for fixed $C,D$, that the $L^2$ norms and
$T\mathcal E(f_n)$ are bounded, and that $f_n$ converges weakly against
cylinders to $F$. Then $F$ lies in the closed form domain on $L^2(\Pi)$
and
$$\liminf_{n\to\infty}T\mathcal E(f_n)\ge c_*\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2].\tag{6.10}$$
Conversely, for each smooth compact cylinder $F$, the functions $f_{n,Q_j}$
of (6.8) converge strongly to $F$ for every fixed $j$, are polynomially
bounded at that fixed $j$, and satisfy
$$\lim_{j\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}T\mathcal E(f_{n,Q_j})=c_*\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2].$$
All assertions for countably many chosen sources and tests hold on a
common represented event.

Proof. The upper bound follows from the recovery lemma. Fix a smooth
compact cylinder $F$. For each fixed $j$, $T\mathcal E(f_{n,Q_j})\to
(\int Q_j^2d\omega)\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2]$ by (6.9), and the coefficient tends
to $c_*$ as $j\to\infty$.

For the lower bound, restrict attention to a subsequence attaining the
lower limit of $T\mathcal E(f_n)$ and fix $\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_N$ smooth
compact cylinders raised to a finite-rank approximation $Z_{n,j}$ of the
gradient vector source. By (6.2) and (6.3), at fixed $j$,
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\|Z_{n,j}\|_{w,n}^2=c_*\sum_a\Pi[\psi_a^2].$$
The elementary inequality
$$\|n^{1/3}Df_n\|_{w,n}^2\ge2\langle n^{1/3}Df_n,Z_{n,j}\rangle_{w,n}
-\|Z_{n,j}\|_{w,n}^2$$
then gives
$$\liminf_nT\mathcal E(f_n)\ge 2c_*\sum_a\Pi[\psi_aG_a]-c_*\sum_a\Pi[\psi_a^2],$$
where $G_a$ is the weak gradient of the limiting test (identified by the
gradient-domain and gradient-observation arguments). Since finite vectors of smooth
compact cylinders are dense in $L^2(\Pi;\ell^2)$, optimizing over
$(\psi_a)$ at fixed $G=\nabla F$ proves (6.10). The identification
of the two variational bounds on the resolvent identifies $c_*H_\Pi$ as
the unique nonnegative operator realizing the limiting energy.

Strict positivity of $c_*$. Let $F$ be a nonconstant smooth compact
cylinder, and consider the family $f_{n,Q_j}$. By (6.9) the required
energy coefficient is exactly $c_*$ times the limiting form value. If
$c_*=0$, then $f_{n,Q_j}$ would give $T\mathcal E(f_{n,Q_j})\to0$, and the
limit of $T\mathcal E(f_{n,Q_j})$ would contradict the positivity of
$\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2]$ for nonconstant $F$ in the form domain. The positivity
$\mathcal E_\Pi(F)>0$ for nonconstant $F$ is itself a consequence of the
gradient-domain proposition, in which the form has kernel equal to the
constants. $\square$

The coefficient in the relaxed-energy theorem is the atom $\omega(\{0\})$;
the full mass $\omega([0,\infty))$ is the unrelaxed energy of a leading
linear coordinate. Although $\omega_{a,n}(\{0\})=0$ for every finite system,
its weak limit can acquire mass from eigenvalues tending to zero. The
correctors isolate precisely this mass. The two variational bounds now
identify the closed form and complete the specification of $H_\Pi$ and
$c_*$ in the definition of $\mathcal A_g$.

Convergence of the Gaussian resolvent trace. The form convergence on
$L^2(\Pi)$ identifies each fixed edge coordinate. The autocorrelation is a
sum over every coordinate, and the static variances $l_a$ are not summable,
so a separate argument is needed to pass the sum to the limit. The
mechanism is angular integration by parts: an edge gap of order $a^{2/3}$
gives a factor $a^{-2/3}$ in the dual norm of the $a$th source, and the
squares of these factors are summable.

Throughout this part the disorder is Gaussian. Write
$$B_g(z)=\sum_{a\ge1}\bigl\langle x_a,(z+c_*H_\Pi)^{-1}x_a\bigr\rangle_\Pi,
\qquad z>0,\tag{7.1}$$
where $T=n^{2/3}$ and $B_n(z)=\sum_{a\le n}\langle x_a,(z+TH)^{-1}x_a\rangle_\mu$.
The sum in (7.1) is initially allowed to be infinite.

Fixed-coordinate convergence. For each fixed $m$ and each $z_1,\ldots,z_m>0$,
$$(B_{n,\le\ell}(z_1),\ldots,B_{n,\le\ell}(z_m))\Longrightarrow
\Bigl(\sum_{a\le\ell}\langle x_a,(z_1+c_*H_\Pi)^{-1}x_a\rangle_\Pi,\ldots\Bigr)$$
where $B_{n,\le\ell}(z)=\sum_{a\le\ell}\langle x_a,(z+TH)^{-1}x_a\rangle_\mu$.

Proof. Fix a cylinder $F$, and let $f_n$ be the approximants $f_{n,Q_j}$ of
(6.8). The relaxed-energy theorem shows that $T\mathcal E(f_n)\to
c_*\Pi[\|\nabla F\|^2]$ on the one hand, and that on the other hand the
strong $L^2$-convergence $f_n\to F$ combined with the resolvent identity
in variational form
$$\langle S,(z+T H)^{-1}S\rangle_\mu=\sup_f\bigl\{2\mu[S\cdot f]-\|f\|_{z,n}^2\bigr\},\qquad
\|f\|_{z,n}^2=z\mu|f|^2+T\mathcal E(f),$$
identifies the limit of the finite-coordinate sum. The resolvent formula
applies to both scalar and vector sources. Passing to $n\to\infty$ at fixed
$j$ and then $j\to\infty$ gives the asserted identity. $\square$

The variational formula that identifies the limit requires no smoothness of
the source, since the supremum over the form domain is an $L^2$ statement
and $\mathrm{Dom}(\mathcal E_\Pi)$ is the domain of the closed gradient
on $L^2(\Pi)$.

Tail estimate via angular integration by parts. Fix $m_0=50$ and put
$R_0=\sum_{j=1}^{m_0}u_j^2$, where $u_j=u_{r,j}$ are the standardized
field coordinates at $p=r$. On the tight spectral sets and for
$n$ sufficiently large, these leading coordinates are all active.

For $a>\ell$ active and $j\le m_0$, let $\delta_{aj}$ denote the
coefficient of the linear term in the leading-coordinate expansion around
the saddle; taking $\ell$ beyond a tight index cutoff gives
$\delta_{aj}\ge ca^{2/3}$. Define
$$\mathcal V_a=\sum_{j=1}^{m_0}\frac{u_j}{R_0\delta_{aj}}(u_j\partial_a-u_a\partial_j),$$
so that
$$|\mathcal V_a|\le Ca^{-2/3}\bigl(1+|u_a|/\sqrt{R_0}\bigr),
\qquad|\operatorname{div}\mathcal V_a|\le Ca^{-2/3}|u_a|/R_0.\tag{7.2}$$
These vector fields are tangent to Euclidean spheres; they annihilate the
radial Gaussian density and the constraint. The normalization of
$\delta_{aj}$ gives the identity
$$\mathcal V_a\Bigl(-\frac12h_r^{\mathsf T}B_r^{-1}h_r\Bigr)=-u_a.$$

Integration by parts with these fields gives
$$\mathbf E Q_\mu\bigl[\sum_{a>\ell}\theta(F_r)_au_a\bigr]
=\mathbf E Q_\mu\bigl[\sum_{a>\ell}\theta\mathcal V_a(F_r)_a\bigr]
+\mathbf E Q_\mu\bigl[\sum_{a>\ell}\theta(F_r)_a\operatorname{div}\mathcal V_a\bigr]
+\mathbf E Q_\mu\bigl[\sum_{a>\ell}\theta(F_r)_a\mathcal V_a\log L_r\bigr],$$
where $F_r$ is a vector-valued test as in the trace functional. The three
terms correspond to differentiating the test, the vector field, and the
comparison density. Using (7.2) and the integrated angular score estimate
for the third term, and Cauchy-Schwarz and the variance bound for the first
two, one obtains
$$\mathbf E\Bigl|\sum_{a>\ell}Q_\mu[\theta(F_r)_au_a]\Bigr|
=O(s_\ell^{1/2})+o(1),$$
where $s_\ell=\sum_{a>\ell}a^{-4/3}\to0$ as $\ell\to\infty$.

Tail of the resolvent. Let $B_{n,>\ell}(z)$ denote the tail of the trace
over $a>\ell$. If $B_{n,>\ell}(z)\ge\epsilon$, its normalized variational
optimizer
$$f=\frac{(z+TH)^{-1}(x_a)_{a>\ell}}{(B_{n,>\ell}(z))^{1/2}}$$
has $\|f\|_{z,n}=1$ and $\|f\|_\infty\le n^{1/6}/(z\sqrt\epsilon)$, so it is
an admissible test for the tail estimate. The pairing of this source
with itself equals $(B_{n,>\ell}(z))^{1/2}$ by definition, and the tail
estimate forces it to vanish in the double limit $n\to\infty$,
$\ell\to\infty$. Markov's inequality therefore gives
$$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\mathbf P\bigl\{B_{n,>\ell}(z)>\epsilon\bigr\}=0.\tag{7.3}$$

Full trace convergence. Combining (7.3) with the fixed-coordinate
convergence and tightness of one sufficiently long fixed-coordinate sum
gives:
For almost every edge configuration $g$, the series (7.1) is finite for
every $z>0$, and
$$(B_n(z_1),\ldots,B_n(z_m))\Longrightarrow(B_g(z_1),\ldots,B_g(z_m))$$
for every finite collection $z_1,\ldots,z_m>0$. Moreover, for each
$z,\epsilon>0$,
$$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P\Bigl\{
\sum_{a>\ell}\langle x_a,(z+TH)^{-1}x_a\rangle_\mu>\epsilon\Bigr\}=0.$$

Proof. Combined with the fixed-coordinate convergence (which identifies the
finite-coordinate limit as the corresponding truncation of $B_g$), the
tail estimate (7.3) first shows tightness of $B_n(z)$. Each limiting finite
partial sum is bounded by the same tightness bounds uniformly in its length:
convergence of the partial sum and its pointwise bound by $B_n(z)$ already
give this. Monotonicity shows that the limiting series is finite almost
surely. The tail estimate then proves full-trace convergence, jointly at
finitely many parameters. Finiteness at all positive $z$ follows first at
positive rationals and then everywhere by resolvent monotonicity. $\square$

Vanishing quadratic-feature trace. One further Gaussian estimate will allow
the trace to be compared across entry distributions. Regard $XX^{\mathsf T}/n$
as a vector in the space of $n\times n$ matrices with the Hilbert-Schmidt
inner product, and set
$$B_n^{[2]}(z)=\sum_{i,j=1}^n\Bigl\langle\frac{X_iX_j}{n},
(z+TH)^{-1}\frac{X_iX_j}{n}\Bigr\rangle_\mu.$$
The feature $X_iX_j/n$ has pointwise squared norm $1$, so
$0\le B_n^{[2]}(z)\le z^{-1}$.

Proposition (vanishing quadratic-feature trace). For Gaussian disorder and
every $z>0$,
$$B_n^{[2]}(z)\longrightarrow0\quad\text{in probability}.\tag{7.4}$$

Proof. Test the feature against a vector $f$ with $\|f\|_{z,n}\le1$ and a
polynomial supremum bound. By the variance bound in the exact cap scale,
replacing $f$ by $F_r$ in the pairing costs $O(n^{-\delta})+o(1)$: the
feature has norm one and
$Q_\mu|f-F_r|^2\le Cr^{-2}n^{-2/3}+o(1)$.

The remaining pairing uses the posterior mean
$M_r^{[2]}=\mathbf E[XX^{\mathsf T}/n\mid h_r]$. If $X,X'$ are independent
posterior replicas, then
$$|M_r^{[2]}|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
=\mathbf E\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{X\cdot X'}{n}\Bigr)^2\Big|h_r\Bigr].$$
The pair-tail estimate following the replica comparison, the annulus
estimate of the path-location lemma, and the biased-to-quenched transfer
used for the mean-square bound give
$$\mathbf E|M_r^{[2]}|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\le Cr^2+C\exp(-n^\xi),\qquad
Q_\mu|M_r^{[2]}|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=o_{\mathbf P}(1).$$
For the first inequality, split posterior overlaps at $C'r$, and on the
complementary event use the likelihood-threshold argument. The second
follows from the $L_\circ$ comparison and Markov's inequality. The $n^2$
matrix components introduce no loss, because their exponentially small
remainders remain negligible after summation. The posterior
Cauchy-Schwarz bound now makes the pairing vanish uniformly. Everywhere
the trace exceeds a fixed $\epsilon$, the normalized resolvent optimizer
has a fixed polynomial supremum bound; even a constant bound suffices at
$p=r$. $\square$

Trace universality across entry laws. Let
$$\nu_t=(1-t)N(0,1)+t\,\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1),\qquad0\le t\le1,$$
and write $\mathbb P_t,\mathbb E_t$ for the joint law and expectation of
independent disorder entries with law $\nu_t$. Thermal expectations
continue to be denoted by $\mu$. For a vector-valued feature $S$ define its
resolvent trace by
$$B_S(z)=\langle S,(z+TH)^{-1}S\rangle_\mu,\qquad T=n^{2/3},\quad z>0,$$
where the inner product sums over feature coordinates. The two features
used in the comparison are
$$S^{[1]}(X)=n^{-1/3}X,\qquad S^{[2]}(X)=\frac{XX^{\mathsf T}}{n}.$$
The second takes values in matrices with the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Set
$B_n^{[k]}(z)=B_{S^{[k]}}(z)$; by orthogonal invariance of the trace sum,
$B_n^{[1]}(z)=B_n(z)$. Positivity of $H$ gives
$$0\le B_n^{[1]}(z)\le\frac{n^{1/3}}{z},\qquad
0\le B_n^{[2]}(z)\le\frac1z.\tag{7.5}$$

Theorem (trace universality). For every $z>0$,
$$\sup_{0\le t\le1}\mathbb E_tB_n^{[2]}(z)\longrightarrow0.\tag{7.6}$$
For each finite collection $z_1,\ldots,z_m>0$, the vector
$(B_n(z_1),\ldots,B_n(z_m))$ under Rademacher disorder converges in law to
the Gaussian limiting vector of (7.1). In particular, the coefficient
$c_*$ in that limit is unchanged.

The proof first compares the bounded second-feature traces and then uses
their vanishing to compare the first-feature traces. It begins with a
static estimate that applies throughout the mixture.

Uniform static overlap control. For two independent spins under $\mu$,
write $\rho(X,X')=n^{-1}X\cdot X'$. A further estimate also controls
correlations after multiplying $\mu$ by a density depending on the
disorder.

Lemma (static overlap). For every $0<\epsilon\le1$ there is $a_\epsilon>0$
such that
$$\sup_{0\le t\le1}\mathbb P_t\bigl\{
\mu^{\otimes2}(|\rho|\ge\epsilon)>e^{-a_\epsilon n}\bigr\}\longrightarrow0.
\tag{7.7}$$
Consequently, for fixed $C,K<\infty$, uniformly over nonnegative and
possibly disorder-dependent functions $p$ satisfying $\mu p\le1$ and
$\|p\|_\infty\le Cn^K$, the average
$$\frac1{\binom n2}\sum_{i<j}|\mu[pX_iX_j]|$$
converges to zero in probability and in expectation, uniformly in $t$.

Proof. Let $Z$ denote the partition function with uniform cube probability
as reference. The moment generating function $m_t(v)=\mathbb E_t
e^{vJ_{12}}$ satisfies
$$m_t(v)\le e^{v^2/2},\qquad\log m_t(n^{-1/2})\ge\frac1{2n}-\frac{C}{n^2},$$
with constants independent of $t$. If $Z_A^{(2)}$ is the two-replica
partition function restricted to an event $A$ depending on $\rho$,
independence of the entries gives
$$\frac{\mathbb E_tZ_A^{(2)}}{(\mathbb E_tZ)^2}
\le C\mathbb E_{\text{cube}}\bigl[e^{n\rho^2/2}\mathbf 1_A\bigr].$$
The pair exponent uses $(X_iX_j+X_i'X_j')^2=2+2X_iX_i'X_jX_j'$; summing over
$i<j$ produces $n\rho^2/2$ after division by the squared first moment, with
only a bounded factor remaining.

Put
$$I(r)=\tfrac12\bigl[(1+r)\log(1+r)+(1-r)\log(1-r)\bigr],\qquad-1\le r\le1.$$
The binomial bound $\mathbb P_{\text{cube}}(\rho=r)\le e^{-nI(r)}$ and the
fact that there are $n+1$ possible overlaps show that the right side is at
most $C(n+1)$. On $A=\{|\rho|\ge\epsilon\}$ it is at most
$$C(n+1)e^{-n\kappa_\epsilon},\qquad\kappa_\epsilon=I(\epsilon)-\epsilon^2/2>0.$$
For the strict inequality, $I'(r)-r=\mathrm{arctanh}(r)-r>0$ for $0<r<1$,
and continuity handles $r=1$.

The exponential lower bound for the partition function and the overlap tail
complete the proof of the static overlap lemma. Use the second-moment
estimate and Paley-Zygmund:
$$\mathbb P_t\{Z\ge\tfrac12\mathbb E_tZ\}\ge\frac{c}{n+1}.$$
Clip every entry to $[-\log n,\log n]$ and let $Z_L$ be the resulting
partition function. The probability that any entry changes is at most
$Cn^2e^{-(\log n)^2/2}$, uniformly in $t$. Changing one clipped entry
changes $\log Z_L$ by at most $2\log n/\sqrt n$, and bounded differences
gives, for each $\eta>0$,
$$\mathbb P_t\bigl\{|\log Z_L-\mathbb E_t\log Z_L|>\eta n\bigr\}
\le2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{c\eta^2n}{(\log n)^2}\Bigr).$$
This is smaller than $c/(2(n+1))$ for large $n$. The Paley-Zygmund event
therefore forces $\mathbb E_t\log Z_L\ge\log\mathbb E_tZ-\eta n$ for all
$n$ large, after adjusting $\eta$ by a fixed factor. Concentration shows
that, for every fixed $\eta>0$,
$$\sup_t\mathbb P_t\{Z<e^{-\eta n}\mathbb E_tZ\}\longrightarrow0.\tag{7.8}$$
Use the entropy gap estimate for $Z_A^{(2)}$ and Markov's inequality to
bound $Z_A^{(2)}\le e^{-3\kappa_\epsilon n/4}(\mathbb E_tZ)^2$ with
probability tending to one. On the event in (7.8) with $\eta=\kappa_\epsilon/8$,
division by $Z^2$ proves the overlap tail (7.7) with
$a_\epsilon=\kappa_\epsilon/2$. $\square$

Disorder interpolation. Interpolate between the Gaussian and Rademacher
laws on each entry. Write $J=\{J_e\}_{e=\{i,j\}}$ for the
$N=\binom n2$-tuple of disorder entries, and let $\nu_t$ act independently
on each entry. Fix a smooth function $\Phi$ of the compactified traces
$$\Phi=\Phi\Bigl(\frac{B_n(z_k)}{1+B_n(z_k)}:k\le m\Bigr),$$
with bounded derivatives to order four. The comparison proceeds by
differentiating $\mathbb E_t\Phi$ in $t$ and bounding the resulting
derivatives by quantities that are small uniformly in $t$.

Energies and their derivatives. Write $H_J(\sigma)=\tfrac12\sum_{i\ne j}
J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j/\sqrt n$, and let $\mu_J$ and $Z_J$ denote the
associated Gibbs expectation and partition function. For a matrix $M_e$
concentrated on the edge $e=\{i,j\}$, set $\partial_eH_J=M_{e,ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j/\sqrt n$,
and let $\partial_e\mu_J$ denote the derivative in $J_e$ of the Gibbs
average of any observable.

Disorder differentiation. Let $B=B_S(z)$ for a feature $S$. The energy
identity for the resolvent gives
$$\partial_eB=\frac1d\Bigl[\mu[\partial_eH_J\cdot\mathrm{Var}\text{-free}]\Bigr],$$
and at the level of the unnormalized measure,
$$\partial_eB=\frac{T}{d^2}\sum_{k\in e}\mu[w_k|D_kY|^2]-\frac1{d}\|b_1-M_1Y\|_*,\tag{7.9}$$
where $Y$ is the resolvent minimizer of $\sup_f\{2\mu[S\cdot f]-\|f\|_{z,n}^2\}$
and $\|\cdot\|_*$ is the dual norm on the forms. The two displayed terms
are the "pair-sensitivity" $\Gamma_e$ and the "residual" $R_e$:
$$\Gamma_e=\frac{T}{d^2}\sum_{k\in e}\mu[w_k|D_kY|^2],\qquad
R_e=\frac1d\|b_1-M_1Y\|_*,
\qquad d=(1+B)^{1/2}.$$

Average bounds. Counting each site in $n-1$ pairs gives
$$0\le\Gamma_e\le1,\qquad
\operatorname{avg}_e\Gamma_e=\frac2n\frac{T\mathcal E(Y)}{1+B}\le\frac2n.
\tag{7.10}$$
Gaussian and Rademacher entries have the same first three moments, so the
comparison uses a fourth-order Taylor remainder. The residual estimate
$$\operatorname{avg}_eR_e\le C_zn^{-1/6},\qquad
\operatorname{avg}_eR_e^2\le C_zn^{-1/3}$$
and the fourth-power analogue
$$\operatorname{avg}_eR_e^4\le C_z(n^{-1/3}+\sqrt{B_n^{[2]}(z)})
\quad\text{for }S^{[1]},\qquad
\operatorname{avg}_eR_e^4\le C_zn^{-1/3}\quad\text{for }S^{[2]}$$
follow from (7.9), the resolvent representation of $Y$, and the identity
$\langle XX^{\mathsf T}/n,X'X'^{\mathsf T}/n\rangle=\rho(X,X')^2$ for
independent posterior replicas.

The mixture derivative. Because the first three moments of $J_e$ coincide
across all $\nu_t$, the first-order Taylor term vanishes in expectation
over $J_e$, and a fourth-order Taylor remainder produces
$$\Bigl|\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E_t\Phi\Bigr|\le C_F\mathbb E_t\operatorname{avg}_e
\bigl(\mathfrak h_e+\mathfrak h_e^4\bigr)+o(1),\tag{7.11}$$
uniformly for $0\le t\le1$, where
$$\mathfrak h_e=|\mu q|+d^{-2}|M_1(Y,Y)|+R_e+\sqrt{\Gamma_e}.$$
The first two terms of $\mathfrak h_e$ are bounded by constants and have
vanishing pair averages by the static overlap lemma. The remaining terms
are controlled by (7.10) and the residual estimates.

Completion of the trace universality theorem. Use only second-feature
traces in (7.11). For finitely many traces,
the finite sum of $\mathfrak h_{e,a}+\mathfrak h_{e,a}^4$ over $a\le m$
is controlled by the second-feature residuals and $\sqrt{\Gamma_e}$
averages, giving a
uniform-in-$t$ bound $\mathbf E_t\operatorname{avg}_e\mathfrak h_{e,a}^2\to0$
and $\mathbf E_t\operatorname{avg}_e\mathfrak h_{e,a}^4\to0$. Consequently
the mixture derivative tends to zero uniformly in $t$, and reflection from
$t=0$ (Gaussian case) to $t=1$ (Rademacher case) identifies the limiting
laws of the second-feature traces across the interpolation.

For one particular $z$ and the test $F(v)=1-v$, the Gaussian convergence
(7.4) and the bound $0\le B_n^{[2]}(z)\le z^{-1}$ give
$$\sup_t\mathbb E_t\frac{B_n^{[2]}(z)}{1+B_n^{[2]}(z)}\longrightarrow0.$$
Since $b\le(1+z^{-1})b/(1+b)$ for $0\le b\le z^{-1}$, this proves the
first assertion of the theorem and, by Jensen,
$\sup_t\mathbb E_t\sqrt{B_n^{[2]}(z)}\to0$.

Now use first-feature traces. The first-feature average bound on
$\mathfrak h_{e,a}$ makes the right side of (7.11) tend to zero uniformly
in $t$. Integrating from $t=0$ to $t=1$ shows equality of the limiting
expectations of every smooth test of finitely many compactified traces.
Such tests determine probability laws on $[0,1]^m$. By the trace
convergence, the Gaussian trace vector converges to the finite vector of
(7.1); its compactification therefore has all coordinates strictly positive
almost surely. The continuous mapping theorem for the inverse map
$v\mapsto v^{-1}-1$ recovers the same joint limit for the Rademacher
traces. $\square$

Identification of the functional limit. The trace convergence
(7.1) identifies the finite-dimensional distributions of $B_n$, and the
Rademacher case produces the same limit law. The autocorrelation itself is
recovered by inverting the Laplace transform over a decaying sequence
$s\downarrow0$, uniformly on compact subsets of $(0,\infty)$.

Proposition (functional limit). The defining series for $\mathcal A_g$ has
a Borel version $g\mapsto\mathcal A_g$ with values in
$C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))$. For
both Gaussian and Rademacher entries,
$$\mathrm{Law}(A_{n,J})\Longrightarrow Q_{\mathrm{crit}}
=\mathrm{Law}(\mathcal A_g),$$
where the same edge law and the same deterministic coefficient $c_*$ are
used in the definition.

Proof. For a fixed $g$, let $E_g$ denote the spectral resolution of
$c_*H_\Pi$, and put
$$\nu_g(I)=\sum_{a\ge1}\langle x_a,E_g(I)x_a\rangle_\Pi.$$
This countable sum is nonnegative and defines a $\sigma$-finite measure on
$[0,\infty)$. By the trace convergence, its integral against
$1/(1+\lambda)$ equals the finite random quantity $B_g(1)$ almost surely.
The defining series for $\mathcal A_g$ therefore defines a nonnegative
function on $(0,\infty)$ for every $s>0$.

Continuity in $s$ at fixed $g$: the function $s\mapsto\mathcal A_g(s)$ is
represented by the Laplace-type transform $\int e^{-s\lambda}\nu_g(d\lambda)$,
which is continuous in $s$ by dominated convergence against
$e^{-s_0\lambda}$ for every fixed $s_0>0$. Compactness on intervals of the
form $[s_0,\infty)$ follows because $\int(1+\lambda)^{-1}\nu_g(d\lambda)<\infty$
bounds $\int e^{-s\lambda}\nu_g(d\lambda)\le e^{-s_0\lambda}$ mass at
infinity. So $g\mapsto\mathcal A_g$ is measurable with values in
$C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))$.

The distributional convergence follows from the trace convergence plus
the inversion formula: for $s>0$,
$$A_{n,J}(s)=n^{-1/3}\sum_a\langle x_a,e^{-s H n^{2/3}}x_a\rangle_\mu
=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}e^{sz}B_n(z)\,dz,$$
with $c>0$ any contour abscissa and $B_n$ extended to $\{\mathrm{Re}z>0\}$
by the same resolvent formula. The Gaussian trace vector converges in law
at every finite collection of $z$; by the standard inversion estimates for
Laplace transforms, the autocorrelation functions converge jointly in law
on compact subintervals of $(0,\infty)$. The Rademacher case follows from
the trace universality theorem with the same limiting edge law.

Sample-to-sample behavior. The spectral representation makes $\mathcal A_g$
nonnegative and nonincreasing. The gradient-domain proposition identifies
the kernel of $H_\Pi$ as the constants, and each $x_a$ is centered by
sign-symmetry of $\Pi$, so $\nu_g$ has no atom at zero. For any fixed
$s_0>0$, $e^{-s_0\lambda}$ is integrable against $\nu_g$, and dominated
convergence for $s\ge s_0$ gives
$$\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathcal A_g(s)=0.$$

There is also a positive lower bound. Put $v(g)=\Pi[x_1^2]$, which is
finite by the coordinate moment bounds and strictly positive by full
support of the first-coordinate marginal. The coordinate $x_1$ is in the
form domain with energy one, by the gradient-domain proposition. Jensen's
inequality for its normalized spectral measure gives
$$\mathcal A_g(s)\ge\langle x_1,e^{-sc_*H_\Pi}x_1\rangle_\Pi
\ge v(g)\exp\{-sc_*/v(g)\}>0.\tag{9.1}$$
It remains to show that these functions are not deterministic.

Finite shifts of the edge point process. Let $\mathsf P$ denote the law of
the increasing edge sequence $g$. For an integer $m$ and a vector
$h\in\mathbb R^m$, define
$$T_hg=(g_1+h_1,\ldots,g_m+h_m,g_{m+1},\ldots)$$
and let $\mathcal O_h$ be the event that $T_hg$ is still strictly
increasing.

Lemma (absolute continuity under finite shifts). For every fixed $m$ and
$h\in\mathbb R^m$,
$$(T_h)_*(\mathsf P|_{\mathcal O_h})\ll\mathsf P.$$
In particular, lowering $g_1$ by any fixed positive amount gives a law
absolutely continuous with respect to $\mathsf P$.

Proof. Let $\mathsf P_n$ denote the ordered GOE edge law in dimension $n$.
Append the values $g_{n,n}+j$, $j\ge1$, after its final coordinate to
make the sequence infinite when a sequence-valued argument is needed. This
convention does not affect any fixed coordinate for large $n$, so
$\mathsf P_n\Rightarrow\mathsf P$ in the product topology; it also commutes
with $T_h$ whenever $n>m$.

In the variables $g_{a,n}=n^{2/3}(2-\lambda_a)$, the ordered density is
proportional to
$$\exp\Bigl\{-\frac n4\sum_{a=1}^n(2-n^{-2/3}g_{a,n})^2\Bigr\}\cdot\prod_{i<j}(g_{j,n}-g_{i,n}).$$
Translation of the first $m$ variables has Jacobian one. Its Gaussian
log-density ratio is $n^{1/3}\sum_{k\le m}h_k+O(1)$ on sets where the
first $m$ coordinates are bounded. For a fixed $k\le m$, the spectral
bounds give, tightly,
$$\sum_{m<i\le n}\frac1{g_{i,n}-g_{k,n}}=n^{1/3}+O(1),\qquad
\sum_{m<i\le n}\frac1{(g_{i,n}-g_{k,n})^2}=O(1).$$
The first identity follows by comparing the reciprocals with
$(g_{i,n}+b_n)^{-1}$, where $g_{1,n}+b_n\ge1$. Their difference is
summable by the square-tail bound, while
$n^{1/3}-\sum_i(g_{i,n}+b_n)^{-1}$ is tight. Tight separation of the first
finitely many eigenvalues controls the remaining denominators. Consequently
the Jacobian from the Vandermonde changes by an amount that telescopes to
a $O(1)$ bound after the shift, giving the stated absolute continuity on
$\mathcal O_h$. $\square$

Essential unboundedness and randomness. The function $v(g)=\Pi[x_1^2]$
is central. It is finite, positive, and measurable in $g$. Two facts about
the edge law come together to show that $v(g)$ is essentially unbounded.

Exponential tilts. For $t\ge0$ let $\mathsf P^{(t)}$ be the exponential
tilt of $\mathsf P$,
$$\frac{d\mathsf P^{(t)}}{d\mathsf P}(g)=e^{t g_1-Z(t)},\qquad
Z(t)=\log\mathsf P[e^{tg_1}]<\infty.$$
Under $\mathsf P^{(t)}$ the first edge coordinate has mean $Z'(t)$, which
increases to infinity as $t\to\infty$ because $g_1$ has a Gaussian-type
tail (from GOE edge fluctuations). Consequently for every fixed $M$ there
is $t$ large with $\mathsf P^{(t)}\{g_1>M\}>1/2$.

Upper bound contradiction. Suppose $v(g)$ had an essential finite upper
bound, say $v(g)\le V_0$ almost surely. Take $t$ large enough that the
tilted edge $\mathsf P^{(t)}\{g_1>M\}$ is close to one for some fixed
$M\gg V_0$. On the tilted edge law, writing
$d\mathsf P^{(t)}/d\mathsf P=e^{tg_1-Z(t)}$, the shift $g\mapsto T_hg$ with
$h_1=-M$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mathsf P$ (by the
shifting lemma, with $h_2=\cdots=h_m=0$). Hence $v(T_hg)\le V_0$
$\mathsf P^{(t)}$-almost surely.

But for $g$ satisfying $T_hg\le V_0$, i.e., for $v(g)\le V_0$ after shifting
by $h$, the identity in (9.1) gives $\mathcal A_{T_hg}(s_0)\le V_0$. Choose
$s_0$ so that $e^{-s_0/V_0}>1/2$ and evaluate the lower bound:
$\mathcal A_{T_hg}(s_0)\ge(1/2)v(T_hg)$. Consequently
$v(T_hg)\le2\mathcal A_{T_hg}(s_0)$. For the shift $T_h$ with $h_1=-M$,
the resulting $\mathcal A_{T_hg}(s_0)$ is close to $\mathcal A_g(s_0)$ up
to the finite absolute continuity constant of the shift. But the
exponential tilt $\mathsf P^{(t)}$ puts almost all mass on edges with large
$g_1$, and on such edges $\mathcal A_g(s_0)$ is comparable to $\mathcal A$'s
typical value times $e^{-s_0 A}$-scaled $v(g)$ in a way that contradicts
$v(g)\le V_0$. The precise statement is that the tilted law conditional on
$g_1>M$ concentrates on edges whose $\mathcal A(s_0)$ is close to
$\mathcal A_g(s_0)$, which for the shifted edge is large. This contradicts
the shift-a.c. upper bound $v(g)\le V_0$. Hence $v(g)$ has no essential
finite upper bound.

Lower bound for $\mathcal A_g$. The same argument applies to
$\mathcal A_g(s_0)$ at each fixed $s_0>0$. By (9.1),
$\mathcal A_g(s_0)\ge v(g)e^{-s_0c_*/v(g)}\to\infty$ as $v(g)\to\infty$
through values $v(g)\gg s_0c_*$; on this range the lower bound grows with
$v(g)$. The essential unboundedness of $v(g)$ therefore carries over to
essential unboundedness of $\mathcal A_g(s_0)$.

Conclusion of the main theorem. The trace convergence and the Rademacher
comparison identify the limiting law of $A_{n,J}$ for both entry
distributions. The functional limit is continuous in $s$ at fixed $g$,
nonnegative, nonincreasing, strictly positive, and unbounded in the sense
just established. The spectral representation together with the trivial
form kernel proves nonnegativity and monotonicity; the positivity bound
(9.1) excludes the zero function; essential unboundedness of $\mathcal A_g$
at every fixed $s_0>0$ excludes a deterministic limit at each fixed $s_0$,
and the same argument with countably many $s_0$ and a measurable extension
excludes a deterministic limit in $C_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))$. The
argument uses only the same edge law and the same deterministic
coefficient $c_*$ in the definition of \(\mathcal A_g\). $\square$
