================================================================================
PLANAR SCHRODINGER ENDPOINT CONVERGENCE
a reader's companion to the 2026 paper
================================================================================

This file is a guided tour of the paper "Endpoint convergence for the
planar Schrodinger equation" (September 2026).  It is aimed at a curious,
motivated high school reader and does not assume any background in real
analysis, harmonic analysis, or measure theory.





================================================================================
PART I: THE QUESTION
================================================================================


1.  A SMALL STORY ABOUT WAVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Imagine you drop a single stone into a perfectly still pond.  A circular
ripple spreads outward.  If you knew the exact shape of the water at the
moment the stone hit, then in principle you know the shape at every
later moment -- the wave just spreads.  This is what the Schrodinger
equation does for quantum-mechanical waves (or, if you prefer, for
waves in a ripple tank).

But there is a subtlety.  In real life, the splash at time zero is a
messy, sloshy thing.  The water is flat far away, but near the stone
the surface is a jumble.  At time t > 0 the wave is smooth.  As t
shrinks to 0 does it "return" to that mess at each point of the pond?
Usually yes, except for a few unlucky points.  But most of the interest
in the Schrodinger convergence problem is in exactly this: does the
smooth-at-t>0 evolution recover the messy t=0 data almost everywhere?

The answer turns out to depend on how "rough" the initial data is
allowed to be.  The rougher the data, the more swirl there is at small
times, and the more likely some points get unlucky.


2.  SCHRODINGER'S EQUATION, CONCRETELY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The equation is

    i (d/dt) u(x,t)  =  -Laplacian u(x,t)         for x in R^2, t in R.

Here u(x,t) is a complex-valued wave function, x = (x_1, x_2) is
position in the plane, t is time, and

    Laplacian  =  (d/dx_1)^2 + (d/dx_2)^2

is the second-derivative operator.  The factor i is the imaginary unit.
The initial condition is u(x, 0) = f(x), where f is some function we
are given.  For nice enough f (say, smooth and decaying at infinity)
there is a unique solution, and it can be written as a Fourier integral:

    S(t) f(x)  =  (1/(2 pi)^2)  INT_{R^2}  e^{i x . xi - i t |xi|^2}
                                    f_hat(xi)  d xi

where f_hat is the Fourier transform of f and xi = (xi_1, xi_2) is the
"frequency" or "momentum" variable.  Don't worry if this looks scary;
we spend Chapter 6 explaining it.  For now, read it as: the initial
data f is a sum of plane waves e^{i x . xi} indexed by their frequency
xi, and each plane wave of frequency xi picks up the phase factor
e^{-it |xi|^2} as time goes from 0 to t.

The natural question is:

    Q.  As t -> 0, does S(t) f(x) -> f(x) for almost every x?

"Almost every x" means: for every x except possibly a set of points
with measure zero.  A set of measure zero is a set that you can cover
with arbitrarily small total area.  Points, curves, and countably many
such things all have measure zero.  So "almost every x" is a very
slightly weaker statement than "for every x" -- and it turns out to be
the right statement.


3.  SMOOTHNESS AND THE SOBOLEV EXPONENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To make Q. precise, we have to say what kind of functions f we allow.
If we allow every function in L^2(R^2) (square-integrable functions),
the answer is "no": there exist f where the pointwise limit fails at
a positive-measure set of x.  So we must demand some extra regularity.

The standard way to say "how regular" is the Sobolev exponent s.
Roughly speaking:

    f is in H^s(R^2)  if  f has "s derivatives" in L^2.

Here s can be any real number, not just a nonnegative integer.  "s
derivatives" is defined via the Fourier transform by a weighting of
the frequency.  Concretely:

    ||f||_{H^s}^2  =  (1/(2 pi)^2)  INT_{R^2}
                       (1 + |xi|^2)^s  |f_hat(xi)|^2  d xi.

For s = 0 this is just ||f||_{L^2}^2.  For s = 1 it is ||f||_{L^2}^2 +
||nabla f||_{L^2}^2.  For s = 1/3 it is a "one-third derivative" version.

The larger s is, the smoother f must be.  The interesting question is:
what is the *smallest* s for which pointwise convergence to the initial
data holds for every f in H^s(R^2)?  That smallest s is called the
*endpoint* of the problem.  Below the endpoint, there are explicit
counterexamples.

Carleson (1980) solved this in one spatial dimension and found
endpoint s = 1/4.  In two spatial dimensions (the plane) the endpoint
turns out to be exactly

    s  =  1/3.

Our paper proves convergence at s = 1/3.  Before this paper, it was
known that convergence holds for s slightly bigger than 1/3 (Du, Guth,
Li, 2017), but the endpoint itself was open.


4.  WHY 1D IS EASY AND 2D IS HARD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In one spatial dimension, waves travel along a line, and "the wave at
time t" is a function of one space variable.  In two dimensions, waves
spread over a plane.  The mathematics gets harder because:

    (a) there are more directions for a wave to travel;
    (b) the linear evolution S(t) f has a less friendly kernel;
    (c) the natural constructions of "worst-case" data are more
        complicated, because a counterexample can be built from
        configurations of points, tubes, and planes, not just from
        points and lines.

In the language of harmonic analysis, the plane is "not a small
perturbation" of the line: it has genuinely two-dimensional geometry.
A whole rich theory (restriction, Kakeya, fractal estimates, decoupling,
polynomial partitioning) has grown up around it.  All of these enter the
proof.


5.  THE STATE OF THE ART BEFORE THIS PAPER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The pre-2026 timeline, in order.

    1980  Carleson (1D): convergence for s >= 1/4.
    1982  Dahlberg-Kenig: s < 1/4 fails.  So the 1D endpoint is 1/4
          and it is sharp.
    1987  Sjolin (higher dimensions): convergence for s > 1/2.
    1988  Vega (higher dimensions): independent proof of s > 1/2.
    1995  Bourgain: connects the plane case to Fourier restriction
          theory.
    1996  Moyua-Vargas-Vega: further restriction-theoretic progress in
          the plane.
    2000  Tao-Vargas: "bilinear" approach, pushing the planar threshold
          down (s > 15/32).
    2003  Tao: sharp bilinear restriction theorem, improving to s > 2/5.
    2006  Lee: s > 3/8 in the plane.
    2013  Bourgain (higher dims, n >= 3): s > 1/2 - 1/(4n).
    2016  Bourgain: a counterexample showing s >= n/(2(n+1)) is
          necessary.  In the plane, this means s >= 1/3 is necessary.
    2017  Du-Guth-Li: s > 1/3 in the plane, plus a maximal estimate in
          L^3.  This paper put s = 1/3 "within reach": almost all the
          analysis was in place, but the limit was strict.
    2018  Du-Guth-Li-Zhang: higher-dim s > (n+1)/(2(n+2)).
    2019  Du-Zhang: for n >= 3, s > n/(2(n+1)).
    2026  This paper: s = 1/3 in the plane.  The endpoint, exactly.

So the gap this paper fills is small in numbers but hard in substance.
We knew s > 1/3 (strict).  We knew s >= 1/3 is necessary (counterexample).
The exact equality case s = 1/3 was open.  This paper proves it.

PART II: THE MAIN THEOREM
================================================================================


6.  ENDPOINT CONVERGENCE IN THE PLANE: THE THEOREM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

First let us say how we "regularize" the evolution so that the Fourier
integral converges absolutely.  For a > 0 define

    U_a f(x, t)  =  (1/(2 pi)^2)  INT_{R^2}
                    e^{i x . xi - i t |xi|^2 - a |xi|^2}  f_hat(xi)  d xi.

Compared to S(t) f, we have added a factor e^{-a |xi|^2}.  This is a
Gaussian taper: it kills high frequencies very quickly.  With that
taper the integral absolutely converges for every (x, t).  As a
decreases to 0, U_a f(x, t) formally tends to S(t) f(x).  The question
of pointwise convergence at time t becomes: does U_a f(x, t) have a
finite limit as a -> 0?

We also need the notion of a Lebesgue point.  Suppose f is locally
integrable.  At a Lebesgue point x, averaging f over a small ball
centered at x, and dividing by the area of the ball, tends to f(x).
Lebesgue's differentiation theorem says: almost every x is a Lebesgue
point.  We write

    f^*(x)  =  LIM_{r -> 0} (1/(pi r^2)) INT_{|y - x| < r} f(y) dy

for this pointwise average value.  Almost always f^*(x) = f(x); the
notation just handles the few places where f is too wild to have a
pointwise value.

THEOREM (main theorem of the paper).

    Take any f in H^{1/3}(R^2).  Then there is a set E_f of x's
    covering the whole plane except for a set of measure zero,
    and there is a function u_f(x, t) defined for x in E_f and
    0 < t < 1, such that:

        (a) for every x in E_f and every fixed 0 < t < 1,

                lim_{a -> 0} U_a f(x, t)  =  u_f(x, t)

            (a finite complex number);

        (b) for every x in E_f, the function t -> u_f(x, t) is
            continuous on [0, 1), and u_f(x, 0) = f^*(x);

        (c) the convergence in (a) is uniform in t on (0, 1).

    In particular, for every x in E_f,

                lim_{t -> 0} u_f(x, t)  =  f^*(x).

That is the entire paper, in one statement.  Everything else is the
proof.


7.  WHAT H^{1/3} MEANS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You can think of H^{1/3} as "square-integrable functions f whose
one-third derivative is also square-integrable."  Concretely, f is in
H^{1/3}(R^2) iff the integral

    INT_{R^2} (1 + |xi|^2)^{1/3} |f_hat(xi)|^2 d xi

is finite.  A function in H^{1/3} is not required to be differentiable
in the classical sense, and it is not required to be bounded.  But it
is smooth enough that its Fourier transform decays a little bit faster
than a typical L^2 function's Fourier transform does.

Why does 1/3 appear?  A heuristic to keep in mind:

    - "one full derivative" of regularity (s = 1) is enough to say
      the function is bounded and continuous.  Convergence at t=0 is
      then automatic.

    - Half a derivative (s = 1/2) is enough in any dimension, via
      the Sobolev embedding theorem.

    - Less than half a derivative requires real work.  The planar
      Schrodinger evolution turns out to be concentrated along the
      "backward light cone" of the initial data's frequency content.
      That concentration is measured by a geometric quantity that, at
      the critical scale, gives s = 1/3 as the exact threshold.

The paper makes this heuristic precise, but the mechanism (why
precisely 1/3 and not, say, 0.32 or 0.36) lives in the endpoint
estimate of [paper Sec 9].


8.  THE ORDER OF LIMITS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There are actually several possible ways to define "the evolution
u(x, t) at time t" for rough initial data f in H^{1/3}, and they differ
at the measure-zero level.  The convention in this paper is:

    (i) fix t > 0 and x (a Lebesgue point of some representative);
    (ii) take the Gaussian limit  a -> 0  first;
    (iii) then take  t -> 0.

That is, u_f(x, t) is defined by the Gaussian limit for each t > 0
separately, and then we ask whether u_f(x, t) -> f^*(x) as t -> 0.

This is NOT the same as:

    compute  lim_{a -> 0}  U_a f(x, t_a)  along some path  t_a -> 0
    with a -> 0 simultaneously.

The order matters because the Gaussian taper is what makes the Fourier
integral absolutely convergent, and interchanging a -> 0 with t -> 0
would allow high-frequency oscillations (with |xi|^2 ~ 1/a) to create
spurious contributions.

The strong content of the theorem is that, with this order of limits,
the convergence holds for every t simultaneously on a single
full-measure set E_f, and the resulting pointwise function is
continuous in t at 0.

This is why the theorem is stated as "the Gaussian limit is uniform
over 0 < t < 1".  Uniformity is the entire point: it says that no
matter how slowly a -> 0, the error is small for all t uniformly.

A related subtlety: the more classical problem, where one asks for
pointwise convergence of S(t) f itself (with no Gaussian taper), is
also true at s > 1/3 (DGL 2017), but their method does not obviously
reach s = 1/3.  The present paper's theorem is stated for the Gaussian
limit precisely because that is the more robust and the more provable
version.


9.  WHY s = 1/3 IS EXACTLY THE RIGHT EXPONENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There are two halves to "exactly":

    (a) Sharpness (upper bound on the achievable s).  Bourgain's
        counterexample [Bourgain 2016] constructs, for any s < 1/3,
        initial data f in H^s and a set of x's of positive measure
        where convergence fails.  So no regularity s < 1/3 can
        possibly work.

    (b) Sufficiency (lower bound on the achievable s).  This paper
        proves convergence at s = 1/3, hence a fortiori for every
        s > 1/3.

Together: the endpoint is exactly 1/3, and this paper reaches it.

Note that Bourgain's counterexample shows s >= 1/3 is necessary but
does NOT show convergence at s = 1/3.  In fact, for a while it was
unclear whether s = 1/3 might also fail.  This paper settles that: it
does not fail, and convergence holds.

PART III: THE TOOLKIT

10.  FOURIER: THE ONE IDEA YOU NEED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

If you remember one thing about Fourier, remember this: any function f on
R^2 (subject to mild technical conditions) is a "superposition of plane
waves":

    f(x)  =  (1/(2 pi)^2)  INT_{R^2}  e^{i x . xi}  f_hat(xi)  d xi.

Each plane wave e^{i x . xi} is a pure oscillation at frequency xi.  The
coefficient f_hat(xi) says "how much of this frequency does f contain".
The function f_hat is called the Fourier transform of f, and the process
of going from f to f_hat (and back) is the Fourier transform / inverse
Fourier transform.

The key facts:

    - The Fourier transform turns derivatives into multiplication.  The
      single derivative (d/dx_j) becomes multiplication by i xi_j on the
      frequency side.

    - The Fourier transform turns convolution into multiplication.  (For
      our purposes: it turns wave evolution into easy pointwise-in-
      frequency dynamics.)

    - Plancherel's theorem: the Fourier transform preserves the L^2 norm,
      i.e. ||f||_{L^2} = ||f_hat||_{L^2} up to a (2 pi) factor.

    - "s derivatives" means "multiply f_hat by (1 + |xi|^2)^{s/2}."  This
      is exactly how the Sobolev norms H^s are defined.

For a high-school reader: think of the Fourier transform as the
"prescription" for synthesizing f out of pure sines and cosines.


11.  WAVE PACKETS: SLICING A FUNCTION INTO BRICKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The main quantitative tool in the paper is a *wave packet decomposition*.
The idea in one sentence: any function of frequency up to ~N can be
sliced into little "bricks", each of which is (1) localized in space to
a small region, and (2) oscillating at a single frequency with a short
"burst" of time.

A wave packet at scale L (in space) and frequency xi_0 has the shape

    A_alpha(x, t)  =  L^{-1}  e^{i (x . xi_0 - t |xi_0|^2)}
                        H( (x - b_0 - 2 xi_0 t)/L,  t / L^2 )

where:

    - b_0 is the center of the packet at time t = 0,
    - L is the width of the packet,
    - L^2 is its natural "lifetime" (before the packet spreads enough
      that it is no longer well-localized),
    - H is a fixed "profile" -- a smooth, compactly supported function
      that describes the shape of the packet,
    - 2 xi_0 is the velocity of the packet (the frequency xi_0
      determines the velocity at which the packet moves).

The reason the packet is described by two parameters (b_0, xi_0) is
fundamental: a packet exists in "phase space", with position b_0 and
momentum xi_0.  The phase-space box has area ~1/L^2 per packet, and the
number of packets in a unit phase-space region is bounded by a fixed
multiple (this is the "phase-space multiplicity" hypothesis).

Wave packets are useful for two reasons:

    (a) They are the natural building blocks for the linear evolution
        S(t) f: at any time t, the packet is located near b_0 + 2 xi_0 t,
        moving at velocity 2 xi_0.  Packets are the "quanta" out of which
        S(t) f is built.

    (b) Pointwise questions about S(t) f can be localized to the packet
        level: if S(t) f has a large value at a point, then a packet
        must be passing near that point.  This allows the pointwise
        problem to be converted into an *incidence geometry* problem --
        counting how many packets pass through which points.


12.  FRACTALITY: HOW SPREAD OUT IS THE SET WHERE f IS LARGE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose you want to understand "how big is the set A where the wave
function |S(t) f| is unusually large".  A is a subset of R^3 (space plus
time).  Naively A might be a ball, a curve, a plane, or a spray of dust.

The key innovation of the last ten years is a *fractal* way of measuring
A: instead of asking "what is its 3-dimensional volume", ask "how does
the number of little boxes of side r needed to cover A grow as r -> 0".

Say A is covered by N(r) boxes of side r.  Then we can define

    dim(A)  ~  (log N(r)) / (log (1/r))

as r -> 0.  This is the *fractal dimension* of A.  Balls give dim = 3
(the full volume).  Smooth curves give dim = 1.  Smooth surfaces give
dim = 2.  Dust can give anything.

Why does this matter?  Because the maximal function

    sup_{0 < t < 1} |S(t) f(x)|

is bounded by a *distribution function*: the size of the set where
|S(t) f| exceeds a threshold lambda.  And the fractal dimension of that
set is directly controlled by how many wave packets are needed to cover
it.

The key fact is called a *refined fractal estimate* (in the paper,
Lemma 2.4 or so; the exact number depends on LaTeX).  Schematically, if
A is a collection of unit lattice cubes satisfying a "ball bound" -- i.e.
every ball of radius r contains at most gamma r^2 cubes of A -- then

    INT_{A}  |S(t) f|^2  dx dt  <=  C(epsilon)  S^{epsilon}
                                     (gamma lambda_0 S)^{1/3}  ||f||_2^2

for a certain constant lambda_0 (the "sqrt(S)-ball occupancy") and a
small error term S^{epsilon}.  This is the Du-Zhang fractal estimate
(2019), specialized to the plane.

The "1/3" in the exponent is the critical exponent of the fractal
estimate.  That is where "s = 1/3" ultimately comes from: the fractal
estimate is sharp at this exponent, and s = 1/3 is exactly the Sobolev
exponent that matches it.


13.  MULTILINEAR RESTRICTION: THREE WAVES ARE BETTER THAN ONE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here is a remarkable fact.

A single wave packet of frequency xi_0 has a big spatial profile.  Two
wave packets of the same frequency can interfere destructively (or
constructively).  But three wave packets, in three *transverse*
(different) directions, behave much better than you might expect from
individual bounds.

The precise statement is the *multilinear restriction theorem* of
Bennett-Carbery-Tao (2006):

    INT_{B_S}  |E v_1|  |E v_2|  |E v_3|  dx dt
                <=  C(epsilon)  S^{epsilon}  ||v_1||_2 ||v_2||_2 ||v_3||_2

provided that the three frequency supports are chosen with a fixed
positive "transversality" (their directions are separated by a fixed
positive amount).

In the plane (2 space + 1 time = 3 dimensions), the relevant statement
is often called a *multilinear Kakeya* estimate in frequency-space
language.

Why does this matter?  Because when we want to bound a product

    |S(t) f_1|  |S(t) f_2|  |S(t) f_3|

where the three functions have packets in transverse directions, the
multilinear inequality gives us a saving over naive single-factor
bounds.  The saving is the fundamental engine of the transverse power
saving in Section 3 of the paper.

When the three directions are NOT transverse -- say they are nearly
parallel -- then multilinear Kakeya degrades.  The paper has to handle
this case separately, and it is the *initial* transversality regime of
Section 3's power saving.  The "transverse power saving" precisely says
that transverse configurations cannot be degenerate: they must have a
small but fixed power saving over the trivial bound.


14.  POLYNOMIAL PARTITIONING: DRAWING A SURFACE THROUGH POINTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A more recent tool, developed by Guth and Katz (2015) and refined many
times since.

Setup: you have N points in R^3.  You want to find a surface (the zero
set of a polynomial of bounded degree) that passes through "a fair
share" of them in a way that lets you split the points evenly.

The Guth-Katz theorem says: for any N points in R^3 and any desired
degree D, there is a polynomial P of degree O(D) such that

    (a) each connected component of R^3 \ {P = 0} contains at most
        O(N / D^3) of the points;

    (b) if D << sqrt(N), then the number of connected components is at
        most O(D^3).

Think of it as: "draw the lowest-degree surface you can that separates
the points into small piles."

The paper uses polynomial partitioning in two places.  First, in the
transverse power saving (Section 3): after reducing a hypothetical
worst-case incidence configuration to a set of points and tubes,
polynomial partitioning (with a suitable degree) shows that the tubes
must fall into a small number of "cells", each of which is well
separated.  The separations contradict a direct incidence estimate.
Second, in the geometry of rich tubes (Section 7): the "plates" that
the tubes lie near are the zero sets of polynomials, and the packing
of those plates uses the geometry of surface neighbourhoods.

If you've never seen polynomial partitioning, the intuition from
computer graphics may help: think of a "3D scatter plot and a curved
mesh that weaves between the dots so no region gets too crowded with
dots".

PART IV: THE PROOF

15.  THE BLUEPRINT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here is the architecture of the paper.  Six self-contained pieces of
analysis, assembled at the end.

    INGREDIENT 1: transverse power saving  (paper Sec 3).
        A transverse configuration of wave packets cannot be
        "saturated": if three families of packets are pointwise large
        and transversally separated, then some fixed power of them is
        lost relative to the trivial bound.  This is a loss of L^{-c}
        for some small c > 0.

    INGREDIENT 2: sparse packet estimate  (paper Sec 4).
        If each packet meets a measure that is *sparse* (not too much
        mass in any tube), then some fixed power is lost from the
        packet sum.  This combines the fractal estimate with Ingredient
        1.

    INGREDIENT 3: bilinear frame estimate  (paper Sec 5).
        For two separated frequency families with arbitrary smooth
        packet profiles, a bilinear incidence count is uniform in the
        number of scales.  This is the estimate for "long, nearly flat"
        frequency configurations.

    INGREDIENT 4: flat-frequency arrays  (paper Sec 6).
        A one-dimensional distribution estimate (one space plus one
        time), with output exponent p_0 > 2.  And a two-dimensional
        "thin strip" projection that extends it.

    INGREDIENT 5: geometry of rich tubes  (paper Sec 7).
        If every tube sees substantial measure and the local directions
        at every point are not too concentrated, then all tubes lie in
        a small number of "plates" (thickened planes).  This is pure
        geometry, using polynomial partitioning.

    INGREDIENT 6: endpoint square summation  (paper Sec 9).
        With an estimate that gives p > 2 output, a binary time tree
        produces a convexity gain, which sums to the full Sobolev norm.
        This is the assembly.

THE ASSEMBLY (paper Sec 8, "Dyadic distribution bound"):
    Level 1: use the sparse estimate to handle the sparsest packets.
    Level 2: use cap gains plus geometry to reduce the remaining
             packets to a small number of plates.
    Level 3: for each plate, use the bilinear frame estimate at a
             shorter packet scale.
    Level 4: sum all plates via the thin-strip projection estimate.
    The overall output is a distribution estimate with p > 2, which is
    the input to the endpoint square summation.

Chapter 22 closes the proof by producing the maximal estimate and then
the Gaussian limit.  Let us go through the chapters one by one.


16.  TRANSVERSE POWER SAVING  [PAPER SEC 3]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  If a set of packets sits in a "saturated" configuration
-- meaning that at many points of space-time, all three of three
transversally separated families of packets are large -- then some fixed
positive power L^{-c} of the total packet energy is lost.

Precisely, Proposition 3.1 (paper numbering).  Fix a bounded frequency
region.  There are c > 0 and L_0 < infinity such that the following
configuration cannot exist when L >= L_0:

    - Q is a set of unit lattice cubes inside a ball of radius L^2 in
      R^3 (space-time), with |Q| >= L^{4 - c}.

    - Q satisfies a ball bound: a ball of radius r meets at most
      L^c r^2 cubes of Q, for every r >= 1.

    - Three functions v_1, v_2, v_3, each with frequency support in
      the fixed region and ||v_i||_2 <= 1.

    - At every cube Q in Q, |E v_i| >= L^{-4/3 - c} for i = 1, 2, 3.

    - The frequency supports of v_1, v_2, v_3 are *transversally
      separated*: every triple of unit directions, one from each
      support, has determinant >= L^{-c}.

BIG IDEA.  Suppose, for contradiction, that such a saturated transverse
configuration existed.  Then information theory (mutual information of
conditioned random variables) and projection theory (a theorem of He,
2020, on projections of fractal sets) produce a contradiction.

STEP 1: BUILD A RANDOM CONFIGURATION.
Sample a point X uniformly from Q.  Given X, sample three tubes
(independently, one from each family) through X.  The hypotheses
guarantee that the sampled tubes are rich and angularly spread, and that
they satisfy the ball bound.

STEP 2: INFORMATION PROFILE AT SUCCESSIVE SCALES.
For a function of X, define its *information* (in the Shannon sense) at
scale s as -log_L of the probability of the scale-s dyadic cell
containing X.  A lemma establishes:

    (a) The information of X at scale s, call it F(s), is a Lipschitz
        function of s, with F(0) = 0 and F(1) = 2.

    (b) The information of X at scale s, conditioned on knowing one
        tube, call it G(s), is also nice: G(1) >= 2/3.

    (c) A chain-rule calculation shows F = G_1 + G_2 + G_3, where G_i
        is the information conditioned on the i-th family.

    (d) A longer calculation using the transverse structure shows
        G_1 = G_2 = G_3 = G, and F = 3G.

STEP 3: G MUST BE PIECEWISE LINEAR WITH SLOPE 0 OR 1.
The derivative of G at almost every scale must be either 0 or 1.  This
is proved by combining:

    (i) the transverse power saving at successive scales (which forbids
        any bounded-away-from-integer slope, on pain of a contradiction
        with the projection theorem);

    (ii) an angular marginal argument (which forbids the "wrong"
        marginal at each block).

STEP 4: PARABOLIC RESTRICTION PLUS PROJECTION.
The function G being piecewise linear with slopes 0 or 1 means it
alternates between flat and steep intervals.  The final geometric
contradiction comes from a "hill argument": near a full-to-empty
transition, a certain balanced block must exist.  At that block,
polynomial partitioning (Guth-Katz) plus the incidence count of packets
gives a contradiction.

The technical core of the whole section is Step 3, and in particular the
way transverse power saving at scale L is propagated into a bound on
"information increments" at every scale.  This is the paper's most novel
technical contribution.

WHERE IT IS USED.  Directly inside the sparse estimate (Chapter 17).
The sparse estimate needs a small power saving in a regime where the
packets are not too clustered; the transverse power saving supplies
exactly that.


17.  SPARSE PACKET ESTIMATE  [PAPER SEC 4]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  If a measure is *sparse* -- meaning that every packet
sees only a small fraction of the total mass, as measured by the "tube
sparsity" condition (2.7) in the paper -- then some small power
D^{-c_s} of the total measure is lost from the packet sum.

Precisely, Proposition 4.1.  Let L^{tau_0} <= D <= L.  Let U be a
finite packet array of scale L.  Let mu be a nonnegative measure on
space-time with mu(B(z, r)) <= m r^2 (a ball bound) and support in
|t| <= C L^2.  Suppose the packet sparsity condition (2.7) holds with
parameter D for every packet.  Then

    SUM_{q unit cube}  mu(q)^{2/3} sup_q |U|^2
                 <=  C  m^{2/3}  L^{4/3}  D^{-c_s}  ||c||_2^2,

where c_s > 0 is a fixed absolute constant.

BIG IDEA.  Broad-narrow.  Decompose the packets into two classes:

    - BROAD: three packets, from widely separated frequency bins, all
      incident to a common point.

    - NARROW: everything else.  At any point, the active packets lie
      near a common line in frequency space.

For broad points, the transverse power saving (Chapter 16) gives the
needed D^{-c_s}.  For narrow points, one can run a *parabolic
rescaling*: rotate so that the frequency strip is axis-aligned, rescale
so that the strip width is of order 1, and apply induction on the number
of scales.  Each rescaling step is accompanied by a *measure transport*
that preserves the ball parameter and the sparsity condition.  This is
why the result is scale-uniform.

The rescaling is the technical heart of the proof.  The key calculation
is the "scale cancellation" (paper equation 4.14): the field factor
from an L-scale packet becomes L^{-1} at the rescaled scale, but the
measure factor from the pushforward is exactly (K^3 m_0)^{2/3}, so the
net measure-times-field is

    (K^{-2/3})(K^3 m_0)^{2/3}(K^{-1} s)^{4/3}  =  m_0^{2/3} s^{4/3}.

Nothing is lost from the scaling.

WHERE IT IS USED.  In the assembly of the dyadic distribution bound
(Chapter 21).  First, to handle the "sparse" regime where D is large
(D >= L^kappa for small kappa).  Second, later in the assembly, sparse
estimates at the "flattened" scale supply the D^{-c_s} factor in the
sum over composite packets.

The first use is the clean one: it immediately proves the dyadic
estimate in the "sparse" range (D >= L^kappa), by Chebyshev.


18.  BILINEAR FRAME ESTIMATE  [PAPER SEC 5]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  For two families of packets (index i = 1, 2) with
frequencies separated by a fixed delta > 0, and with sufficiently smooth
arbitrary profiles, an incidence count uniform in the number of scales.

Precisely, Theorem 5.1.  Fix bounded frequency and profile boxes and a
phase-space multiplicity bound.  Then there is a constant C' and a fixed
finite number of profile derivatives for which the following holds
uniformly in all parameters.

    Let h >= 1 and h^{-1/2} <= delta << 1.  Let V_i be two packet sums
    at scale h with profiles in the fixed box.  Let S_i^2 be the
    "envelope" -- a smooth upper bound for |V_i|^2 based on the packet
    positions.  Let H_i^* be the total packet energy in the i-th family.
    Then for any A_i, B_i with A_i / B_i >= C (a fixed large number),

    # {unit lattice squares containing a point where |V_i| >= A_i and
       S_i <= B_i, for i = 1, 2}

       <= C'  delta^{-C'}  (A_1/B_1)^{-2}  (A_2/B_2)^{-2}
             (H_1^* / A_1^2 + H_2^* / A_2^2).

The point is: the constants C' and the "number of scales" are
independent.  This is the paper's key uniformity of the frame estimate
in the packet scale.

BIG IDEA.  A dyadic iteration on the packet scale.

At each scale, the packets are presented as a smooth large packet.  The
algorithm:

    (i) MERGE: combine finer-scale packets into coarser-scale "frames"
        (this is the standard positive-frame construction, involving
        smooth caps P_a and "positive masks" rho_a).

    (ii) DELETE: at each merge, delete any part of the frame whose
        spatial amplitude exceeds a fixed multiple of the target.  This
        is the "positive mask" step.  Because the mask is nonnegative,
        it can only remove amplitude, not add it.

    (iii) CONTROL LEAKAGE: report the "removed pieces" d_a.  Their total
        is bounded by the flux estimate (Lemma ~5.x), which uses a
        Gaussian quartic potential.  The quartic potential is a
        positive increasing quantity that directly measures how much
        smoothing scale is being "spent" at each step.  Because the
        potential is positive and increasing, the total spent is
        bounded by the initial potential -- independent of the number
        of steps.

    (iv) RECOVER: after the iteration, recover each initial segment of
        amplitudes via a maximal inequality without a logarithm loss.

The key innovation relative to earlier work (GMW 2024, GLY 2023, DFGGL
2026, Li-Yung 2026) is the *smooth-mask, energy-flux, quartic-potential*
handling of profile leakage.  Earlier proofs used exact truncation or
Fourier-support identities, which do not work for general smooth
profiles.  Here the paper handles smooth profiles directly with a
positive-mask mechanism.

WHERE IT IS USED.  Directly in the flat-frequency estimates (Chapter
19), where the two separated families come from partitioning one
frequency interval into separated bins.  In turn, the flat-frequency
estimates are used inside the dyadic assembly.

The estimate is stated as a theorem because it is intrinsically useful;
but note the hypothesis h^{-1/2} <= delta: the two frequency families
must be separated by at least a small but fixed power of h.  This is
exactly the "not too narrow" hypothesis that makes the scale-uniformity
possible.




19.  FLAT-FREQUENCY ARRAYS  [PAPER SEC 6]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  Two distribution estimates.

(1) The one-dimensional flat estimate (Proposition 6.1).

    Let V be a 1D packet array at scale L with frequencies in a bounded
    interval I.  Let nu be a nonnegative measure on R x R (space-time)
    with a "tube bound":

        nu{ |t - t_0| <= r, |x - b - 2 v t| <= rho }
            <= m_1 r (1 + rho / L)

    for every t_0, b, v in I, and rho, r with 1 <= rho <= r <= L^2.
    Then there is p_0 > 2 (in fact one may take p_0 = 21/10) such that

        nu{ |V| >= g L^{-5/6} }
            <= C m_1 L^3 g^{-p_0} ||c||_2^{p_0}.

(2) The thin-strip estimate (Proposition 6.2).

    Let U be a 2D packet array at scale L, whose frequencies lie within
    C/L of an affine line in frequency space.  Let mu be supported in a
    spatial square of side L^2 and |t| <= L^2, with mu(B_r) <= m r^2.
    Then with the same p_0,

        mu{ |U| >= g L^{-4/3} }
            <= C m L^4 g^{-p_0} ||c||_2^{p_0}.

BIG IDEA.  Part (1) uses the frame estimate (Chapter 18) recursively.

    Choose a dyadic partition K of the frequency interval I into K equal
    pieces.  At a point, either two of the K pieces are "significant"
    (broad case), or only a few adjacent pieces are significant (narrow
    case).

    Broad case: apply the bilinear frame estimate to pairs of significant
    pieces, discarding the points where the envelope S_i is too large.
    Sum over pairs.

    Narrow case: the significant pieces cluster into a single interval J
    of length u = 3/K of I.  Rescale in a manner that preserves the
    hypotheses (this is where the parabolic rescaling enters).  The
    narrowed packet array has all the original required properties, and
    its measure has ball parameter m_1' = u^{-2} m_1.  Apply the
    distribution estimate to the rescaled array -- this is induction on
    the packet scale.

    Crucially, the gain per rescaling step is u^{1 - p_0/3}, which is
    positive because p_0 = 21/10 < 3.  If p_0 = 3, this gain would
    vanish, and the argument would fail.  This is where "p > 2 is
    essential" comes from.

    After summing the narrowed pieces, one closes the induction and gets
    the 1D distribution estimate.

Part (2) uses the 1D estimate after slicing by a normal variable.

    Rotate so that the frequency line is horizontal.  Split the spatial
    normal coordinate y into slabs of width L.  In each slab, the packets
    from a slab have bounded (y, t)-frequency coordinates, so their
    cross-section is a 1D packet array.  A Fourier expansion in y/L
    exponentializes the normal variable, turning the 2D field into a
    sum of 1D fields with exponential amplitude decay.  Apply the 1D
    estimate to each mode.

    The magic number L^{-4/3} in the 2D threshold follows from the 1D
    threshold L^{-5/6} via the L^{-1/2} = sqrt(L/L^2) relation between
    2D and 1D packet normalizations.  This is exactly the place where
    the exponent 1/3 (rather than something else) ends up controlling
    the endpoint.

WHERE IT IS USED.  The flat-frequency estimates are used directly in
the assembly of the dyadic distribution bound (Chapter 21).  After
geometric reduction to a list of plates, the frequencies in each plate
form a thin strip.  The thin-strip estimate at the plate level gives
the required distribution control.


20.  RICH TUBES IN A SHORT LIST OF PLATES  [PAPER SEC 7]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  If every tube in a collection "sees substantial mass"
against a common measure nu, AND if the local directions at each point
are not too concentrated, then all tubes lie near one of a small number
of "plates" (thickened planes).

Precisely, Proposition 7.1.  Fix constants and an exponent eta > 0.
There are constants A_*, C_*, C' such that: for every X, H with
H >= C_* X^{A_*} and X >= 2, and for every nu satisfying a ball bound,
and every family of tubes T satisfying:

    (i) Every tube is *rich*:

        INT (1 + d_T(q))^{-M} d nu(q)  >=  c_r H X^{-A_r},

        for M = 100.

    (ii) At nu-almost every q, there is a probability p_T(q) supported
         on tubes through q (within distance C_n of q) such that every
         direction ball B(e, r) receives mass at most C_a X^{A_a} r^eta,
         for r >= H^{-1}.

Then every tube T can be assigned to one of at most X^{C'} plates (planes
thickened by X^{C'}).  The assigned plate contains T's central line,
and any normal n to that plate satisfies

    |n . (2 omega_T, 1)|  <=  X^{C'} / H.

BIG IDEA.  The proof has five steps.

    STEP A.  For a fixed target line ell, find a rich plane through ell,
             using the local richness of nearby tubes.

    STEP B.  Localize the probability over nearby tubes onto a
             manageable grid of azimuth directions.

    STEP C.  For each azimuth bin, form an "auxiliary strip" whose
             second moments (via a Levy-type energy functional) are
             bounded by a fixed power of X.

    STEP D.  Use Cauchy-Schwarz to get a lower bound on the strip mass
             from the upper bound on the strip energy and the lower
             bound on the strip first moment.  This forces a substantial
             part of the mass to be in ONE plane -- the rich plane
             through ell.

    STEP E.  Pack the resulting planes into a short list.  Two planes
             that are close in normal direction must be separated by
             their offsets; two planes that are separated in normal
             direction intersect in a controlled amount.  A
             Cauchy-Schwarz / first-and-second-moment argument packs
             X^{C'} of them.

The polynomial partitioning enters in Step C (via the Du-Zhang fractal
estimate) and in Step E (via the standard incidence bound on well-
separated surfaces).  This is the "geometry" chapter: it turns the
abstract measure-theoretic hypotheses into concrete planar structure.

WHERE IT IS USED.  Directly in the dyadic assembly (Chapter 21).  After
the sparse-packets step and the angular pruning, we are left with a set
of packets whose direction distribution at every point satisfies the
hypotheses of Proposition 7.1.  The result assigns all of them to a
short list of plates.  Then each plate is handled by the thin-strip
estimate, and we sum.


21.  DYADIC DISTRIBUTION BOUND  [PAPER SEC 8]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  A uniform-in-scale distribution bound for a single
packet array.

Precisely, Theorem 8.1.  There are fixed p > 2 and sigma > 0 with the
following property.  Let U be a finite packet sum at scale L, with sum
of squares of coefficients <= 1.  Suppose mu is supported in a spatial
box of side O(L^2) and in |t| <= L^2, with mu(B_r) <= m r^2 for every
ball radius r >= 1, and suppose the tube sparsity condition (2.7) holds
for every label, with 1 <= D <= L.  Then for every g > 0,

    mu{ |U| >= g L^{-4/3} }
        <= C m L^4 D^{-sigma} g^{-p}.

BIG IDEA.  The proof is an assembly of the previous four chapters.
Structure:

    STEP 1.  If D >= L^kappa (sparse regime), Chebyshev + the sparse
             estimate closes immediately.

    STEP 2.  Otherwise (dense regime), iterate.  In each round, delete
             packets that don't see much mass (using the sparse estimate
             at parameter D_* = BD^2 > D); also delete angular
             concentrations (using a bounded-capacity tree argument) and
             cap concentrations.

    STEP 3.  After the deletions, the surviving mass is supported on a
             set where the direction law is angularly spread.  Apply the
             rich-tube geometry (Chapter 20) to get a short list of
             plates.

    STEP 4.  Split each plate into thin strips.  Apply the thin-strip
             estimate (Chapter 19) for the largest levels g.

    STEP 5.  For the remaining "middle" levels, flatten all intermediate
             scale packets into composite smooth packets at the "shorter"
             scale h = L/a.  Each composite packet is a single unit-scale
             packet in a different coordinate system, and the bilinear
             frame estimate (Chapter 18) applies directly.

    STEP 6.  Sum the resulting bounds over all plates and all packets.
             This chapter only gives the *distribution* bound with
             exponent p > 2.  The Sobolev square norm comes from the
             endpoint estimate in Chapter 22.

The value of p is chosen as 2 < p < 3 (specifically p <= p_max <
min(p_0, 3), where p_0 = 21/10).  The value of sigma is chosen so that
the various competing exponent losses match up.  This is a fiddly but
standard "order of choices" argument, spelled out in the paper as
equation (8.17) and following.

WHERE IT IS USED.  Directly in the endpoint chapters (22 below).


22.  ENDPOINT SQUARE SUMMATION  [PAPER SEC 9]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT PROVES.  Two things.

    (A) Local strong maximal estimate at s = 1/3:

        INT_{B(x_0, 1)}  sup_{0 <= t < 1} |S(t) f(x)|  dx
            <=  C  ||f||_{H^{1/3}},

        for f with compact Fourier support, with C uniform in x_0.

    (B) Gaussian convergence (the main theorem, see Chapter 6).

BIG IDEA (for part A).  Square summation on a common time tree.

We need to go from the distribution bound (exponent p > 2) to the
Sobolev norm.  The main step is:

    STEP 1.  For each frequency scale N, apply the distribution bound
             with L = sqrt(N) and D = 1.  This gives a weak L^p bound
             for sup over 0 < t < 1/N of |S(t) f_N|:

                 ||  sup_{0 < t < 1/N} |S(t) f_N|  ||_{L^p, infty}
                     <=  C N^{1/3} ||f_N||_2.

    STEP 2.  Define a dual quantity

                 G_N(I) (xi)  =  chi_N(xi) INT_{E_I}
                                  w(x) e^{i(x . xi - t(x)|xi|^2)} dx

             for a time interval I, where E_I is the set of x whose
             associated time t(x) lies in I.  This is the "dual" to
             the frequency localization.  Its L^2 norm behaves like
             N^{-2/3} times a distributional quantity.

    STEP 3.  Prove a square summation bound: for any measurable time
             function t(x),

                 SUM_{j >= 1}  2^{-2j/3}  ||G_{2^j}([0,1))||_2^2  <=  C.

             This is the paper's Lemma (Sec 9).  It is true because the
             same measure on time is used at every j.  This "common time
             tree" is essential: a per-j optimization of the time choice
             would lose the square summation.

    STEP 4.  The square summation bound is exactly the second factor in
             the Cauchy-Schwarz estimate on the dual pairing.

    STEP 5.  Recover the maximal function by finitely many choices
             (measurable selection), then take a limit to a countable
             dense set of times.  Conclude (A).

The key inequality behind Step 3 is the "convexity deficit" at exponent
alpha = 3/2 - 1/p > 1:

    for a parent dyadic interval with child masses a >= b,

        (a + b)^alpha - a^alpha - b^alpha  >=  (2^alpha - 2) b^alpha.

Because alpha > 1 (equivalently p > 2), the deficit (2^alpha - 2) is
strictly positive.  At p = 2, it would be exactly zero, and the
argument would fail.  So p > 2 is not just a convenience; it is
load-bearing.

BIG IDEA (for part B).  Once (A) holds, the Gaussian limit follows from
a standard weak-limit argument.

Choose a sequence f_n -> f in H^{1/3} with f_n smoother.  On a
full-measure set E_0, the pointwise limits of S(t) f_n converge
uniformly in t to a jointly measurable function v_f(x, t), and
v_f(x, 0) = f^*(x).  Separately, for each fixed t, the convolution
(K_a * v_f)(., t) equals the Gaussian-smoothed solution U_a f, by
Plancherel.  Taking a -> 0, dominated convergence plus the maximal
estimate gives U_a f(x, t) -> v_f(x, t) for every x in a slightly
smaller full-measure set, uniformly in t.

The "order of limits" subtlety is precisely what makes this work: the
pointwise representative v_f is continuous in t, and the Gaussian limit
correctly recovers it for every fixed t.  This closes the proof.

WHERE IT IS USED.  It IS the endpoint.  This closes the proof of the
main theorem.

23.  WHAT THE PAPER PROVES, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For initial data f in the Sobolev space H^{1/3}(R^2) -- the level of
regularity that a 1980 paper of Carleson conjectured was the endpoint for
the one-dimensional analogue, and that a 2006 paper of Lee got down to
for the planar analogue -- the free Schrodinger evolution S(t) f
converges to f pointwise almost everywhere as t -> 0, provided we
regularize the evolution by a Gaussian taper first (a -> 0 before
t -> 0), and provided the convergence is asked to hold uniformly over
0 < t < 1.  The endpoint s = 1/3 is exactly the correct Sobolev
exponent; it is sharp by Bourgain's 2016 counterexample.


24.  WHAT REMAINS OPEN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(a) Endpoint in higher dimensions.

    For n >= 3, Du-Zhang (2019) proved convergence for s > n/(2(n+1)),
    and Bourgain's counterexample gives the necessity of
    s >= n/(2(n+1)).  Whether the endpoint s = n/(2(n+1)) actually
    holds in dimension n >= 3 is open.  This paper is specifically for
    the plane.

(b) Endpoint maximal estimate in L^3.

    Du-Guth-Li (2017) proved a local L^3 maximal estimate for s > 1/3,
    which implies convergence for those s.  But their method does not
    reach s = 1/3.  The present paper only proves a local L^1 maximal
    estimate at s = 1/3 (which still implies the endpoint convergence).
    Whether the L^3 estimate extends to the endpoint is a different,
    open question.

(c) Pointwise convergence without Gaussian regularization.

    The paper's theorem is stated for the Gaussian limit.  Whether
    S(t) f itself converges (with no a) for arbitrary f in H^{1/3}(R^2)
    is a slightly different, presumably harder question.  The paper
    does not claim it.

(d) Quantitative boundary.

    The paper's constants are finite but not explicit.  The exact
    "spectrum" of bad points (their Hausdorff dimension, for example,
    for f exactly in H^{1/3} but not smoother) is not addressed.


25.  GLOSSARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALMOST EVERYWHERE.  "For all x except possibly a set of measure zero."
A set of measure zero can be covered by unions of balls of arbitrarily
small total area.

BALL BOUND.  A condition on a measure: mu(B(z, r)) <= m r^d for every
ball of radius r, in dimension d.  Also called a "Frostman" or
"dimensional" bound.

CARLESON'S PROBLEM.  The pointwise convergence question for the free
Schrodinger evolution, initiated by Carleson (1980).  It asks: for which
s does S(t) f converge as t -> 0 pointwise a.e., for every f in
H^s(R^2)?

CONVEXITY DEFICIT.  For exponent alpha > 1 and masses a >= b >= 0, the
quantity (a + b)^alpha - a^alpha - b^alpha is strictly positive.  It is
used in the endpoint chapter to guarantee a "budget" that sums over
scales.

DUALIZATION.  The use of a duality argument to bound a norm: if you can
pair the object with every test function of bounded norm and get a
bounded pairing, then the original object has bounded norm.

ENVELOPE.  A smooth, positive bound on the pointwise size of a packet
sum, constructed out of the individual packet envelopes with fixed
P > 2 decay in the transverse direction.

FOURIER TRANSFORM.  The decomposition of a function into plane waves.
In these notes, denoted f_hat(xi).

FRACTAL DIMENSION.  Roughly, the exponent d such that the number of
radius-r balls needed to cover a set grows like r^{-d}.

H^{s}(R^2).  The Sobolev space of s derivatives in L^2(R^2), defined via
Fourier weights: (1 + |xi|^2)^{s/2} f_hat is in L^2.

INCIDENCE.  A pairing of a point and a set (e.g. a tube) that contains
it.

INCIDENCE GEOMETRY.  The study of how many incidences can exist between
a family of points and a family of curves/surfaces/tubes.

LATTICE CUBE.  A cube of the form [m_1, m_1 + 1] x ... x [m_n, m_n + 1]
for integer m_i.  In R^3, "unit lattice cube" is [m_1, m_1 + 1] x
[m_2, m_2 + 1] x [m_3, m_3 + 1].

LEBESGUE POINT.  A point x where the local average of f over a ball
centered at x tends to f(x) as the radius shrinks to zero.

MULTILINEAR RESTRICTION.  The Bennett-Carbery-Tao theorem (2006)
giving a saving for transverse products of three extension operators.

PARABOLIC RESCALING.  The linear change of space-time and frequency
coordinates (x, t) -> ((x - 2 v t)/K, t/K^2), xi -> K(xi - v), which
preserves the form of the Schrodinger equation and of a wave packet
array.

PHASE SPACE.  The joint space of positions and momenta (for a wave
packet, the pair (b_0, xi_0)).

POLYNOMIAL PARTITIONING.  The Guth-Katz theorem (2015) giving a
bounded-degree polynomial whose zero set partitions space so that each
cell contains at most a small fraction of the points.

SCHRODINGER EQUATION.  The evolution equation i d/dt u = -Laplacian u.
Free-particle case.

SOBOLEV EXPONENT.  The s appearing in H^s(R^2).

TRANSVERSALITY.  A quantitative notion of "not parallel".  Three tubes
are L^{-c}-transverse if every triple of their unit directions has
determinant at least L^{-c}.

WAVE PACKET.  A spatially localized, frequency-localized oscillation
e^{i(x . xi_0 - t |xi_0|^2)} times a compactly supported smooth profile.
See Chapter 11.
