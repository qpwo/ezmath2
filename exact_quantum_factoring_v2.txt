The integer factoring problem asks: on input a binary integer $N ge 2$,
for the nondecreasing list of primes whose product is $N$, including
multiplicities. For $N = 360$ the answer is $2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 5$.

A quantum circuit operates on quantum bits (qubits) whose joint state is
a unit vector of complex amplitudes, acting by unitary matrices drawn
from a fixed finite set.

Theorem 1. There is a polynomial-time uniform family of polynomial-size
quantum circuits with the following properties.

  - On input $N ge 2$, the circuit outputs the complete prime
    factorization of $N$ with probability 1.
  - Gate count and qubit count are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
    $n = n(N)$, roughly the bit length of $N$.
  - Every circuit uses only the fixed finite gate set $G$: NOT, CNOT,
    Toffoli, Hadamard, $S = diag(1,i)$, their inverses, and
    their singly controlled versions.

"Polynomial-time uniform" means that some single deterministic classical
algorithm, running in time polynomial in the input length, writes down
the description of the circuit for each input length. "Probability 1"
means no failure. Both are worst case.

Shor's algorithm (P. W. Shor, SIAM J. Comput. 26 (1997) 1484-1509) gave
polynomial-time quantum factoring with bounded error. The reduction
uses multiplicative orders: if $a$ has order $r$ modulo $m$ and $r$ is
even, then $gcd(a^{r/2}-1, m)$ can split $m$. The splitting argument is
proved directly below.

./pdf/shor-1997-factoring-discrete-log.pdf

Three obstacles stand between "bounded error" and "probability 1":

  (i)   Retrying a bounded-error algorithm until success gives expected
        polynomial time, not worst-case polynomial time. There is no
        a priori bound on the number of retries.
  (ii)  Approximating arbitrary-angle rotations to ever higher precision
        still leaves a nonzero error, so it does not by itself yield
        probability-one correctness over a fixed gate set.
  (iii) The final step of Shor's, extract-an-order-from-a-Fourier-peak,
        yields the true order only with nonzero error; the order is
        unknown, so its test probability cannot be checked ahead of
        time.

All three obstacles fall to one design. Orders are made to have an
exactly computable probability, the run verifies its own auxiliary data
afterwards, and the final step uses a single exact amplitude-
amplification reflection at probability exactly 1/4. No parameter is
tuned; the polynomial-size parameters are chosen explicitly.

The factoring reduction from order finding is due to Gary L. Miller
(J. Comput. System Sci. 13 (1976) 300-317). Exact quantum algorithms
exist in stronger models. Michele Mosca and Christof Zalka (Int. J.
Quantum Inf. 2 (2004) 91-100) use computed rotations (gate parameters
set during the computation) to obtain exact transforms and report
Mosca's exact factoring algorithm; Muhammad Imran (arXiv:2205.04240)
gives exact order finding that requires a supplied multiple of the
unknown order, which factoring does not provide; Harumichi Nishimura
and Masanao Ozawa (Phys. Rev. A 81 (2010) 012317) show why approximate
universality cannot replace an exact gate analysis. The construction
below uses only the fixed gate set $G$, receives only $N$, and accepts
no advice.

The proof has two main ingredients, beyond the order-to-gcd reduction.

First, an order trial whose mass on the true order is a prescribed
dyadic rational. For a unit $a$ modulo $m$, its order $r$ is the least
positive integer with $a^r = 1 pmod m$. The trial returns no
candidate, or a positive multiple of $r$, and the probability of
returning $r$ is exactly $varphi(r)lambda(r)$, where
$lambda(d) = 2^{-lceillog_2 drceil - 10}$. The construction uses a
phase register that realizes a piecewise-linear interpolant $g$ of the
complex exponential $e(z) = exp(-2pi i z)$. Controlled modular shifts
give a phase-kickback construction in the style of Richard Cleve, Artur
Ekert, Chiara Macchiavello, and Michele Mosca (Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A
454 (1998) 339-354), but the auxiliary state is a four-phase step
state, not a Fourier eigenstate. Testing the auxiliary return yields
the interpolated matrix with its actual, unnormalized probability.

./pdf/cleve-ekert-macchiavello-mosca-1998-quantum-algorithms.pdf

Felix M. Lev's four-phase approximation to the order-sampling
transform (arXiv:quant-ph/0109103, Sections 3-4) gives a related route
to avoiding fine phase rotations; the interpolation and corrections
below form a different selected-branch transform with a specific
success law. The residue-class probabilities admit dyadic majorants
whose sums come from a bounded number of polynomial pieces and exact
power sums. Mosca and Zalka (Sections 4.1-4.2) approximate a
transform's success probability to arbitrary precision by power sums,
then use a computed-angle rotation to obtain an exact transform. The
finite polynomial pieces give exact dyadic sums. Two small correction
branches make the true-order mass exact; neither the true order nor its
factorization is required to run the trial.

./pdf/lev-2001-integral-shor.pdf

Second, a factoring run also generates the prime factorizations of
$p-1$ for its prime factors $p > 2$, recursively. This auxiliary
occurrence tree $D(N)$ has polynomial size. The recursive shape
resembles Vaughan R. Pratt's succinct primality certificates (SIAM J.
Comput. 4 (1975) 214-220), but the role differs: prime labels are
checked by a deterministic primality test proved in the appendix, and
the auxiliary factorizations determine orders and transition
probabilities retrospectively. The appendix adapts the
polynomial-congruence method of Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal, and
Nitin Saxena (Ann. of Math. 160 (2004) 781-793), with the constants
and auxiliary-prime choice established directly.

./pdf/agrawal-kayal-saxena-2004-primes-in-p.pdf

Once a run has completed and its data have been verified, those data
determine the exact probabilities needed to test earlier outputs.
Ordinary transitions are mixed with uniform guesses of their outputs,
and filters are computed from the completed data. Each transition has
the same exact retained mass. The crucial argument identifies these
deferred tests with a hypothetical sequence of tests using the true
data. Both descriptions mark the same recorded runs. The analysis
introduces neither advice nor conditioning on eventual completion.

This separation --- "make a useful event have a computable exact
mass" and "recover the information needed to evaluate that mass on
completed runs" --- is the technical heart of the proof.

Quantum rejection sampling (Maris Ozols, Martin Roetteler, and Jerome
Roland, arXiv:1103.2774) is a related use of selective retention and
amplification, with different state-conversion inputs and parameterized
rotations. The one-transition completion argument is stated
independently in Lemma 17. Efficient application also requires
verification of the auxiliary data and the all-good generation
property. A second dyadic correction makes the full preparation
success probability exactly 1/4.

As a first computation, take $N = 15$ and $a = 2$, a unit mod 15. The
powers of 2 mod 15 are $2, 4, 8, 1, 2, ...$, so
$ord_{15}(2) = 4$. Since $r = 4$ is even,
$gcd(2^{r/2} - 1, 15) = gcd(3, 15) = 3$, splitting $15 = 3 times 5$.
Every factoring run reduces to this. The remaining work is
exact-probability engineering that makes $r$ (a multiple of the true
order) appear with a prescribed dyadic mass, using only a fixed finite
gate set.

The order trial comes first, then the factoring process with its
auxiliary data and verifier, then an equalization of transition masses
that identifies the deferred and analytical events; one exact
amplification step proves Theorem 1. A deterministic polynomial-time
primality test, needed to verify prime-factor lists, appears at the
end.

Exact probabilities require fixed computational conventions. Every
polynomial takes its parameter $n$ as defined below unless indicated
otherwise; every logarithm in a bit bound is base two.

Definition 1. For an input integer $N ge 2$, set

    $n = max{128, lfloorlog_2 Nrfloor + 1}$,   $B = 2^n$.

Then $N < B$. The cutoff at 128 is a fixed constant; it has no effect
on polynomial-time uniformity and simplifies every strict inequality
below by avoiding small-$n$ edge cases. These $n$ and $B$ apply
throughout.

Definition 2. The fixed finite gate set G consists of:

  NOT = [[0,1],[1,0]],
  CNOT = the two-qubit gate |a>|b> -> |a>|b XOR a>,
  Toffoli = the three-qubit gate |a>|b>|c> -> |a>|b>|c XOR (a AND b)>,
  H = (1/sqrt(2)) [[1,1],[1,-1]],
  S = diag(1, i) = [[1,0],[0,i]],

together with their inverses and their singly controlled versions.

This is a fixed finite set of bounded-arity gates. Two consequences are
used repeatedly.

  (a) Gates with more controls are free: to test the conjunction of k
      controls, compute the conjunction into a scratch bit with
      k-1 Toffolis, apply a singly controlled target gate, then
      uncompute the conjunction.

  (b) Signs conditioned on a polynomial-size Boolean test are exact:
      compute the test into a scratch bit, apply S^2 = diag(1,-1) to
      its result, then uncompute the test.

Definition 3. A dyadic rational is a number A/2^T with A an integer
and T a nonnegative integer. Its representation has polynomial bit
length when T and the bit length of |A| are both polynomially bounded.

Integers, rationals, and Gaussian rationals are always represented
exactly. Euclidean division, gcd, and modular powering with
polynomial-length binary exponents take polynomial bit time. We use the
reversible simulation and cleanup method of Bennett [Bennett],
compiled into the Boolean gates of Toffoli ([Toffoli] Section 5):

  - Unroll a deterministic computation with a polynomial clock bound
    into a uniformly generated Boolean circuit.
  - Compute each intermediate AND into fresh scratch with a Toffoli;
    use NOT and CNOT for complements, XOR operations, and copies.
  - Retain the intermediate values, copy the answer, and reverse the
    gates to clear scratch.

This gives polynomial time and space overhead. Exact arithmetic networks
over this basis are also developed by Vedral-Barenco-Ekert [VBE].

Lemma 4 (Exact dyadic sampling). Let a classical basis label specify
a dyadic q in [0,1] by a polynomial-time computation with polynomially
bounded bit lengths. A circuit over the stated gates can append a flag
that equals 1 with probability exactly q, in polynomial time, without
altering the label.

Proof. Choose a common polynomial bound T on the denominator exponent,
and compute A such that q = A/2^T. Apply T Hadamards to fresh zero bits
to obtain a uniform label u in {0, 1, ..., 2^T - 1}. Reversibly compute
the flag [u < A]. Keep u and all required work bits. Exactly A labels
set the flag, so the flag equals 1 with probability A/2^T = q. Padding
to the common T handles a denominator that depends on the input label.
[]

When a procedure is said to RETAIN an event with probability q, it
appends such a flag and requires that flag for its eventual success. It
does not discard and renormalize the other branches.

An ancillary projection likewise denotes a tested event whose actual
squared norm contributes to the success probability. It is not an
instruction to force that outcome.

The next lemma lets us analyze procedures that mix quantum gates with
classical measurements and classical controls as if single
unitaries whose history is retained.

Lemma 5 (Retained classical records). Consider a procedure with
polynomially bounded time and workspace that uses the stated gates,
computational-basis measurements, and polynomial-time classical control.
Suppose subsequent quantum operations are controlled by the measurement
outcomes as classical data. It has a polynomial-size uniform unitary
implementation with the same distribution on its classical outputs and
success flag, while retaining polynomially many history and work
qubits. The inverse implementation uses the same fixed gate set.

Proof. In place of a basis measurement, copy its outcome, by CNOT
gates, into fresh history bits. Retain this copy and thereafter use it
only as a classical control. The branches indexed by different recorded
histories remain orthogonal.

Induction over the procedure shows that their squared norms and
subsequent outcome distributions equal those in the measured procedure.
Quantum work that is not measured in the original procedure remains
quantum work; in particular, no label is copied before an intended
interference computation. Thus no phase-register value is copied between preparation and test.

For the classical controller: run it for a fixed polynomial number of
ticks, keeping its history and using a halted flag after termination.
If a bounded-arity instruction selects registers by addresses, enumerate
all tuples of addresses in the polynomial workspace. For each tuple,
compute whether the controller selects it, apply the corresponding
singly controlled primitive, and uncompute this test. The number of
tuples is polynomial because the arity is fixed. A polynomial bound on
ticks, workspace, and stored records therefore gives a polynomial
circuit whose description can be generated in polynomial time. Reverse
the gate list and invert each primitive to obtain the inverse. []

The following consequence will be used when analyzing an adaptive
procedure.

Lemma 6 (Fresh registers reset the distribution). Fix a retained
classical history h. Its current classical inputs are fixed basis
labels, while old quantum work may be in an arbitrary state rho_h. If
the next trial uses fresh zero registers and leaves the old work alone,
it acts as I_old (x) U_h. Its outcome probabilities are those of U_h
alone, independently of rho_h.

Proof. Immediate from the composition rule: the ancilla in state
|0...0> is unaffected by rho_h, and tensor products of ancilla outcome
distributions with an arbitrary rho_h yield the same scalar marginal
over the ancilla. []

This observation permits conditional probability calculations along
recorded histories, even though the full implementation is unitary.

Exact deterministic primality is needed in Section 4 to verify

only required interface is an exact yes-or-no decision with a

The technical heart is a quantum trial whose
probability of returning the true order is an explicit dyadic
function of that order, with no knowledge of the order or its
factorization. That control is what makes the final algorithm exact.

Definition 7. For a unit a modulo m, write ord_m(a) for its
multiplicative order modulo m: the least positive integer r with
a^r = 1 mod m. Write phi(d) for Euler's totient:

    phi(d) = |{0 <= j < d : gcd(j, d) = 1}|,   phi(1) = 1.

For an integer d >= 1, write lambda(d) = 2 ^ (-ceil(log_2 d) - 10).

So lambda(d) is a dyadic rational bounded between 2^{-10}/(2d) and
2^{-10}/d. For small d this is around 1/1024 to 1/2048; it becomes
small as d grows but stays polynomially representable because d < B.

Proposition 8 (Order trial). Let 2 <= m < B and let a be a unit
modulo m, of order r. There is a uniform quantum trial of worst-case
cost polynomial in n that returns either no candidate or an integer d
satisfying 1 <= d < B and r | d. Moreover,

    Pr(trial returns r) = phi(r) * lambda(r).

The trial uses only the fixed finite gate set G and does not require r
or any factorization of r.

(1) "No candidate" is allowed; a returned d is a positive multiple of
r. (2) The mass at r is exactly phi(r) lambda(r), not just bounded
below. This is the crucial feature.

The construction has two parts. First, the Fourier phases in
order sampling by a piecewise-linear phase function g that admits exact
dyadic arithmetic. Second, two small classical sampling branches are added
that make the mass at each reduced fraction of denominator r exactly
lambda(r). The proof of Proposition 8 is completed at the end of
this section (Section 3.8).

Set

    Q = B^16 = 2^b,   b = 16n.

So Q is a large power of two, and log_2 Q = b = 16n is linear in n.

Definition 9. Let g : R -> C be the continuous, 1-periodic function
obtained by linear interpolation of exp(-2 pi i z) at the
quarter-integers. Explicitly, for integer q and 0 <= t <= 1:

    g((q + t)/4) = (1 - t) (-i)^q + t (-i)^{q+1}.

Two facts will be used repeatedly.

    (a) g(z + 1/2) = -g(z).
    (b) g has Lipschitz constant at most 4 sqrt(2), and |g(z)| <= 1.

The controlled-shift mechanism below is a form of phase kickback
([CEMM] Section 7). Here the auxiliary state is not a shift eigenstate;
its overlap with a shifted copy gives g. The success test in Lemma 10
is therefore part of the matrix's probability, rather than an
instruction to apply an ideal Fourier transform.

Lemma 10 (Piecewise-linear transform). There is a circuit of size
polynomial in b, on a b-qubit data register and ancillary registers
initially in the all-zero state, with a designated ancillary success
outcome, such that its unnormalized matrix on that outcome is

    A_{k,x} = Q^{-1/2} g(k x / Q),    0 <= x, k < Q.

The circuit uses only the fixed finite gate set G.

Proof. Prepare a b-qubit phase register in the state

    |chi> = Q^{-1/2} sum_{y=0}^{Q-1} i^{floor(4y/Q)} |y>.

This requires Hadamards on its bits, followed by a Z = S^2 phase on the
most significant bit (this contributes (-1)^{y_{b-1}}) and an S phase
on the next bit (this contributes i^{y_{b-2}}). The two together
produce i^{2 y_{b-1} + y_{b-2}} = i^{floor(4y/Q)}, because
floor(4y/Q) = floor(y / 2^{b-2}) = 2 y_{b-1} + y_{b-2}.

For an integer D, let T_D denote the forward cyclic shift
|y> -> |y + D mod Q> on this phase register. First verify the
phase-overlap identity

    <chi | T_D | chi> = g(D/Q).                              (*)

Write D = q Q/4 + s modulo Q with 0 <= q < 4 and 0 <= s < Q/4, and
put t = 4s/Q. The sum defining <chi|T_D|chi> is sum_y conj(chi_y)
chi_{y-D}. Let a(y) = 4y/Q. Then floor(4(y-D)/Q) equals
floor(a(y) - t) - q, and floor(a(y) - t) equals
floor(a(y)) - [frac(a(y)) < t]. Thus the difference

    Delta(y) := floor(a(y)) - floor(4(y-D)/Q) = q + [frac(a(y)) < t],

and conj(chi_y) chi_{y-D} = i^{-Delta(y)}. The Q values a(0), a(1),
..., a(Q-1) are equally spaced by 4/Q in [0, 4), and a fraction t of
them have fractional part below t. Therefore

    <chi|T_D|chi>
    = (1 - t) i^{-q} + t i^{-(q+1)}
    = (1 - t)(-i)^q + t (-i)^{q+1}
    = g((q + t)/4) = g(D/Q),

where the third line uses i^{-1} = -i, so i^{-q} = (-i)^q and
i^{-(q+1)} = (-i)^{q+1}. The FORWARD convention is fixed by checking a
shift by Q/4 (q=1, s=0, t=0): its overlap is g(1/4) = -i.

For fixed input x and output k, each data bit has exactly its specified
value before and after its single Hadamard. The product of the Hadamard
matrix elements is Q^{-1/2} (-1)^h, where h = sum_{i+j=b-1} x_i k_j,
and the total phase-register shift is D, where

    D = sum_{i+j < b-1} 2^{i+j} x_i k_j.

All omitted terms in the product x k are accounted for by

    x k = D + (Q/2) h  (mod Q).

Using (*) and g(z + 1/2) = -g(z) gives the claimed matrix entry after
projecting the phase register back onto |chi>:

    Q^{-1/2} (-1)^h <chi | T_D | chi>
    = Q^{-1/2} (-1)^h g(D/Q)
    = Q^{-1/2} g(x k / Q).

Now the bit-by-bit construction. Number input and output bits from the
units bit: x = sum_{i=0}^{b-1} 2^i x_i and k = sum_{j=0}^{b-1} 2^j k_j.
For j = 0, 1, ..., b-1 perform the following operations in order:

  - apply a Hadamard to the physical bit that initially carried
    x_{b-1-j}; its new value is k_j;
  - for every i < b-1-j, apply the shift T_{2^{i+j} x_i k_j} to the
    phase register.

Both controls are available without extra work: k_j is the
bit just produced, whereas x_i has not yet been overwritten. The
logical output bits are in reverse physical order from the input bits;
a wire reversal (swaps) puts them in conventional order.

[For a cross-check of (*), the case b=4, Q=16, D=1 gives both sides
equal to 3/4 - i/4. The phase-kickback idiom is the one of [CEMM]
Section 7.]

There are O(b^2) controlled shifts. Each is a reversible modular binary
addition on b bits, with its scratch bits uncomputed afterwards, and
therefore has polynomial cost in the stated finite gate set. All other
operations also have polynomial cost. []

Given a phase-register output k in {0, ..., Q-1}, we must recover which
reduced fraction j/d it is close to. This is the classical decoding
step of the order-sampling algorithm, made exact.

For every reduced fraction j/d with 0 <= j < d < B, define

    k(d,j) = floor( (j Q)/d + 1/2 ),
    eta    = k(d,j) - (j Q)/d.

Then |eta| <= 1/2, and 0 <= k(d,j) < Q. The pair (d, j) is called the label of
this bin.

Lemma 11 (Recovering labels). Given 0 <= k < Q, one can determine in
polynomial bit time whether k = k(d,j) for a reduced fraction
0 <= j < d < B, and recover (d, j) when it exists. Such a label is
unique.

Proof. Compute the finite simple continued fraction of k/Q, and let
p/v be its last convergent with denominator less than B. If all
convergents have denominator less than B, use the terminal one. We
claim

    |k/Q - p/v| <= 1/(v B).                                  (**)

Indeed, suppose p/v is not terminal. Let (p', v') be the preceding
numerator-denominator pair, using the formal pair (1, 0) when p/v is
the first convergent. The remaining continued-fraction tail theta
gives

    k/Q = (p theta + p') / (v theta + v'),   |p v' - p' v| = 1.

The next denominator is (v c + v') >= B, where c is the integer part
of theta. Consequently the error is

    1 / [v (v theta + v')] <= 1 / [v (v c + v')] <= 1 / (v B).

The determinant identity follows inductively from the convergent
recurrence, which replaces each numerator-denominator pair by c times
the latest pair plus the preceding pair. If the convergent is terminal,
its error is zero.

Now suppose k has a label (d,j). If j/d != p/v, integrality of the
cross-product gives

    |j/d - p/v| = |j v - p d| / (d v) >= 1/(d v) >= 1/((B-1) v)
               > 1/(v B) + 1/(2 Q).

For the strict inequality, note that 2 Q > v B (B-1) because v < B and
Q = B^16. This contradicts (**) together with |k/Q - j/d| <= 1/(2Q).
Thus j/d = p/v. It remains only to check 0 <= p < v < B and the exact
rule k(d,j) = k; these checks accept precisely the valid labels. These
also prove uniqueness. When k = 0, the terminal convergent is 0/1, so
the case d = 1 is included. The Euclidean algorithm and all these
checks use integers with O(n) bits and take polynomial bit time. []

Apply Lemma 10 to the order-sampling state

    Q^{-1/2} sum_{x=0}^{Q-1} |x> |a^x mod m>.

Reversible modular powering prepares this state in polynomial time; its
temporary work is uncomputed, leaving no additional information about
x. This is the order-sampling setup of the quantum factoring algorithm
[Shor], with the transform of Lemma 10 in place of the Fourier
transform.

For any reduced label (d,j) and 0 <= t < d, define the arithmetic
quantity

    P(d,j,t) = | (1/Q) sum_{0<=x<Q, x=t mod d} g(k(d,j) x / Q) |^2.  (***)

For d = r = ord_m(a), it has the following direct probabilistic
meaning:

    Pr(phase-register success and k = k(r,j))
    = sum_{t=0}^{r-1} P(r, j, t).

To see this, the unnormalized second-register vector for a fixed
output k is

    (1/Q) sum_{x=0}^{Q-1} g(k x / Q) | a^x mod m >.

The labels a^x mod m coincide exactly on residue classes modulo r; the
distinct classes give orthogonal basis states. Taking its squared norm
proves the identity, including the actual probability of the
phase-register test.

Definition (***) will also be used for candidate values d != r; for
those values no physical-probability interpretation is asserted. The
next step is to compute and correct the masses using arithmetic on
candidate labels, without knowing the true order.

The quantum experiment gives the probabilities in (***), but their
dependence on the bin is inconvenient. We will supplement the
experiment with classical random choices so that, at the true order
d = r, every reduced numerator contributes exactly lambda(d). The next
lemma supplies the arithmetic needed to do this without knowing r.

The construction has three steps. First, compute each progression sum
exactly. Second, approximate its mass by a piecewise polynomial J.
Third, round that polynomial upward so its TOTAL can be summed without
enumerating residues. Mosca-Zalka use power sums to approximate a
success probability to arbitrary precision ([MZ] Section 4.1); here the
polynomial pieces have bounded degree, so the dyadic envelope can be
summed exactly.

Lemma 12 (Dyadic majorant with exact sum). For every 1 <= d < B and
0 <= j < d with gcd(j, d) = 1, there are nonnegative dyadic numbers
U(d,j,u), 0 <= u < d, such that

    0 <= U(d,j,jt mod d) - P(d,j,t) <= 128/Q    for 0 <= t < d,   (a)

    1/(128 d) <= S(d,j) := (1/2) sum_{u=0}^{d-1} U(d,j,u) <= 2.  (b)

Given their integer arguments, P(d,j,t), U(d,j,u), and S(d,j) are
exactly computable in time polynomial in n, and are dyadic numbers of
polynomial bit length. In particular, computing S(d,j) does not require
enumerating its d summands.

Proof. Fix d, j, and abbreviate k = k(d,j) and eta = k - jQ/d (so
|eta| <= 1/2 by Lemma 11 and eta = 0 exactly when jQ/d is an integer).

Step 1: exact P. For x = t mod d, put u = jt mod d, represented in
{0, ..., d-1}. Because g is 1-periodic,

    g(kx/Q) = g(u/d + eta x/Q).                                  (i)

As x traverses the progression (x = t, t+d, t+2d, ...), the argument
u/d + eta x/Q moves a distance at most |eta| * (d/Q) * (Q/d) = |eta|
<= 1/2 in total, so it meets only a bounded number of the quarter-
integer breakpoints of g. Split the progression at those breakpoints by
rational comparisons and integer division. On each resulting part, the
real and imaginary coordinates of g are affine in the progression
index, so their sums come from the number of terms and the sum of
consecutive integers. This computes P(d,j,t) with a bounded number of
arithmetic operations on O(n)-bit integers and rationals.

P is dyadic. Each g(kx/Q) is built from (-i)^{floor(4kx/Q)} and
(-i)^{floor(4kx/Q)+1} with real and imaginary parts having denominators
dividing Q. Summing Q/d such terms gives a sum whose denominator divides
Q. Dividing by Q gives a value with denominator dividing Q^2. Squaring
and adding gives a modulus-squared with denominator dividing Q^4 = 2^{64n}.
This has polynomial bit length.

Step 2: a Riemann-sum approximation. Although each P(d,j,t) is
efficiently computable, direct summation over t requires d evaluations,
which need not be polynomial in n. The summand is replaced by a smooth
approximation whose total is accessible.

For real 0 <= u < d, define

    I(u) = (1/d) int_0^1 g(u/d + eta v) dv,
    J(u) = |I(u)|^2.

Two uniform estimates are recorded first:

    |P(d,j,t) - J(jt mod d)| <= 32/Q,   and
    1/(8d) <= |I(u)| <= 1/d.                                       (ii)

For the first estimate, let h = d/Q < 1 and f(v) = g(u/d + eta v). The
function g has magnitude at most one and Lipschitz constant at most
4 sqrt(2); multiplying by eta (|eta| <= 1/2) gives f Lipschitz constant
at most 2 sqrt(2) < 3. The progression points x/Q are the left
endpoints of intervals of length h; together spanning [0, 1] up to two
endpieces of total length less than 2h. The error of the left rectangle
rule is at most 2h(1+h), and the two endpoint discrepancies contribute
at most 2h. Consequently, writing

    A_t = (1/Q) sum_{0 <= x < Q, x = t mod d} g(kx/Q),

with u = jt mod d,

    |A_t - I(u)| <= (1/d)(2h(1+h) + 2h) <= 6/Q <= 8/Q.

Since |A_t| <= 1/d + 1/Q and |I(u)| <= 1/d, we have
|A_t + I| <= 2/d + 1/Q <= 3/d, and

    |P(d,j,t) - J(u)| = | |A_t|^2 - |I(u)|^2 |
                     = |(A_t - I)(A_t + I)|  (up to real part)
                     <= (8/Q)(3/d) <= 24/Q <= 32/Q.

This gives the first inequality in (ii). The upper bound |I(u)| <= 1/d
follows from |g| <= 1.

For the lower bound on |I|, temporarily replace g in the integral by
the exact exponential e(z) = exp(-2 pi i z). The resulting integral has
magnitude

    (1/d) | int_0^1 exp(-2 pi i eta v) dv |
    = (1/d) |sin(pi eta) / (pi eta)|
    >= 2/(pi d),

where the quotient is interpreted as 1 at eta = 0, and the last
inequality follows from concavity of sin on [0, pi/2]: sin(x)/x is
decreasing on [0, pi/2], so on |pi eta| <= pi/2 it is at least
sin(pi/2)/(pi/2) = 2/pi.

Now bound the interpolation error between g and e. Linear interpolation
on intervals of length 1/4 changes e by at most

    (1/8)(1/4)^2 sup_z |d^2/dz^2 exp(-2 pi i z)|
    = (1/8)(1/16)(4 pi^2) = pi^2/32.

(This interpolation bound follows by Taylor expansion at the
interpolated point and linear averaging of the two endpoint remainders;
it applies equally to complex-valued functions.) Therefore

    |I(u)| >= (1/d)(2/pi - pi^2/32) > 1/(8d).

(Numerical check: pi < 2 sqrt(3) gives 2/pi > 1/sqrt(3), and pi^2 < 12
gives pi^2/32 < 3/8; so 2/pi - pi^2/32 > 1/sqrt(3) - 3/8 > 1/8. The
upper bound |I| <= 1/d follows from |g| <= 1.)

Step 3: piecewise-polynomial representation of J. Let G be the
continuous primitive of g satisfying G(0) = 0. Because g is piecewise
linear with breakpoints at the quarter-integers, G's real and imaginary
parts are quadratic polynomials with rational coefficients on each
quarter interval. When eta != 0,

    I(u) = (G(u/d + eta) - G(u/d)) / (d eta).                    (iii)

The two arguments range in [-1/2, 3/2], so only a bounded number of
quarter-pieces occur, and their boundaries in u arise when either
argument is a quarter-integer. On each piece, I is a complex polynomial
in u of degree at most two; hence J is a rational polynomial of degree
at most four. If eta = 0, the identity I(u) = g(u/d)/d gives the same
conclusion (in fact degree at most two for J). All boundaries and
coefficients have O(n) bits and can be computed with a bounded number
of rational operations.

One point deserves note. In (iii), eta = (kd - jQ)/d, so every nonzero
eta has |eta| >= 1/d. The division by d eta in (iii) has no hidden
precision cost: no cancellation blow-up occurs because the numerator
has a matching lower bound.

Step 4: dyadic rounding upward. On each piece write
J(u) = sum_{q=0}^{4} a_q u^q, allowing zero coefficients, and set
D = Q B^8. Replace a_q by

    tilde_a_q = ceil(D a_q) / D.

Denote the resulting piecewise polynomial by tilde_J, assigning boundary
points consistently to one adjacent piece, and define

    U(d,j,u) = tilde_J(u) + 64/Q     for u = 0, 1, ..., d-1.     (iv)

Because u >= 0, rounding each coefficient upward increases the value
even when that coefficient is negative. Moreover,

    0 <= tilde_J(u) - J(u) <= (1 + u + u^2 + u^3 + u^4) / (Q B^8)
                           <= 5 B^4 / (Q B^8) = 5/(Q B^4) < 1/Q.

Together with (ii), this yields

    0 <= U(d,j,jt mod d) - P(d,j,t)
    = (tilde_J - J)(jt mod d) + (J(jt mod d) - P(d,j,t)) + 64/Q
    >= 0 + (-32/Q) + 64/Q = 32/Q,
    <= 1/Q + 32/Q + 64/Q = 97/Q <= 128/Q,

proving (a). It also gives

    (1/2) sum_{u=0}^{d-1} U(d,j,u)
    >= (1/2) sum_u (J(u) + 64/Q)
    >= (1/2) d (1/(64 d^2) + 64/Q) >= 1/(128 d),

where the middle step used the lower bound |I(u)| >= 1/(8d) from (ii),
and

    (1/2) sum_{u=0}^{d-1} U(d,j,u)
    <= (1/2) sum_u (J(u) + 1/Q + 64/Q)
    <= (1/2) d (1/d^2 + 65/Q)
    = 1/(2d) + 65 d / (2 Q)
    <= 1/2 + 65 B / (2 Q) <= 2,

using d < B and Q = B^16 (so 65 B / (2 Q) = 65/(2 B^15) < 1/2). This
proves (b) for the STATED U.

Step 5: computing S(d,j) without enumerating residues. U is a sum of a
bounded number of piecewise polynomials over integer ranges. Intersect
the pieces with integers 0, ..., d-1 using floors and ceilings,
assigning each boundary integer once. There are at most 17 pieces
(quarter intervals of [-1/2, 3/2] crossed with u/d, plus eta = 0).
Sum each piece by the power sums through degree four, computed
recursively from

    M^{q+1} = sum_{l=0}^{q} binom(q+1, l) sum_{u=0}^{M-1} u^l
                (0 <= q <= 4),

which follows from M^{q+1} = sum_{u=0}^{M-1}((u+1)^{q+1} - u^{q+1}).
The zeroth sum is M, with u^0 = 1 also at zero. Sums have O(n) bits
for M <= B. Since D = Q B^8 is a power of two, both U and S are
dyadic with O(n)-bit representations. The proof is complete. []

Lemma 12 lets us add the discrepancy between P and U by uniform random
choices. A further, smaller correction makes the mass per reduced
numerator exactly lambda(d). Neither the true order nor its
factorization needs to be known to run the trial; the additional
classical randomness is drawn in the same basis labels and retained by
Lemma 5.

For each reduced label, the natural scaling coefficient lambda(d)/S(d,j)
need not be dyadic. Rounding downward reserves the remaining mass
for the third mode.

Definition 13. For 1 <= d < B and 0 <= j < d with gcd(j, d) = 1, put

    c(d,j)   = 2^{-10n} * floor( 2^{10n} * lambda(d) / S(d,j) ),
    rho(d,j) = lambda(d) - c(d,j) * S(d,j).

From Lemma 12(b) and lambda(d) <= 2^{-10}/d (because lambda is
2^{-ceil(log_2 d) - 10} <= 2^{-log_2 d - 10} = 2^{-10}/d), we have

    0 <= c(d,j) <= lambda(d)/S(d,j) <= (2^{-10}/d)/(1/(128d)) = 1/8,
    0 <= rho(d,j) <= 2^{-10n} * S(d,j) <= 2 * 2^{-10n}.

Both c(d,j) and rho(d,j) are dyadic rationals of polynomial bit length,
and both are exactly computable from (d, j) in polynomial time.

Definition 14. The trial chooses one of three modes with probabilities
1/2, 1/4, 1/4 respectively. In EVERY mode, the label is first passed
through the validation predicate

    valid(d, j) := (1 <= d < B) AND (0 <= j < d)
                   AND (gcd(j, d) = 1) AND (a^d = 1 mod m).

An invalid label, or a failed retention test (below), produces NO
candidate. Only valid labels are ever returned.

  Mode 1 (probability 1/2). Run the quantum experiment of Lemma 10 on
  the state Q^{-1/2} sum_x |x> |a^x mod m>. Require its phase-register
  success. Recover the reduced label (d, j) from the observed phase
  output k via Lemma 11. Require valid(d, j), and retain the candidate
  d with probability c(d, j).

  Mode 2 (probability 1/4). Choose independent uniform n-bit labels
  d, j, t (so each triple has probability B^{-3}). Require t < d and
  valid(d, j). Retain the candidate d with probability

      2 B^3 * ( U(d, j, jt mod d) - P(d, j, t) ) * c(d, j).

  Mode 3 (probability 1/4). Choose independent uniform n-bit labels
  d, j (each pair probability B^{-2}). Require valid(d, j). Retain the
  candidate d with probability

      4 B^2 * rho(d, j).

These retention probabilities are all dyadic and in [0, 1]. Mode 1 is
in [0, 1/8] via c <= 1/8. Mode 2 is at most

    2 B^3 * (128/Q) * (1/8) = 32 B^{-13} <= 1

via (a) and c <= 1/8. Mode 3 is at most

    4 B^2 * (2 * 2^{-10n}) = 8 B^{-8} <= 1.

Each retention test is therefore implemented exactly by comparing fresh
fair bits with an integer numerator. The number of bits used and all
arithmetic and quantum costs are polynomial in n.

Proof of Proposition 8. Let r = ord_m(a). The exact mass is computed
the trial places on r, and check that every returned candidate is a
multiple of r.

Mass at a reduced label (r, j). Fix d = r and a reduced numerator j
with 0 <= j < r. The three modes contribute disjoint types of runs:
Mode 1 runs execute the quantum experiment, Mode 2 runs sample
classical triples, Mode 3 runs sample classical pairs. The mass at
(r, j) is the antichain sum (no run executes two modes).

Mode 1 contribution. By (***) with d = r, the probability that the
phase-register success occurs and the recovered label is (r, j) equals

    sum_{t=0}^{r-1} P(r, j, t).

The retention multiplies by c(r, j), and the mode has probability 1/2.
So Mode 1 contributes

    (1/2) * c(r, j) * sum_{t=0}^{r-1} P(r, j, t).

Mode 2 contribution. Each triple (d, j, t) = (r, j, t) has probability
1/B^3. The retention probability is
2 B^3 ( U(r, j, jt mod r) - P(r, j, t) ) c(r, j). The mode has
probability 1/4. So Mode 2 contributes

    (1/4) * sum_{t=0}^{r-1} [ (1/B^3) * 2 B^3
                              (U(r, j, jt mod r) - P(r, j, t)) c(r, j) ]
    = (1/2) * c(r, j) * sum_{t=0}^{r-1}
                            (U(r, j, jt mod r) - P(r, j, t)).

Mode 3 contribution. Each pair (d, j) = (r, j) has probability 1/B^2.
The retention is 4 B^2 rho(r, j), so Mode 3 contributes

    (1/4) * (1/B^2) * 4 B^2 rho(r, j) = rho(r, j).

Adding Mode 1 and Mode 2. Their P terms cancel:
    (1/2) c sum_t P + (1/2) c sum_t (U - P) = (1/2) c sum_t U.
Since gcd(j, r) = 1, the map t -> jt mod r permutes {0, ..., r-1}. Hence
sum_{t=0}^{r-1} U(r, j, jt mod r) = sum_{u=0}^{r-1} U(r, j, u) = 2 S(r, j),
and Mode 1 + Mode 2 = c(r, j) S(r, j). Then

    Mode 1 + Mode 2 + Mode 3 = c(r, j) S(r, j) + rho(r, j) = lambda(r).

This holds for every reduced j.

Sum over j. By Lemma 11, the bins for the true order are distinct: for
each reduced j with 0 <= j < r, the value k(r, j) is a distinct element
of {0, ..., Q-1}. So the total mass at d = r is the sum over phi(r)
reduced numerators, giving

    Pr(trial returns r) = sum_j lambda(r) = phi(r) * lambda(r).

The case r = 1 is included: its sole reduced label is j = 0.

Multiple guarantee. Every returned candidate has passed the validation
predicate valid(d, j), whose conjunction includes a^d = 1 mod m. Since
r is the least positive integer with this property, r | d. A return
with no candidate is also allowed by the statement.

Polynomial cost. All arithmetic is exact: P, U, S, c, rho are dyadic
with polynomial bit length, and integer arithmetic on them is
polynomial. The quantum part is Lemma 10, applied a bounded number
of times. The classical checks (validation, retention comparisons,
summation over phi(r) numerators) are polynomial bit operations. There
is no dependence on r or on its factorization. []

Remark. The error term in (a) is not zero, but at false labels d != r
the trial makes no claim about P(d, j, t): validation rejects those
labels before any probability is used. At the true label r, (**) is a
genuine quantum probability, and the trial's mass at r is exact.

The order trial is next organized into a factoring process. Its
successful output contains enough auxiliary information to compute,
retrospectively, the exact probabilities of all its useful transitions.
This information is produced by the process itself; it is not supplied
as advice.

Throughout this section, n and B = 2^n retain their values for the
original input N, even on smaller integers. Take K
repetitions within each randomized transition, at most L transition
slots, and W bits per transition output, where

    K = n^5,   L = n^10,   W = n(K + 1).

Definition 15. For M >= 2, define D(M) as the following rooted
occurrence tree.

  - The root records M and its distinct prime factors in increasing
    order, together with their positive exponents.
  - For each distinct odd prime p in that factorization, it has one
    child containing D(p - 1); the children are ordered by p.
  - Equal labels under different parents are separate occurrences.
  - There is no child for p = 2.

Every descent decreases the label, so this defines unique finite data.
Write D = D(N).

Example. The root of D(21) has children D(2) and D(6), associated with
its prime factors 3 and 7. The second child has its own D(2) child,
associated with the prime factor 3 of 6. The two occurrences of D(2)
remain separate.

The recursive p - 1 structure recalls Pratt's succinct primality
certificates [Pratt]. Here there are no primitive-root witness fields:
prime labels are checked by Lemma 20, and the auxiliary factorizations
determine exact orders and transition probabilities retrospectively. The
order-to-gcd splitting mechanism follows the Miller-Shor reduction
[Miller, Shor]; the required unconditional counting argument is
included in Section 4.7.

Proposition 16 (A process with verifiable transition probabilities).
There is a uniformly polynomial-time process, using the order trial of
Proposition 8, with the following properties.

  (i) It either aborts or generates D. A deterministic polynomial-time
  verifier accepts exactly these complete data. The classical controller
  makes at most L transitions, and completed runs are padded to exactly
  L by constant-output dummy transitions. Each transition output has a
  fixed W-bit encoding.

  (ii) Given D and the recorded classical input of a transition, one
  can compute a good-output predicate, one canonical good encoding, and
  the exact probability s that an ordinary execution of that transition
  has a good output. The probability s is dyadic, has polynomial bit
  length, and obeys s >= 1 - 2^{-2n}. For a dummy transition, its
  constant output is good and canonical, and s = 1.

  (iii) Every evolution whose transition outputs are all good
  completes the tasks and generates D without aborting or reaching a
  resource cap. This assertion also holds when good outputs are
  supplied directly. The work is polynomially bounded on arbitrary
  output sequences, with malformed outputs causing an abort.

Only the transition output is included in its W-bit encoding. Internal
order-trial registers and recorded computational history are separate
workspace and may be retained.

The W-bit output is the string consumed by the controller. The completed
tree supplies the arithmetic information for later tests, while the
separate history and quantum work registers are retained for the inverse
circuit. These are three different records; the output width does not
bound the whole workspace.

Suppose that a subfactor m is being split while factoring a node M.
From D, m can be factored by division from the prime list for M. Moreover,
D supplies the factorization of p - 1 for every odd prime p | m. Thus D
supplies the factorization of

    phi(m) = prod_{p^e || m} (p - 1) p^{e-1}.                    (4.1)

Here the formula follows by counting units modulo a prime power and
using the Chinese remainder bijection; the latter follows from Bezout's
identity. The contribution from p = 2 needs no child.

Given this factorization and a unit a modulo m, its order is computable
exactly.

  - Start with exponent v = phi(m).
  - For each prime q in its known factorization, repeatedly replace v
    by v/q while q | v and a^{v/q} = 1 mod m.
  - The final v is the order, and the remaining prime exponents give
    its factorization.

The order divides phi(m) by the coset proof of Lagrange's theorem.
Throughout the procedure it divides v, and at termination no excess
prime factor can remain. Hence the final v is ord_m(a). There are
polynomially many modular powerings. The same procedure applies to each
prime-power component of m.

These retrospective computations are used to evaluate
recorded transitions once the data have been generated.

The elementary bound

    phi(d) / d >= 1/(n + 1)   for 1 <= d < B.                   (4.2)

Indeed, if p_1 < p_2 < ... < p_k are the distinct prime divisors of d,
then k <= n and p_i >= i + 1, so the product formula gives

    phi(d)/d = prod_{i=1}^k (1 - 1/p_i)
             >= prod_{i=1}^k (1 - 1/(i + 1))
             = prod_{i=1}^k i/(i + 1) = 1/(k + 1) >= 1/(n + 1).

The empty product covers d = 1.

To factor a node, apply the deterministic primality test in Lemma 20
to each current factor. A prime is recorded; an even composite can be
split by 2; and a composite perfect power can be split by finding an
integral root. Root searches for exponents at most n take polynomial
time. Consequently the remaining case is an odd m having at least two
distinct prime divisors. Two kinds of transition
for this case.

Encoding. Let b_m = ceil(log_2 m), so m <= 2^{b_m} < 2m. A list output
consists of K fields of b_m bits, followed by zero padding to width W;
an order output consists of one n-bit integer followed by zero padding
to width W. A dummy output is the all-zero W-bit string. The controller
rejects malformed padding, and every good-output predicate below
requires a valid encoding.

List transition. It generates K independent uniform b_m-bit integers.
Entries outside [0, m) and nonunits are ignored. A list is GOOD if some
usable entry a has unequal values of

    v_2( ord_{p^e}(a) ),   p^e || m,

where v_2 is the exponent of 2 in a positive integer's factorization.
(The prime 2 contributes no component to the comparison, because there
is no order modulo a power of 2 in D(M).)

A canonical good list has first entry equal to the Chinese remainder
residue that is 1 on the component corresponding to the smallest prime
and -1 on all other components, followed by zeros. Its first entry has
2-adic levels 0 on the smallest-prime component and 1 on every other,
so unequal by inspection. No entry beyond the first is usable, so this
canonical list spawns one order transition.

Order transition. For each usable entry a in a list, run K independent
order trials of Proposition 8. Output the smallest returned candidate,
or 0 if there was none. If r = ord_m(a), goodness means the output is
r, which is also the canonical good output for this input.

Dummy transition. Outputs the all-zero W-bit string. Its good predicate
is constantly true and its canonical good output is the empty field.

Splitting. For every positive even order output r', try
gcd(a^{r'/2} - 1, m) and accept it only if it is a proper nontrivial
divisor. If no entry produces a split, abort. Section 4.7 will show
that an all-good run always finds a split.

The good probability is now computed of the list transition without
enumerating residues. The key fact is that a uniform unit modulo m has
all 2-adic levels equal with probability at most 1/2.

Cyclic structure of F_p. Let p be an odd prime with p^e || m. The
nonzero elements of F_p form a cyclic group.

Proof. Let T be the exponent of F_p^*: the least T with x^T = 1 for all
x != 0. Lagrange gives T | p - 1. The exponent of a finite abelian
group is attained: for each prime power q^k || T, an element whose
order contains q^k yields, after taking a power, an element of order
exactly q^k; the product of these has order T. But X^T - 1 has at most
T roots in F_p, and every nonzero element is a root, so p - 1 <= T.
Thus T = p - 1 and F_p^* is cyclic.

Two-adic levels in F_p. Write p - 1 = h 2^l with h odd. In a cyclic
group of order p - 1, the count of elements with v_2(order) = t is

    c_p(0) = h,    c_p(t) = 2^{t-1} h   for 1 <= t <= l.

The sum is h(1 + 1 + 2 + ... + 2^{l-1}) = h 2^l = p - 1. The maximum
is at t = l, with c_p(l) = 2^{l-1} h = (p - 1)/2. So no level has more
than half the nonzero elements.

Lifting to p^e. The reduction map (Z/p^e)^* -> (Z/p)^* has kernel of
size p^{e-1}, which is odd. So the order of any lift has the same
2-adic level as the reduction. The level counts modulo p^e are
therefore p^{e-1} times the counts modulo p:

    p^{e-1} h,  p^{e-1} h,  2 p^{e-1} h,  ...,  2^{l-1} p^{e-1} h.

The maximum is 2^{l-1} p^{e-1} h = phi(p^e)/2, again exactly half the
units modulo p^e.

Independence under CRT. The Chinese remainder bijection makes
(Z/m)^* isomorphic to prod_i (Z/p_i^{e_i})^*. For a uniform unit mod m,
the level of the i-th component is independently distributed as above.

All-equal count. Let c_i(t) be the count of level t in the i-th
component, phi_i = phi(p_i^{e_i}), and q_i(t) = c_i(t)/phi_i, so q_i(t)
is the level-t probability for component i. Since at least two
components exist (the odd m with >= 2 distinct primes case),
the number of units a mod m whose levels are ALL equal to t is
prod_i c_i(t). The total all-equal count is

    sum_{t} prod_i c_i(t) = phi(m) * sum_{t} prod_i q_i(t).

For each t, prod_i q_i(t) <= q_1(t) q_2(t) because every factor is in
[0, 1]. Counting,

    sum_t prod_i q_i(t) <= sum_t q_1(t) q_2(t)
                        <= (max_t q_1(t)) * (sum_t q_2(t))
                        <= (1/2) * 1 = 1/2.

Hence sum_t prod_i c_i(t) <= phi(m)/2. Define

    F = phi(m) - sum_t prod_i c_i(t)  >=  phi(m)/2.

F is exactly the count of units with at least one unequal level pair.

Good probability. A single uniform b_m-bit integer is a unit with
unequal levels with probability F/2^{b_m}. Across K independent
draws,

    s = 1 - (1 - F/2^{b_m})^K.

By (4.2), phi(m) >= m/(n+1), and by definition 2^{b_m} < 2m, so

    F/2^{b_m} >= phi(m)/(2 * 2^{b_m}) >= m/(2 * 2m * (n+1))
                                        = 1/(4(n+1)).

Hence with K = n^5,

    s >= 1 - (1 - 1/(4(n+1)))^{n^5}
      >= 1 - exp(-n^5/(4(n+1)))
      >= 1 - exp(-n^4/8)
      >= 1 - 2^{-2n}    (for n >= 128).

The denominator of s divides 2^{nK}, so s has at most nK <= n^6 bits.

Let m be an odd composite with at least two distinct prime divisors,
let a be a usable entry (a unit mod m), and let r = ord_m(a). The order
transition runs K trials of Proposition 8 and outputs the smallest
returned candidate, or 0 if there was none. Goodness means the output
is r, which is also the canonical good output.

By Proposition 8, every candidate is a positive multiple of r, and
the probability that a single trial returns r is phi(r) lambda(r).
Therefore

    s = 1 - (1 - phi(r) lambda(r))^K.

Bound. Using (4.2) with d = r, phi(r) >= r/(n+1). And

    lambda(r) = 2^{-ceil(log_2 r) - 10}.

Since 2^{ceil(log_2 r)} < 2r, it follows that
lambda(r) > 2^{-log_2 r - 11} = 2^{-11}/r. Therefore

    phi(r) lambda(r) > (r/(n+1)) * (2^{-11}/r) = 1/(2048 (n+1)).

Then with K = n^5,

    s >= 1 - (1 - 1/(2048(n+1)))^{n^5}
      >= 1 - exp(-n^5/(2048(n+1)))
      >= 1 - exp(-n^4/4096)
      >= 1 - 2^{-2n}    (for n >= 128).

Denominator. Since r < B = 2^n, phi(r) < 2^n. And
lambda(r) = 2^{-ceil(log_2 r) - 10} with ceil(log_2 r) <= n. So
phi(r) lambda(r) has denominator dividing 2^{n+10}. Then s has
denominator dividing 2^{(n+10)K}, so s has at most (n+10)K = O(n^6) bits.

Suppose the list is good (some usable entry has unequal levels) and
every order transition on its usable entries is good (each returns r_a
= ord_m(a) for its input a). The split procedure finds a
proper nontrivial divisor of m.

Choose an entry a with unequal levels. Let r = ord_m(a); this is the
output of the good order transition on a. Write p_i^{e_i} || m for the
distinct odd prime powers and r_i = ord_{p_i^{e_i}}(a). Then

    r = lcm_i r_i,   and so   v_2(r) = max_i v_2(r_i).

Let M = { i : v_2(r_i) = v_2(r) } be the highest-level set. By
definition M is nonempty. Since levels are unequal, some i has
v_2(r_i) < v_2(r), so the complement of M is also nonempty. Since the
levels are unequal and every level is >= 0, the maximum v_2(r) is at
least 1; otherwise all levels would be 0 and equal. So r is even.

Compute a^{r/2} modulo each p_i^{e_i}.

  - If i is in the complement of M (that is, v_2(r_i) < v_2(r)):
    then r/r_i is even, so r_i | r/2, and a^{r/2} = 1 mod p_i^{e_i}.
  - If i is in M (that is, v_2(r_i) = v_2(r)): then r/r_i is odd, say
    r/r_i = 2k + 1. Then r = (2k+1) r_i and
    r/2 = k r_i + r_i/2. Therefore
    a^{r/2} = (a^{r_i})^k * a^{r_i/2} = a^{r_i/2} mod p_i^{e_i}.
    The element a^{r_i/2} has order 2 modulo p_i^{e_i} (its square is
    a^{r_i} = 1, and it is not 1 since ord(a) = r_i). The only square
    roots of 1 modulo an odd prime power are +1 and -1: in
    (x-1)(x+1) = 0 mod p_i^{e_i}, the entire odd prime power p_i^{e_i}
    divides one factor. So a^{r_i/2} = -1 mod p_i^{e_i}.

Assembling by CRT, a^{r/2} is +1 on the complement of M and -1 on M.

Now gcd(a^{r/2} - 1, m). On a component i in the complement of M, the
value a^{r/2} - 1 is 1 - 1 = 0 mod p_i^{e_i}, so p_i^{e_i} divides the
gcd. On a component i in M, a^{r/2} - 1 is -1 - 1 = -2 mod p_i^{e_i},
which is coprime to p_i^{e_i} (since p_i is odd). Hence the gcd is
exactly the product of p_i^{e_i} over the complement of M.

Since both M and its complement are nonempty, this product is a
nonempty proper subproduct of the prime power factorization of m, so it
is a proper nontrivial divisor of m. Therefore an all-good run always
produces a split.

The following bounds hold for any output sequence, good or bad.

Binary splitting tree per node. For one node M, the binary splitting
tree has at most log_2 M leaves: every leaf is a prime >= 2 and their
product is M. A partial run (starting from M and splitting) has at
most log_2 M + 1 split attempts as well, counting a possible final
failed attempt.

Auxiliary tree. At each auxiliary-tree descent,

    sum_{p | M, p > 2} log_2(p - 1) <= sum_{p | M} log_2 p <= log_2 M.

Hence the total logarithmic size at any depth of D is at most
log_2 N < n.

Halving. In two successive descents M -> p - 1 -> q - 1, q > 2 is an
odd prime divisor of p - 1, so q <= (p - 1)/2 and

    q - 1 < (p - 1)/2 < p/2 <= M/2.

Level count. After every two descents the label halves. Since every
label is at least 2, the tree has fewer than 2n + 2 levels.

Node count. Since every node contributes at least 1 to the log, the
number of node occurrences per level is at most n; the total number of
node occurrences is at most (2n + 2) n.

Transition count. Summing the split-attempt bound log_2 M over all
node occurrences gives at most (2n + 2) n split tasks. Each split
consumes at most K + 1 transitions (a list transition plus up to K
order transitions). Therefore

    # { list and order transitions }
    <= (2n + 2) n (K + 1) <= 6 n^7 < n^10 = L.

All deterministic work and all K trials within an order transition
have polynomial bounds.

The complete data are polynomially verifiable. The verifier is a
deterministic classical algorithm that accepts exactly the legitimate
trees D.

Structural checks. Require the root label to be N. Before any
arithmetic, reject node labels outside [2, B), proposed prime labels
outside [2, M] at a node M, exponents outside [1, n], and any list or
tree exceeding the polynomial size caps (2n + 2) n node occurrences and
n entries per node.

Arithmetic checks. At a node M, require strictly increasing prime
labels, check primality using Lemma 20, and check the factorization's
product. A partial product may be rejected as soon as it exceeds M;
this keeps the verification polynomial even on arbitrary invalid
records.

Children. Then require exactly the ordered children p - 1 for the
distinct odd primes p in that node's list, and apply the same checks
recursively. Reject missing or extra children and malformed or
oversized records.

Enumeration of accepted trees. Unique prime factorization implies that
an accepted tree is exactly D(N). Only the operational split history
(the order in which splits occurred, and which entries produced them)
may vary. The all-good splitting argument (Section 4.7) ensures that
every all-good evolution supplies such a complete tree.

Bounded storage. Each node's prime list has at most n entries of
polynomial bit length, so the node-occurrence bound also bounds the
storage for the complete data. A list has at most nK unpadded bits,
and an order value (including zero) has n bits. Hence the specified
encodings fit within W bits and give each canonical output a unique
string. (The controller depends only on these classical outputs; the
internal quantum work can be retained without being treated as part of
an output.)

Assembling the pieces of the preceding subsections.

(i) Abort or generate D. A run either terminates by submitting a
well-formed tree D' (accepted by 4.9) or aborts. By 4.7, each node's
good list yields at least one split; the recursion terminates because
labels strictly decrease. Completed runs are padded to exactly L by

(ii) Good predicate, canonical output, and exact s. Given D and the
recorded classical input of a transition, the good predicate, the

  - List transition: good predicate and canonical list are stated in
    4.4; s is computed by 4.5 as a dyadic with s >= 1 - 2^{-2n}.
  - Order transition: good predicate and canonical output are stated
    in 4.4; s is computed by 4.6 as a dyadic with s >= 1 - 2^{-2n}.
  - Dummy transition: s = 1 by 4.4.

Denominators are bounded by 2^{nK} and 2^{(n+10)K} respectively, so
they have polynomial bit length.

(iii) All-good runs complete and generate D. Suppose every transition
output is good. Then every list has an unequal-level entry, and every

resulting gcd is a proper nontrivial divisor of m. Recurse until all

(4.8). The assertion also holds when canonical good outputs are
supplied directly: the canonical list splits, the canonical

established by 4.4-4.9. For arbitrary (not necessarily good) output
sequences, malformed encodings abort immediately, and bad outputs can
only shorten a run or waste one useful attempt; the L-transition cap

This proves all three parts of Proposition 16.

The process in Proposition 16 has two properties. Every transition
has a high probability of a good output, and a completed run supplies
the data needed to calculate that probability exactly. We now use
these data, but only after they have been generated. The aim is a
preparation whose success probability is known BEFORE it runs.

There are two normalization stages. First, every transition will have
the same retained mass z, so L slots have known success probability z^L.
Second, a further correction applied to the complete preparation will
make that probability exactly 1/4, and one exact amplification step will
make it 1.

Lemma 17 (Dyadic completion). Let W be a nonnegative integer. Consider
an ordinary transition with output in {0,1}^W, a designated set of good
outputs of dyadic probability 0 < s <= 1, and a specified good output
y_0. Let delta, z be dyadic rationals with 0 < delta < 1 and
0 < z <= A := (1 - delta) s, and suppose delta = 2^{-t} for a positive
integer t. With probability 1 - delta run the ordinary transition, and
otherwise guess a uniform W-bit output. For E = W + t + 2, define

    C = 2^{-E} * floor( 2^E * z / A ),
    R = z - C A.

In the ordinary branch require a good output and retain with
probability C. In the guessing branch require the particular output
y_0 and retain with probability R 2^W / delta. These are valid dyadic
retention probabilities, and the total probability of passing is
exactly z. All computations are polynomial-time when the dyadic inputs
and the integers W, t are polynomially bounded.

Proof. Since z <= A, 0 <= z/A <= 1, hence 0 <= C <= 1.
Downward rounding gives

    0 <= R = z - CA < A * 2^{-E} <= 2^{-E}.

Consequently the guessing-branch retention R 2^W / delta satisfies

    0 <= R 2^W / delta < 2^{-E} 2^W / 2^{-t}
                       = 2^{W + t - (W + t + 2)} = 2^{-2} = 1/4 < 1.

The two passing masses are:

  - ordinary branch: (1 - delta) * s * C = AC.
  - guessing branch: delta * 2^{-W} * (R 2^W / delta) = R.

Their sum is AC + R = AC + (z - CA) = z.

Although z/A need not itself be dyadic, the scaled floor
floor(2^E z/A) is obtained by exact integer division of two integers of
polynomial bit length whenever z and A are dyadic with polynomial
representations. Every probability that is actually sampled (C and
R 2^W / delta) is a dyadic rational. The values of C, R, and their
denominators have polynomial bit length. []

Use the process, output width, and slot bound of Proposition 16:

    K = n^5,   L = n^10,   W = n(K + 1).

Every completed execution has exactly L slots, with constant-output
dummy transitions after its real work. Their good probability is 1. Set

    delta = 2^{-2n},   z = 1 - 2^{-n},   E = W + 2n + 2.

(So t = 2n.) At each slot, run the ordinary transition with probability
1 - delta, and otherwise guess its W-bit output uniformly. Record the
branch and the output. Subsequent deterministic computation uses that
output in the usual way. Malformed encodings may abort, and a proposed
divisor is always checked before a split is made. In an ordinary order
transition the internal trial registers are retained separately; the
guessed string encodes only the transition's classical output.

The actual algorithm does not yet test whether these outputs are good.
It first attempts to complete all the factorizations defining D(N).
If it aborts or its generated data fail the complete verifier in
Section 4.9, it sets its success flag to zero. Otherwise, for every
recorded slot it reconstructs the transition inputs and computes, from
the verified data, the good-output predicate, its probability s, and
the canonical output y_0. It then applies the two filters of Lemma 17,
using fresh independent dyadic coins. Success requires every filter
to pass.

The filters are well-defined. Writing epsilon = 2^{-n}, the transition
bound s >= 1 - 2^{-2n} gives

    (1 - delta) s >= (1 - epsilon^2)^2
                   = 1 - 2 epsilon^2 + epsilon^4
                   >= 1 - epsilon = z,

where the last step uses epsilon - 2 epsilon^2 + epsilon^4 > 0 for the
present range (n >= 128, epsilon <= 2^{-128}). The same bound holds in
dummy slots. Exact dyadic sampling is provided by Lemma 4.

Proposition 18 (Known probability from deferred filters). The
implemented preparation just described has success probability exactly

    p_* = (1 - 2^{-n})^{n^{10}}.

Every successful branch outputs the correct prime factorization of N.
The preparation has polynomial worst-case time and workspace, and
p_* > 1/2 is an initially known dyadic with polynomial bit length.

Proof. Let D(N) be the true auxiliary tree. It is fixed for the
analysis only; the algorithm never sees it, and uses only its own
generated data D' after completion.

Two descriptions of acceptance. Let h = (h_1, ..., h_L) denote a
recorded controller run with h_i = (branch_i, output_i) for each slot
i in {1, ..., L}. Let xi = (xi_1, ..., xi_L) denote fresh retention
coins, one per slot, drawn after completion.

  Implemented event A(h, xi). The run completes, verification accepts
  its generated data D', and every deferred filter using D', the
  recorded outputs, and xi passes.

  Analytical event T(h, xi). All L slots are recorded and pass their
  tests against the true data D(N) using the same outputs and xi.
  Concretely, for each i:
    - if h_i is (ordinary, y), then y is good against D(N) (that is,
      good_predicate(D(N), input_i, y) = 1), and xi_i passes for the
      threshold C(D(N), input_i);
    - if h_i is (guess, y), then y = y_0(D(N), input_i), and xi_i
      passes for the threshold R(D(N), input_i) 2^W / delta.
  These tests are computed with D(N) only for their deferred
  THRESHOLDS; these do not alter the controller's evolution.

Claim 1: T implies A. If T holds, every recorded output is good
against D(N) (each ordinary-branch output passed its good predicate,
and each guess-branch output equals its canonical y_0, which is good).
By Proposition 16(iii) the controller completes and generates D(N).
Verification (4.9) accepts D(N). The deferred filters computed from
D' = D(N) use the same outputs and coins as in T, so the two coincide
with T's tests and all pass. Thus A.

Claim 2: A implies T. If A holds, verification accepts some D'. By
4.9, accepted trees are exactly D(N) (by unique prime factorization),
so D' = D(N). Then the deferred filters used D(N), getting exactly
the corresponding true-data tests on the same outputs and coins. Thus
T.

So T = A as events on the SAME probability space (h from the run's
internal randomness, xi fresh).

Computing P(T). Structural induction over i from 1 to L. The
induction hypothesis is: for any prefix h_{<i} = (h_1, ..., h_{i-1})
whose own slots all pass (in the T sense), the conditional probability
that slot i passes (in the T sense) equals z. Later slots are
independent of this slot given the prefix; the product over i from 1
to L is z^L.

Base step (i = 1). The controller's first slot is chosen by
Proposition 16 (real transition on N, or dummy). By Lemma 6 (fresh
registers), any quantum work from elsewhere is in a fixed state and
this slot's distribution depends only on its classical input. By Lemma
5.1 with the D(N)-based s, C, R, y_0, the pass probability is z.

Inductive step (i > 1). Condition on h_{<i} all-passing. The
controller is in a well-defined state: either a real transition (with
fixed input (m, or (m,a)) determined by the prefix) or a dummy. By
Lemma 6 and Lemma 17 as above, the pass probability for slot i
equals z, independent of xi_i.

Therefore P(T) = z^L = (1 - 2^{-n})^L = (1 - 2^{-n})^{n^{10}}.

Correctness of output. On any successful branch, verification
accepted the D' = D(N) generated. Its root prime list is exactly the
factorization of N, by the D labels and factorization checks in 4.9.
So the output is correct.

Polynomial bounds. Each of the L conditional pass probabilities is a
dyadic with denominators dividing 2^E where E = W + 2n + 2 =
O(n^6), or from the transition's own s with denominators dividing
2^{(n+10)K} = 2^{n^6 + 10 n^5}. Both denominators have polynomial
bit length. Evaluating the deferred tests over L slots, using the
retained histories, costs polynomial time and workspace. The output
string is polynomially long. This completes the proof. []

Final bound. p_* = (1 - 2^{-n})^{n^{10}}. Bernoulli gives
(1 - 2^{-n})^{n^{10}} >= 1 - n^{10} 2^{-n}. At n = 128 the subtracted
term is 2^{-58}, and the function x^{10} 2^{-x} is decreasing for real
x >= 128 because its logarithmic derivative 10/x - ln 2 is negative
there. So p_* > 1 - 2^{-58} > 1/2. Its denominator is 2^{n^{11}}, whose
bit length n^{11} is polynomial in n. []

The target 1/4 is the one-step exact-amplification case of the
standard reflection formula ([BHMT] Section 2), used explicitly for
exact transforms by Mosca-Zalka ([MZ] Section 2.1). The task here is
to reach that probability with the fixed gates; the remaining rounding
is independent of the factoring process.

Encoding. Encode the root factorization by n entries of n bits each:
positive primes in nondecreasing order with multiplicity, followed by
zeros. Put J = n^2. Because N < 2^n, there are fewer than n prime
factors with multiplicity, and each prime is below 2^n. Exactly one
J-bit string is valid. Lemma 20, the ordering check, and the product
check recognize it in polynomial time. The product of an arbitrary
guessed list has at most n^2 bits, so this verification is also
polynomial on invalid guesses.

Lemma 19 (Quarter-success preparation). There is a polynomial-size
uniform unitary V_N whose flag is 1 with probability exactly 1/4 and
whose flagged output is always the fixed encoding of the prime
factorization of N.

Proof. With probability 1/2, run Proposition 18 and retain its success
with the further probability

    c_* = 2^{-(J+2)} * floor( 2^{J+2} / (2 p_*) ).

With probability 1/2, guess a uniform J-bit string, require it to be
the valid root factor list, and retain with probability

    2^{J+1} e,    e = 1/4 - p_* c_* / 2.

Since 1/2 < p_* <= 1, 0 <= p_* c_* <= 1/2, hence 0 <= c_* <= 1
and e >= 0. Downward rounding gives

    0 <= e < p_* 2^{-(J+3)},    0 <= 2^{J+1} e <= 1/4.

Both retentions are valid polynomial-bit dyadics.

The first branch contributes p_* c_* / 2 to the success probability.
The second branch: the unique valid guess has probability 2^{-J}, its
retention is 2^{J+1} e, and the branch probability is 1/2, so the
contribution is (1/2) * 2^{-J} * 2^{J+1} e = e. Their sum is

    p_* c_* / 2 + e = 1/4.

Place the same fixed-encoding root factor list in the output register
on either successful branch. All work and flags are retained by
Lemma 5, giving the unitary V_N. []

Proof of Theorem 1. Let psi = V_N |0>, let Pi project onto flag 1, and
put g_* = Pi psi. By Lemma 19, ||g_*||^2 = 1/4. First apply the sign
flip I - 2 Pi, then the reflection 2 |psi><psi| - I. Since

    <psi | psi - 2 g_*> = 1 - 2 ||g_*||^2 = 1/2,

the resulting state is

    (2 |psi><psi| - I)(psi - 2 g_*) = 2 g_*.

It has norm one and lies entirely in the successful subspace. Every
basis outcome in that subspace has the correct factor list, so
measuring the output gives the factorization with certainty.

  - For fixed classical input N, the reflection around psi is

        V_N (2 |0><0| - I) V_N^{-1},

    where the middle test concerns all initially zero workspace, and
    the read-only input is held fixed. Its sign convention may differ
    by an irrelevant global minus sign from a sign flip on zero.
  - A circuit for inputs of a fixed bit length performs these same

Equation above is a single amplification step at the exactly known

The factorization procedure needs an exact test for its prime leaves
and for the prime factors appearing in its certificates. We give the
required test here, including its correctness proof. It is a
convenient variant of the polynomial-congruence method of
Agrawal-Kayal-Saxena ([AKS] Section 4); the auxiliary prime and the
constants used below are proven directly.

Lemma 20. There is a uniform deterministic algorithm that, given
integers n >= 128 and 2 <= m < 2^n, decides whether m is prime using a
number of bit operations polynomial in n.

Proof. The algorithm has four steps.

  1. Reject m if it is a perfect power b^e with integers b, e >= 2.
  2. Find a prime s <= n^8 such that
       gcd(s, m) = 1   and   ord_s(m) > n^2.
  3. If m <= 8s, decide primality by trial division and stop.
     Otherwise, reject if any integer from 2 to 8s divides m.
  4. Accept if and only if all the congruences
       (X + a)^m = X^m + a   mod (m, X^s - 1),   1 <= a <= 8s,
     hold.

To be proved: (i) the search in step 2 succeeds, (ii) the algorithm is
correct, (iii) the running time is polynomial.

(i) Existence of s. Let R = 2 floor(n^8/2), and let
P_R = prod_{l <= R, l prime} l be the product of primes at most R.
The central binomial coefficient C = binom(R, R/2) satisfies
C >= 2^R / (R + 1). For every prime l, the factorial valuation formula
gives

    v_l(C) = sum_{j >= 1} ( floor(R/l^j) - 2 floor(R/(2 l^j)) )
           <= floor(log_l R).

Each summand is 0 or 1, and each term vanishes when l^j > R. Hence,
writing pi(R) for the count of primes <= R,

    log_2 C <= pi(R) log_2 R <= (log_2 P_R) log_2 R,

where the second inequality uses log_2 P_R >= pi(R) (since each l >= 2
contributes log_2 l >= 1). Combining with the lower bound on C:

    log_2 P_R >= (R - log_2(R + 1)) / log_2 R > n^6.

For the last inequality: 8 log_2 n + 1 < n for n >= 128 (the bound
holds at n = 128 and is monotonic in n), so a short computation gives
(R - log_2(R+1)) / log_2 R > (n^8 - 1 - n) / n > n^6.

On the other hand,

    log_2 (m * prod_{j=1}^{n^2} (m^j - 1)) < n (1 + n^2(n^2+1)/2) < n^6.

Therefore some prime s <= R divides P_R but not m and not any m^j - 1
with 1 <= j <= n^2. (The condition for all j = 1, ..., n^2 that s does
not divide m^j - 1, together with gcd(s, m) = 1, is exactly
ord_s(m) > n^2.) Note that s = 2 cannot be chosen: for s = 2 the group
(Z/2)^* is trivial, so ord_2(m) = 1 < n^2. The search can therefore
enumerate primes s = 3, 5, 7, ... up to R and try each; at least one
qualifies.

(ii) Correctness. Primes pass the perfect-power and trial-division
checks. If m is prime, the binomial theorem in characteristic m gives
(X + a)^m = X^m + a^m = X^m + a mod m, so the congruences also hold.

Suppose conversely that a composite m is accepted. It is not a perfect
power, and every prime p dividing it satisfies p > 8s. Choose one such
p. In an algebraic extension of F_p, choose a root w of
1 + X + ... + X^{s-1}. Its value at 1 is s != 0 in F_p (since
gcd(s, m) = 1 and p | m, gives s != p; and s is prime), so w != 1.
Since s is prime, w has multiplicative order exactly s.

For a product f of the tested linear factors X + a, reduction of the
congruence modulo p gives f(X)^m = f(X^m) mod (X^s - 1). Frobenius
gives f(X)^p = f(X^p) mod (X^s - 1). These identities compose:
substitution X -> X^b preserves the ideal (X^s - 1), so identities for
exponents b, c imply the identity for the product bc. Hence, for all
nonnegative integers u, v,

    f(w)^k = f(w^k),   k = m^u p^v.

Let H be the subgroup of (Z/sZ)^* generated by the residues of m and
p, and put h = |H|. By the choice of s, n^2 < h <= s - 1. The
nonnegative powers m^u p^v already range over H, because inverses in a
finite group are nonnegative powers.

Incompatible lower and upper bounds on the number of
values f(w). These bounds adapt the root-counting arguments in
[AKS] Lemmas 4.7-4.8 and their footnotes.

Lower bound. Consider all products of h - 1 distinct factors from
X + 1, ..., X + 8s. The factors are pairwise distinct over F_p
because p > 8s, so different subsets give different monic polynomials.
If two such polynomials f_1, f_2 had the same value at w, then the
propagation identity would give f_1(w^k) = f_2(w^k) for every k mod s
in H. This would give h distinct roots of the nonzero polynomial
f_1 - f_2, whose degree is at most h - 1. Contradiction. Thus all
these values are distinct, and their number is at least

    binom(8s, h - 1) >= (8s/(h-1))^{h-1} >= 8^{h-1}.

Here binom(M, t) >= (M/t)^t follows by comparing each factor
(M - i)/(t - i) with M/t. No division by f(w) has been used: indeed
f(w) = 0 is itself impossible, since otherwise the propagation
identity would give h roots of the nonzero polynomial f, of degree
h - 1.

Upper bound. Put q = floor(sqrt(h)), so (q+1)^2 > h. Among the
(q+1)^2 > h pairs (u, v) with 0 <= u, v <= q, two distinct pairs give
integers m^u p^v that coincide modulo s. Call them k_1 and k_2. These
are different as integers: since m is composite and not a perfect
power, it has at least two distinct prime divisors, so there is a
prime p' != p dividing m. Equality of k_1 = m^u p^v and
k_2 = m^{u'} p^{v'} would force u = u' by taking p'-adic valuations of
both sides, and then v = v'.

Now w^{k_1} = w^{k_2}, so the propagation identity makes every value
counted in the lower bound a root of the nonzero polynomial
T^{k_1} - T^{k_2}. Its degree is at most

    max(k_1, k_2) <= m^{2q} < 2^{2nq} <= 2^{2n sqrt(h)} < 2^{2h}.

But the lower bound is at least 8^{h-1} = 2^{3h-3} > 2^{2h}, since
h > n^2 >= 128^2. Contradiction. So composite m is rejected.

(iii) Running time. Every exponent in a representation m = b^e with
b >= 2 satisfies e < n, so binary search for integer roots with
exponents 2, ..., n implements step 1 in polynomial time. The search
for s uses trial division to recognize primes up to n^8, followed by
gcd and successive modular powers to test the condition. These
operations are polynomial in n and do not use the primality test under
construction, so no circularity. The trial divisions involving m are

For the congruences: elements of (Z/mZ)[X]/(X^s - 1) are represented
by s coefficients of at most n bits each. Schoolbook cyclic
multiplication and repeated squaring test each of the 8s congruences
using polynomially many bit operations, since s <= n^8 and the
exponent m has at most n bits. The prime factor p, the extension
field, and the exponents in the root count are used only in the
correctness proof and need not be computed. All loops have uniform
polynomial bounds, completing the proof. []
