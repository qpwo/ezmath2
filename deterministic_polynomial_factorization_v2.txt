================================================================================
DETERMINISTIC POLYNOMIAL FACTORIZATION OVER PRIME FIELDS
A complete, readable account for a curious high-school student
================================================================================

    based on the research paper of the same title (OpenAI, October 2026)

    Audience
    --------
    You have seen polynomials, prime numbers, and modular arithmetic.
    You have not necessarily seen abstract algebra, algebraic geometry,
    or analytic number theory. Everything is introduced here. Nothing is
    assumed except comfort with the idea of "prove it, do not just claim it".

    What you will learn
    -------------------
    (1) A precise statement of the problem: given a prime p and a
        polynomial with coefficients in 0,1,...,p-1, find its irreducible
        factors and their multiplicities.

    (2) Why the "obvious" randomized algorithm is fast, and why making it
        deterministic with a guaranteed polynomial-time bound is genuinely
        hard.

    (3) The construction that finally achieves the deterministic bound.
        It has three surprising ingredients:
            -- a curve Y^q = F(X) attached to a polynomial F,
            -- a geometric obstruction that forces two unknown roots apart,
            -- an analytic input (a zero-free region for Hecke L-functions)
               that supplies the small primes we need.

    (4) Why the running-time bound is polynomial in the input size, and
        how the paper sweeps every messy constant into one big exponent.

    How to read this
    ----------------
    Sections 1-6 are background. Read them all. Sections 7-14 build the
    algorithm. Sections 15-17 are context and worked examples. A glossary
    at the very end collects symbols. When a proof is long, the
    mathematically-interested reader should read it; the rest can skim the
    statement and trust that every claim is justified in full.

PART I.  THE PROBLEM AND ITS HISTORY

================================================================================
1.  What "factor a polynomial" means
================================================================================

    A polynomial over a prime field is an expression like

        f(x) = 3x^4 + x^2 + 5x + 2

    where the coefficients 3, 1, 5, 2 are integers between 0 and p-1 for
    some fixed prime p. Arithmetic with coefficients is done modulo p:
    add, subtract, multiply, then reduce.

    A polynomial is irreducible if it cannot be written as a product of
    two polynomials of smaller positive degree. Example: over the integers,
    x^2 - 2 is irreducible (in the sense that its roots are not integers,
    though it factors over the reals). Over F_2 = {0,1}, x^2 + x + 1 is
    irreducible but x^2 + 1 = (x + 1)^2 is not.

    Every nonzero polynomial over a prime field factors uniquely as

        f = c * (g_1)^(e_1) * (g_2)^(e_2) * ... * (g_r)^(e_r)

    where c is a nonzero constant (the leading coefficient), the g_i are
    distinct monic irreducible polynomials, and the e_i are positive
    integers (the multiplicities). This is the analogue of prime
    factorization for integers.

    THE PROBLEM. Take as input a prime p written in binary (that is,
    using about log_2 p bits) and a polynomial f of degree n written as
    its list of n+1 coefficients. Output c, the g_i, and the e_i.

    The "size" of the input is n+1 coefficients, each about log_2 p
    bits, so total input length on the order of (n+1) log_2 p. We want
    an algorithm whose running time is a FIXED polynomial in that length.
    It must work for every prime p and every polynomial f.

================================================================================
2.  Numbers, polynomials, and the modular world
================================================================================

    Quick refresher, because every later section rests on this.

    A PRIME p has exactly two divisors: 1 and itself. Examples: 2, 3, 5,
    7, 11, 13, 101, 65537.

    MOD p ARITHMETIC. Take integers and reduce every result modulo p. So
    mod 7: 5+4 = 9 = 2, 3*5 = 15 = 1, 2-5 = -3 = 4. A key fact (because
    p is prime): every nonzero residue has a multiplicative inverse. So
    the residues {0, 1, ..., p-1} form a FIELD: you can add, subtract,
    multiply, and divide by anything nonzero.

    We denote this field F_p. It is called the prime field of characteristic p.

    TWO CRUCIAL SMALL THEOREMS.

    FERMAT'S LITTLE THEOREM. For every a in F_p, a^p = a. That is:
    raising anything to the p-th power does nothing, modulo p.

    Consequence: a nonzero element of F_p satisfies a^(p-1) = 1, so its
    multiplicative ORDER (the smallest positive k with a^k = 1) divides
    p-1. The set of nonzero elements F_p^* is a cyclic group: there is
    some element g, called a generator or primitive root, whose powers
    1, g, g^2, ..., g^(p-2) hit every nonzero element exactly once.

    THE FRESHMAN'S DREAM. Over F_p, (a + b)^p = a^p + b^p. The binomial
    coefficients binom(p,k) are all divisible by p for 1 <= k <= p-1,
    so they vanish mod p. This is FALSE over the integers but TRUE in
    characteristic p. You will see it used many times.

================================================================================
3.  Finite fields: the smallest playgrounds
================================================================================

    F_p is not the only finite field. For every prime power Q = p^d there
    is exactly one field with Q elements, denoted F_Q. Its elements can
    be described in several equivalent ways, the simplest being:

        F_Q = F_p[T] / (h(T))

    where h is an irreducible polynomial of degree d over F_p and the
    "quotient" means we compute polynomial arithmetic mod h. A concrete
    example with p=3 and d=2: pick h(T) = T^2 + 1 (irreducible over F_3
    because -1 = 2 is a nonsquare mod 3). Then F_9 = F_3[T]/(T^2 + 1),
    elements are a + bT mod 3, and T plays the role of sqrt(-1).

    BASIC STRUCTURE (finite field structure theorem).
    (a) The multiplicative group F_Q^* is cyclic of order Q - 1.
    (b) F_Q contains F_p as a subfield; its degree d over F_p is fixed.
    (c) If F_Q and F_R are two finite fields of characteristic p, one
        is a subfield of the other if and only if Q | R or R | Q.
    (d) The Frobenius map x -> x^p is a field automorphism of F_Q that
        fixes exactly F_p.

    When an algorithm in the sequel "works over an extension field K of
    F_p of degree at most something", we always represent K explicitly:
    a basis over F_p plus a multiplication table. That means arithmetic
    in K costs a fixed overhead compared to F_p. Nothing magical.

================================================================================
4.  What "polynomial time" really means
================================================================================

    The parameter that measures the problem is

        S = (n + 1) * log_2(p),

    the bit length of the input. An algorithm is POLYNOMIAL TIME if there
    is a fixed polynomial P (like S^3 or S^1000 or S^(10^12)) and a bound

        time(algorithm) <= P(S)

    for every possible input.

    The exponent 10^12 is spectacularly generous and perfectly acceptable.
    Nobody in this paper claims an exponent like 3 or 4. The point is to
    establish SOME fixed polynomial. Tightening it is a separate problem
    for another paper.

    Warning: if you measure "time" in field operations (each costing
    about log p bits), you have to multiply by log p at the end. In this
    paper the accounting is always in bit operations, so it is directly
    comparable between different branches.

    A first easy observation: if you tried to enumerate all residues
    mod p to "find a factor by brute force", you would use p steps,
    which is 2^L where L = log_2 p. That is EXPONENTIAL in the input
    length. Such brute-force searches appear in the paper only inside
    exponential-size regions of the parameter space that are separately
    bounded by a fixed (huge) polynomial in S.

================================================================================
5.  The classical randomized algorithm
================================================================================

    Here is the famous algorithm of Cantor and Zassenhaus (1981). It
    factors a polynomial f over F_p in expected polynomial time. Its
    cleverness is a template for what the deterministic version must
    eventually replace.

    Step 1. Make f square-free. Compute g = gcd(f, f'), the greatest
    common divisor with the formal derivative. Any factor h^2 of f
    divides g. Splitting off g and f/g reduces to square-free input,
    recursively.

    Step 2. Reduce to totally split. Use the Berlekamp algebra (Section 7)
    or a factorization algorithm to reduce to the case that f is
    monic, square-free, and all its roots are already in F_p. We are
    then asked to SPLIT f into linear factors, but we do not know them.

    Step 3. Pick c in F_p at random. Consider f(x) and try to find a
    factor via gcd(f(x), c^((p-1)/2) - 1) (for p odd) or via the
    polynomial gcd(f(x), (x + c)^((p-1)/2) - 1).

    Why does this work? In the totally split case, f has roots
    x_1, ..., x_N in F_p. For c chosen at random, the expression
    c^((p-1)/2) takes two values: +1 on the quadratic residues mod p
    and -1 on the nonresidues. So gcd(f(x), c^((p-1)/2) - 1) picks out
    exactly those roots x_i whose (x_i + c) is a residue. That splits
    the roots into two groups. Repeated trials quickly isolate one
    root.

    The ELEMENT that makes this work is a "quadratic nonresidue" -- an
    element whose square root does not exist in F_p. It exists in every
    F_p with p > 2 because the squares are exactly half of F_p^*.

    The trick is that the RANDOM choice of c usually separates any
    single pair of roots, and with enough tries it separates all of them.
    The expected number of tries is polynomial.


================================================================================
6.  Why randomization is annoying here
================================================================================

    Randomization is a beautiful tool. You pick something random, you
    get lucky with high probability, and the number of tries is small.
    For many practical purposes, that IS the right algorithm.

    But there are problems where we want more. Two of them matter here:

    (A) GUARANTEES. A randomized algorithm with expected time O(S^c) has
    a nonzero chance of running much longer than the expectation. For a
    single run, that chance might be tiny, but it is not zero. Deterministic
    algorithms have no flake-out. They produce their answer on every input
    with the same time bound.

    (B) DERANDOMIZATION. In complexity theory, "BPP = P" is the conjecture
    that randomization never helps much. Every deterministic version of a
    randomized algorithm provides evidence toward that conjecture.

    For polynomial factorization, the randomized approach had been an
    industry standard since 1981. Its cost was always bounded by a
    polynomial in the INPUT, but that polynomial depended on RANDOM LUCK.

    Making the same algorithm work deterministically is not just a
    technical convenience. It is a genuinely different kind of object.
    The rest of this document is about how to do it.

    THE SHIFT. The C-Z algorithm uses "the element c^((p-1)/2) is a
    residue or nonresidue". The proof of correctness relies on the fact
    that a suitably random c will land on different values at different
    roots. To derandomize, we need to construct, EXPLICITLY and
    DETERMINISTICALLY, some arithmetic element that plays the same role.
    Constructing it uniformly for every possible f is where the geometry
    and the number theory enter.

7.  Berlekamp: turn factors into linear algebra
================================================================================

    The Berlekamp algebra is the universal tool for finite field
    factorization. It replaces "find the irreducible factors of f" with
    "do linear algebra over a finite ring".

    SETUP. Let f in F_p[X] be monic, square-free, and of degree n. Work
    in the quotient ring

        R_f = F_p[X] / (f(X)).

    Elements of R_f are polynomials modulo f. Their degree is at most
    n-1, and arithmetic is done modulo f.

    By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, if f factors as

        f = h_1 * h_2 * ... * h_r

    with the h_i pairwise coprime (they are, because f is square-free),
    then

        R_f  is isomorphic to  F_p[X]/(h_1) x ... x F_p[X]/(h_r).

    If f is TOTALLY SPLIT -- that is, each h_i is a linear factor X - x_i
    for some x_i in F_p -- then R_f is isomorphic to F_p x F_p x ... x F_p
    (r copies), where a polynomial a(X) mod f maps to the tuple

        (a(x_1), a(x_2), ..., a(x_r)).

    THE BERLEKAMP ALGEBRA. Consider the set of elements a in R_f with

        a^p = a.

    Call this set B_f. It is a subring of R_f, and it is an F_p-vector
    space. In the totally-split case, an element a corresponds to a
    tuple (a_1, ..., a_r) of F_p elements, and a^p = a is automatic for
    every F_p element (Fermat). So B_f is the WHOLE F_p^r in that case.

    In general, B_f is the direct product of the F_p-subfields of the
    various F_p[X]/(h_i). If h_i has degree d_i, then F_p[X]/(h_i) is
    F_{p^(d_i)} and its fixed subfield under x -> x^p is F_p, one copy.
    So the dimension of B_f over F_p is exactly the NUMBER OF
    IRREDUCIBLE FACTORS of f.

    HOW TO COMPUTE B_f. Since (x + y)^p = x^p + y^p in characteristic p,
    the map phi: a -> a^p is F_p-linear. Computing a basis of B_f is the
    same as finding the kernel of "phi minus the identity", a linear
    algebra problem. Great: we can compute dim B_f in polynomial time.

    THE KEY OBSERVATION. If dim B_f = 1, then f is irreducible. If dim B_f > 1,
    then there exist elements a in B_f that take DIFFERENT values at
    different roots x_i. Take such an a. Then gcd(f, a - c) picks out
    some subset of the roots. So finding a NONSCALAR element a in B_f
    amounts to being able to SPLIT f.

    THE STRATEGY of Berlekamp's algorithm: compute B_f, find a nonscalar
    a, take a = some clever linear combination of B_f's basis. If f is
    NOT totally split, that is fine: we can then recurse into gcd(f, a)
    and gcd(f, f/gcd(f,a)) as long as both are nontrivial.

    THE SHORTCOMING. Berlekamp's algorithm still requires FINDING a
    suitable a. In the totally-split case, if p is huge (as it may be),
    we can be cleverer: the RANDOMIZED approach of Section 5 provides
    an a by sampling. That is where randomization enters.

    Berlekamp's framework itself is deterministic. What is not deterministic
    is the SEARCH for a nonscalar element in B_f.

================================================================================
8.  Reduction to the "totally split" case
================================================================================

    The splitting procedures we describe build up step by step.

    STEP A: SQUARE-FREE. Already described. If f has repeated factors,
    then gcd(f, f') is nonconstant and recursively reduces the problem.
    When f = g^p for some polynomial g, we work with g and multiply
    multiplicities by p. Done in Section 17.

    STEP B: TOTALLY SPLIT. Given a monic square-free f, we want to
    factor it. But the strong splitting procedures (Sections 9-16) work
    only when f is TOTALLY SPLIT: every irreducible factor has degree 1.

    How do we get to that case? The general f splits into a product
        f = f_1 * f_2 * ... * f_b
    where f_d is the product of the irreducibles of degree exactly d.

    The classical DISTINCT-DEGREE FACTORIZATION of Cantor-Zassenhaus
    computes the f_d for all d, using the observation that

        gcd(f(X), X^(p^d) - X)

    contains exactly the factors of f of degree dividing d. Taking successive
    differences gives f_d. This is deterministic and polynomial time
    (careful: X^(p^d) is computed by repeated squaring in about d * log p
    polynomial multiplications, not by building the enormous number X^(p^d)).

    We then need to further split each f_d into its degree-d irreducible
    factors. That still might be nontrivial. But here is the reduction
    that we will use in the paper:

    Pick a nonscalar element b in B_{f_d}. If b has two distinct values
    at some two irreducible factors h, h', then gcd(f_d, b - c) splits
    off a proper part. Since each h has degree d, we can do this in at
    most d * log(p) attempts. When f_d is a product of LINEAR factors,
    we are in the "totally split" target, and we want a procedure whose
    number of attempts does not depend on p at all.

    THE FOCUS OF THE PAPER. Reduce f to the case where f is totally split
    and of degree N >= 2, and give a DETERMINISTIC SPLITTING PROCEDURE
    f -> (factor of f). Then recurse. All the difficulty is in the split.
    The reduction to that case is standard engineering.

================================================================================
9.  Pairs, orientations, and a tournament
================================================================================

    Let p be odd (p=2 is a special case handled separately). Let F be
    monic and square-free of EVEN degree N >= 2, all its roots
    x_1, ..., x_N in F_p.

    We are going to find a nontrivial factor of F deterministically,
    polynomial time, no randomness.

    SETUP. Let A = F_p[T]/(F), so A is F_p^N via a(T) -> (a(x_1), ..., a(x_N)).
    Let R = A tensor A, so R is F_p^(N^2) via the map

        a(X) tensor b(Y) -> (a(x_i), b(x_j))  at coordinate (i,j).

    Consider the element

        Delta = T tensor 1 - 1 tensor T.

    At coordinate (i,j) it evaluates to x_i - x_j. Off the diagonal this
    is a nonzero element of F_p. On the diagonal it is 0.

    ORIENTING PAIRS. Write p - 1 = 2^s * t with t odd. Define

        e = Delta^(p-1).

    At coordinate (i,j) with i != j, Delta^(p-1) = (x_i - x_j)^(p-1) = 1
    (Fermat). On the diagonal it is 0. So e is exactly the "off-diagonal
    idempotent": it is 1 at every coordinate (i,j) with i != j, and 0 on
    the diagonal. So the algebra e*R is the ring of functions on ORDERED
    PAIRS of DISTINCT roots.

    Now consider Delta^t, where t is the odd part of p - 1. At coordinate
    (i,j) this is (x_i - x_j)^t. What order does Delta^t have in the
    multiplicative group? At coordinate (i,j), the element Delta^t has
    order dividing 2^s. In fact:

        (Delta^t)^(2^s) = Delta^(t * 2^s) = Delta^(p-1) = 1,

    so Delta^t has order dividing 2^s. And its 2^(s-1)-th power is
        (Delta^t)^(2^(s-1)) = Delta^((p-1)/2),

    which takes value +1 on pairs where x_i - x_j is a quadratic residue,
    and -1 when it is a nonresidue. If we let u be an element of A with
    full 2-primary order, then we can compute logarithms of Delta^t in
    the group of order 2^s, coordinate by coordinate.

    THE PRIMARY GENERATOR. Let u be an element of F_p^* of order 2^s (so
    u generates the full 2-primary subgroup of F_p^*). Such a u is the
    (p-1)/2^s-th power of any quadratic nonresidue, and one is found by
    simply asking: which of 2, 3, 4, ..., (p-1)/2 is a nonsquare? For
    any of those, the answer is immediate by computing u = a^((p-1)/2^s)
    and checking u has order 2^s. (For deterministic polynomial time, we
    can just test small candidates; at most O(sqrt(p)) tests suffice for
    a small enough candidate. In the paper the search is O(B^1000) at
    worst and is trivial at the scale of the main bound.)

    Now at coordinate (i,j), (Delta^t)^(2^(s-1)) = +1 means x_i - x_j is
    a residue; = -1 means nonresidue.

    DEFINE ORIENTATION. For each unordered pair {i,j} of distinct roots,
    exactly one of (i,j) or (j,i) has (Delta^t)^(2^(s-1)) = +1. Check:
    swapping i and j changes Delta^t to -(Delta^t) (because t is odd).
    Raising to 2^(s-1) multiplies value by (-1)^(2^(s-1)) = -1 (since s
    >= 1). So one orientation gives +1 and the other gives -1. Exactly
    one is selected.

    So we get an ORIENTATION of the complete graph on roots: for every
    pair, we pick one direction. This is a TOURNAMENT in the sense of
    graph theory!

================================================================================
10.  The row-count argument
================================================================================

    Now use the tournament. For each root i, define

        c_i = number of pairs {i,j} oriented TOWARD i (that is, where the
              +1 orientation is (j, i)).

    Equivalently: c_i is the number of roots j such that (x_j - x_i) is
    a quadratic residue mod p. (Up to whether we orient toward or away;
    the important fact is that it is an integer between 0 and N-1.)

    Simple count: every unordered pair contributes exactly one to exactly
    one c_i. There are N(N-1)/2 pairs total. So

        c_1 + c_2 + ... + c_N = N(N-1)/2.

    AVERAGE CLAIM. If all c_i were equal, then each would be (N-1)/2.
    But (N-1)/2 is not an integer when N is EVEN. So the c_i cannot all
    be equal.

    THIS IS THE WHOLE TRICK. In an even tournament, the row counts
    cannot all have the same value. There must be at least two DISTINCT
    row counts.

    HOW TO EXPLOIT IT. We now have a way to define a polynomial in A:

        c(T) = sum over i of c_i * e_i(T)

    where e_i(T) is the idempotent that is 1 at x_i and 0 at other roots.
    Concretely, we compute c as an element of A without knowing the roots.

    Then we test gcd(F, c - a) for a = 0, 1, ..., N-1. Some value a
    corresponds to at least one root (since the c_i take at least two
    distinct values), and the gcd splits F.

    HOW TO COMPUTE c. Here is the sequence, all in algebra form:

        (i)  Compute Delta^t in R (polynomial arithmetic).
        (ii) Use a logarithm-in-2-primary-group procedure (Section 12)
             to determine, at each coordinate (i,j), whether the +1
             orientation is (i,j) or (j,i).
        (iii) Build the idempotent mask e_* = sum over oriented (i,j).
        (iv) Apply trace in the second factor to get c in A.

    The whole thing uses only linear algebra over F_p and F_p-arithmetic.

    THE POINT. We have a deterministic procedure for the EVEN case.

    COST. R has dimension N^2 over F_p. The logarithms have at most
    s <= log_2(p) digits, each with two possible outcomes. So the whole
    thing is polynomial in N and log p. This is polynomial in the input
    size.

    GOOD. Now the hard case: N odd.

================================================================================
11.  The curve Y^q = F(X) and the points we cannot see
================================================================================

    Setup for this entire part. Let F in F_p[X] be monic and square-free
    of ODD degree N >= 3, all roots x_1, ..., x_N in F_p. We will build
    a deterministic splitter for F.

    The even-case trick used pairs of roots and a tournament. For odd N,
    there is no such easy algebraic invariant (a tournament on an odd
    number of vertices can be REGULAR -- every vertex has the same out-
    degree (N-1)/2 -- which is exactly what makes the trick fail).
    We need something more elaborate.

    IDEA. Embed the roots into a richer geometric object where extra
    arithmetic information becomes available. The object is an algebraic
    curve.

    THE WORKING FIELD. Choose an odd prime q dividing N. Recall N is odd,
    so q is odd. Let r = v_q(N) be the q-adic valuation of N (so
    q^r divides N but q^(r+1) does not).

    We need a field K / F_p containing a primitive q-th root of unity
    ζ (ζ^q = 1, ζ != 1), and an element ω_K of K^* whose order is exactly
    q^(v_q(|K|-1)). This is the "full q-primary generator" of K^*. Its
    existence follows from the fact that K^* is cyclic. Constructing
    it explicitly requires an auxiliary prime, as we discuss in Section 18.

    Extend K to k via k = K[T]/(T^(q^r) - ω_K). This is again a finite
    field (Section 3), and it now contains an element ω = T (mod the
    relation) of order q^(s_K + r) where s_K = v_q(|K|-1). In particular
    ω generates the FULL q-primary subgroup of k^*.

    Choice of q. Any odd prime dividing N works. The algorithm in
    Section 17 takes q = some odd prime factor of N.

    THE CURVE. Over k, define the projective curve

        C :  Y^q = F(X).

    Concretely, the AFFINE part is the set of (X, Y) in k x k satisfying
    that equation. The PROJECTIVE completion adds points at infinity
    where X or Y becomes unbounded. We can describe the whole thing via
    the function field k(C) = k(X, Y) / (Y^q - F(X)).

    BASIC FACTS.

    (a) C is a SMOOTH curve over k. Proof: a potential singularity would
        satisfy Y = 0 and F(X) = F'(X) = 0, contradicting square-free
        (which means gcd(F, F') = 1).

    (b) The projection pi: C -> P^1, (X,Y) -> X has degree q. So PI
        is a q-to-1 cover of the projective line.

    (c) There are N RAMIFICATION POINTS, one over each root x_i of F:
            P_i = (x_i, 0).
        Each P_i is totally ramified: locally pi looks like X - x_i = Y^q.
        These are the "images" of the roots we cannot directly compute.

    (d) There are exactly q RATIONAL POINTS AT INFINITY. To see this,
        put z = X^{-1}, U = Y * z^(N/q). Then U^q = z^N * F(z^{-1}),
        which at z = 0 equals 1. So U(infinity_j) = ζ^j for j = 0,...,q-1.
        There are q distinct infinity points:
            infinity_0, infinity_1, ..., infinity_{q-1}.

    (e) The GENUS of C (a measure of geometric complexity) is
            g = (q-1)(N-2)/2.
        For our range of parameters, g is bounded above by (q-1) * n / 2,
        polynomial in n and q.

    (f) The automorphism sigma of C defined by

            sigma(X, Y) = (X, ζ Y)

        acts on points of C. It fixes each ramification point P_i (since
        Y = 0 there) and permutes the infinity points: sigma(infinity_j) =
        infinity_{j+1} (indices mod q).

    (g) sigma acts on FUNCTIONS via (sigma h)(X, Y) = h(X, ζ^{-1} Y).
        So div(sigma h) = sigma div(h): taking divisors commutes with the
        action.

    Let us repeat with emphasis: THE ROOTS OF F ARE THE RAMIFICATION
    POINTS OF C. This is the hinge on which the whole construction turns.

================================================================================
12.  Divisors: how to compute at unknown points
================================================================================

    A DIVISOR on a smooth projective curve is a formal finite integer
    combination of points:

        D = sum_P n_P * P,     n_P in Z,  all but finitely many n_P = 0.

    Divisors add in the obvious way. The DEGREE of D is the integer
    deg(D) = sum_P n_P.

    To every nonzero rational function h on C we associate its PRINCIPAL
    DIVISOR:

        div(h) = sum_P v_P(h) * P

    where v_P(h) is the ORDER OF VANISHING of h at P: positive if h has
    a zero, negative if h has a pole. Two functions give the same
    principal divisor if and only if they differ by a nonzero constant,
    and every principal divisor has degree zero.

    DIVISOR CLASS GROUP. Two divisors are LINEARLY EQUIVALENT, written
    D ~ D', if their difference D - D' is principal. The equivalence
    classes form the PICARD GROUP Pic(C). The degree-zero ones form the
    JACOBIAN J(C) = Pic^0(C), a group.

    WHY DIVISORS. A divisor is a piece of bookkeeping. It records what a function does
    at every point of the curve. "Does h have a zero at P_i?" or "Does h
    have a pole of order 3 at infinity_1?" These are all divisor
    questions. What makes divisors useful is that:

    (1) They can be combined, added, negated.
    (2) The set of points where a function has a pole or a zero is often
        a manageable object (the divisor of h has degree = number of zeros
        minus number of poles, which is 0).
    (3) Linear equivalence "quotients out" the boring parts.

    THE SPECIAL CONSTRUCTION. In the application, our curves have a
    big symmetry: the automorphism sigma has order q. Any sum

        1 + sigma + sigma^2 + ... + sigma^(q-1)

    acts as 0 on both the Jacobian and on the "natural" divisor lattices
    at infinity (because it is the norm from k(C) to k(X)). This is
    exploited in the key constructions.

    THE MAIN OPERATOR. We define

        lambda = 1 - sigma,

    which acts on divisors (as formal sums), on divisor classes, and on
    functions:

        (lambda * h) = h * (sigma h)^{-1} = h / (sigma h).

    Some basic facts about lambda, proven in the paper:

    (a) LAMBDA IS SURJECTIVE ON THE GEOMETRIC JACOBIAN. For every class
        D in J(C), there exists a class D' in J(C) with lambda * D' = D.
        This follows because multiplication by q is invertible on J(C)
        (q != char k), and q = unit * lambda^(q-1), so lambda is invertible
        on J(C). This sounds like it makes life easy, but...

    (b) LAMBDA IS INJECTIVE ON THE INFINITY LATTICE Lambda. Which is the
        set of integral divisors of degree zero supported at infinity:

            Lambda = { sum_j a_j * infinity_j : a_j in Z, sum_j a_j = 0 }.

        Since sigma permutes the infinity_j cyclically and the orbit-sum
        is 0 on Lambda, Lambda is the natural lattice on which sigma acts.
        Multiplication by lambda = 1 - sigma is INJECTIVE on Lambda,
        because Lambda is a free Z-module and lambda has no kernel
        (a * e = 0 with e = sigma(infinity_0) - infinity_0 generates Lambda
        would force a = 0).

    THE TENSION. On the Jacobian, lambda is SURJECTIVE: taking a lambda-
    division is possible. On the infinity lattice, lambda is INJECTIVE
    (not surjective): taking a lambda-division is IMPOSSIBLE for most
    lattice elements. The paper makes this tension into a splitting
    mechanism. Here is what that means concretely.

    Every divisor D decomposes as

        D = D_fin + D_inf

    where D_fin involves only finite points and D_inf lives entirely on
    infinity points. The "position vector" at infinity is D_inf.

    Suppose we take a class D in J(C), and we want to divide it by
    lambda^m for some integer m. We can do the division as a CLASS
    (lambda is surjective on J). But each step of division produces a
    specific divisor representative, and the infinity part of that
    representative is affected by the choice of representative.

    THE KEY OBSERVATION. If we track the infinity part through divisions
    carefully, we can detect a failure: eventually we will be forced to
    divide an integral lattice element by lambda, which is only possible
    up to a finite limit (because lambda is injective and the exponents
    grow).

    Concretely: consider the specific class d = [P_i - infinity_0] for
    each root i. This has a lambda-division, but the class of the result
    is not "canonical"; it can be represented by a divisor whose infinity
    part varies. Choose any division, take its infinity part W, then
    divide AGAIN.

    This is the source of the label tests at the end.

================================================================================
13.  The infinity lattice and why division must fail at the end
================================================================================

    Let us set up the crucial machinery.

    INTEGRALITY BLOCKER. As above, Lambda is injective under lambda. So
    integral lambda-divisions of an element of Lambda exist only in
    special cases. Precisely: lambda W' = W has a solution W' in Lambda
    if and only if W lies in image of lambda on Lambda. This is a
    proper sublattice of Lambda.

    THE LIFTING PROCEDURE. Fix a root i. Consider the class

        d_{i, 1} = [P_i - infinity_0].

    We compute lambda d_{i, 1} = [e] where e is the specific lattice
    element e = sigma(infinity_0) - infinity_0. So class-wise, division is
    possible.

    Now apply LambdaDivide to get d_{i, 2} with lambda d_{i, 2} = d_{i, 1},
    and continue. At each step t we get d_{i, t} which satisfies:

        lambda^t * d_{i, t} = [e].

    Every step is possible as a class computation. The GEOMETRIC theorem
    (Section 13 of the paper) is that all these classes can be chosen
    over an extension of degree POLYNOMIALLY BOUNDED above k.

    SUMMING AND DIVIDING DOWN. Sum over i:

        d_m = sum over i of d_{i, m}.

    Since each d_{i, t} satisfies lambda^t d_{i, t} = [e] and there are
    N such, we get

        lambda^m * d_m = [N * e].

    This is one specific relation. Now we go back DOWN: define

        d_t = lambda^(m-t) * d_m.

    So d_0 = lambda^m d_m = [N * e], and d_m is our original summed
    class.

    Now the KEY DIVISIBILITY CRITERION. Consider the lattice element
    N * e. It lives in the image of lambda on Lambda UP TO A SPECIFIC
    POWER: precisely, if N = q^r * a with q not dividing a, then
    N * e = lambda^(q-1)r * (unit) * e, and (after adjusting by units)
    is exactly in lambda^(m-1) Lambda but not lambda^m Lambda, where
    m = 1 + (q-1)r.

    WHY? The identity q = u * lambda^(q-1) for some unit u in Z[zeta]
    (cyclotomic units) means q is a unit times lambda^(q-1). So the
    lattice element q * e is lambda^(q-1) times something integral, but
    not lambda^q times something integral, essentially because the
    cyclotomic unit u is a genuine unit (invertible in Z[zeta], so it
    doesn't affect the ideal structure). Iterating gives the bound.

    THE LABEL TESTS. This is where the obstruction manifests. Let us
    describe the successive label tests.

    For each t = 1, ..., m, we want to compute a set of labels
        l_{i, t} in F_q,
    one for each root i, satisfying the following. The pair (d_t, W_t)
    where W_t = lambda^{-(t-1)} N e is an element of a certain finite
    quotient module T_t. If we expand in the Ramification-generator basis
    { [P_i - infinity_0] : i = 1,...,N } of T_1 (over F_q), the labels
    l_{i, t} are exactly the coefficients in this expansion:

        (d_t, W_t) = sum_i l_{i, t} * [P_i - infinity_0].

    The label test: check whether all l_{i, t} are equal.

    THE FORCED DISTINCTION. Here is the theorem (Proposition 16 in the
    earlier version; I renumber but keep the substance):

    If all labels l_{i, t} are equal at some level t, then the pair
    (d_t, W_t) represents the zero class in the module T_1. Returning
    to the actual divisor identity, this means we can write d_t as the
    class of an integral infinity divisor W', and the lattice equation
    lambda W' = W_{t-1} has a SOLUTION in Lambda. This propagates the
    invariant to level t+1. But at the top level t = m, the lattice
    obstruction says W_m is NOT in lambda * Lambda. So the labels CANNOT
    all be equal at level m.

    THEREFORE: at some level between 1 and m, the labels must be unequal.
    And here "unequal labels" is EXACTLY a distinction between two roots.

    Return to Section 10: we applied this idea to even N with the
    tournament. Now, for odd N, the label test plays the same role:
    once we have labels l_{i, t} not all equal, we can pick a target
    value a in F_q and form gcd(F, "the idempotent that selects i with
    l_{i, t} = a") and get a factor.

    THAT IS THE WHOLE THEORETICAL MECHANISM.

    WHAT REMAINS. Two huge implementation tasks.

    (A) DIVISION. We promise "compute LambdaDivide(D, P)". This needs
        to be a polynomial-time algorithm. Section 14-16 handles this
        via solving norm equations, which reduces to linear algebra
        and idempotents in matrix algebras.

    (B) DIVISOR ARITHMETIC AT UNKNOWN POINTS. The divisors above are
        specified without knowing the actual P_i (they refer to
        "P_i = (x_i, 0)" where x_i is unknown). The divisor algorithms
        must work with this implicit representation. That is handled
        by an IDEAL-REPRESENTATION trick: represent the finite part of
        a divisor by a fractional ideal in the Dedekind ring O = k[X,Y]/(Y^q - F(X)) =
        k[x, y], and reduce the arithmetic to polynomial algebra over k.

    Sections 14-16 handle (A). A sidebar handles (B) explicitly. That
    is where the concrete polynomial algorithms live.


================================================================================
PART V.  NORM EQUATIONS AND IDEMPOTENTS
================================================================================

    We now fill in the missing piece: an algorithm for LambdaDivide(D, P).
    The reduction has three steps:

        STEP 1. Turn class division into a NORM EQUATION on functions.
        STEP 2. Turn the norm equation into a RANK-ONE IDEMPOTENT in a
                matrix algebra.
        STEP 3. Compute a rank-one idempotent via linear algebra over a
                space of global sections of a vector bundle on P^1.

    Each step is a different flavour of mathematics, and all three are
    needed for the final algorithm. Let us walk through them.

================================================================================
14.  From a norm equation to a matrix algebra
================================================================================

    Let k, q, C = { Y^q = F(X) }, sigma be as in Section 11. Let
    L_curl = k(C) be the function field, and let K_0 = k(X) be the base
    rational function field. The map L_curl -> K_0 is the projection
    (X, Y) -> X, with degree q. The NORM of a function h in L_curl is

        Norm(h) = h * sigma(h) * sigma^2(h) * ... * sigma^(q-1)(h),

    which is an element of K_0. (This is the "product over all q conjugates".)

    THE NORM EQUATION PROBLEM. Given a nonzero G in K_0, find h in
    L_curl^* with Norm(h) = G. (If the promise is that such h exists;
    otherwise reject.)

    In the application, G is a specific rational function computed from
    a divisor via Lemma 12 (compression): G equals h^+ / h^- multiplied
    by a normalizing constant, where h^+ and h^- come from characteristic
    polynomials of multiplication maps.

    THE CLASS-DIVISION REDUCTION. Suppose we have a divisor D and want to
    divide its class by lambda. The paper does the following:

        (i)   Compute the pushforward j = h^+ / h^- in K_0 with
              div_C(j) = sum over sigma-orbit of D. (This is the
              "compression + orbit sum" step of Lemma 12.)
        (ii)  Normalize j by c in k^* so that the resulting G = cj has
              residue 1 at the specific ramification point P = (x, 0)
              of interest.
        (iii) Solve Norm(h) = G.
        (iv)  Define E = D - div(h). Then sum over sigma of E equals 0
              (this is exactly the "orbit sum" condition, and h is
              chosen so that this holds).
        (v)   Take the coefficientwise maximum V of the partial sums
              S_j = E + sigma E + ... + sigma^(j-1) E for j = 0,...,q-1.
        (vi)  Return V - (deg V) * P, reduced via Riemann-Roch.

    The point is that step (v) produces a divisor V satisfying

        (1 - sigma) V = E = D - div(h).

    So the class of (V - deg V * P) divides D by lambda, but as an actual
    divisor with explicit representation.

    WHY DOES THE NORM EQUATION HAVE A SOLUTION? Because step (i) gives
    div(h) = D - (1-sigma) D_0 for some divisor D_0 which is promise-given
    by the input, and the norm of any such h has div = 0 on P^1, hence is
    a constant multiple of j. The normalization by c adjusts the constant
    to be a specific q-th power, making G exactly a norm. The paper proves
    this carefully.

    SO: the entire class-division problem reduces to solving a NORM
    EQUATION for a specific rational function G.

    WHY IS THAT HARD? Norm equations over function fields can have very
    large solutions. A naive search over functions of every degree fails.
    We need a direct construction.

    THE MATRIX-ALGEBRA TRICK. Consider the K_0-algebra E defined by

        E = L_curl + L_curl * V + L_curl * V^2 + ... + L_curl * V^(q-1)

    where V is a formal symbol subject to Va = sigma^(-1)(a) V for a in
    L_curl, and V^q = G. (Equivalently V a = sigma^{-1}(a) V, so V a V^{-1}
    = sigma^{-1}(a).)

    E is a "CYCLIC ALGEBRA": q-dimensional over L_curl, hence q^2-
    dimensional over K_0. The multiplication is explicit in the standard
    basis W_{a,b} = Y^a V^b (0 <= a,b < q).

    A basic fact (which the paper proves): E is a full q x q matrix
    algebra over K_0, i.e. E ≅ M_q(K_0), IF AND ONLY IF G is a norm.

    So restated: the norm problem is "given E with explicit multiplication
    table, decide whether E ≅ M_q(K_0), and if so find an explicit
    isomorphsm."

    Actually, more useful: the paper does not find an isomorphism. It
    finds a RANK-ONE IDEMPOTENT e in E, which is an element e with
    e^2 = e and (in the M_q(K_0) model) image a 1-dimensional subspace.

    WHY IS THAT USEFUL? Because once you have a rank-one idempotent e,
    extracting a norm solution is linear algebra:

        LEFT IDEAL. E * e is a q-dimensional left ideal in E. The map
        L_curl -> E*e given by a -> a*e has kernel zero (since every
        nonzero element of L_curl is invertible and e != 0), so it is an
        isomorphism by dimension. In particular there is a UNIQUE h in
        L_curl with V e = h e.

        SOLVE FOR h. Write e in the standard basis, and V in the standard
        basis. The equation V e = h e is a system of q^2 equations in q
        unknowns (the coefficients of h in the basis 1, Y, ..., Y^(q-1)).
        Solve it. The resulting h is unique.

        CHECK NORM. Multiply V e = h e by V repeatedly on the left,
        using V a = sigma^{-1}(a) V at each step, and use V^q = G. After
        q steps:

            G e = V^q e = h sigma^{-1}(h) sigma^{-2}(h) ... sigma^{-(q-1)}(h) e.

        So the coefficient G equals the norm of h, because e is nonzero
        and the coefficient on both sides is in K_0.

    So finding a norm solution is EQUIVALENT to finding a rank-one
    idempotent in E.

    HOW TO CONSTRUCT e. This is the technical heart of the section.
    The construction uses orders: rings of integral elements in E, one
    for each closed point of P^1. Each order is explicitly computable
    from local expansion data at that point (residue roots, Hensel
    lifting, matrices). Then the Q_global space of elements integral at
    every point is computed as H^0(P^1, End(V)) for a rank-q vector
    bundle V on P^1 (the vector bundle whose local lattices are the
    various orders).

    A vector bundle on P^1 is classified by the Grothendieck splitting
    theorem: every vector bundle is a direct sum of O(d_i) for integers d_i.
    The ENDOMORPHISM bundle End(V) has global sections that correspond
    to a specific space of block upper triangular matrices (after
    suitable basis change).

    The FIBER of this bundle at the point at infinity is a SUBALGEBRA
    B of M_q(k). The construction then uses linear algebra to extract a
    rank-one idempotent from B, entirely without knowing the isomorphism.
    Finally, lifting that matrix idempotent back to the global sections
    and taking b^q gives a global idempotent e in E.

    This is not the sort of thing you can compute by hand. It is a
    genuine construction that happens to work because of the projective-
    line splitting theorem.

    The full flow, as a diagram:

        NORM EQUATION (rational functions)
            |
            v [construct cyclic algebra E]
        MATRIX ALGEBRA E ≅ M_q(K_0)
            |
            v [integral structure: maximal orders at every point]
        VECTOR BUNDLE V on P^1, with End(V) = global sections
            |
            v [splitting theorem: V = sum O(d_i)]
        BLOCK UPPER TRIANGULAR subalgebra B of M_q(k)
            |
            v [trace pairing gives nested column space]
        RANK-ONE IDEMPOTENT a_0 in B
            |
            v [lift to global section b in E, take e = b^q]
        RANK-ONE IDEMPOTENT e in E
            |
            v [solve V e = h e]
        NORM SOLUTION h

    Each step is deterministic and polynomial-time in the underlying
    polynomial sizes. The rank-one idempotent step (trace pairing) is
    the trickiest to motivate, and is explained in Section 15.

================================================================================
15.  Vector bundles and the fiber calculation
================================================================================

    This section explains the ONE algebraic trick that turns a "block
    upper triangular algebra" into an explicit rank-one idempotent. It
    is the last piece of the norm solver.

    THE SETUP. We have a finite-dimensional k-algebra B ⊆ M_q(k), and
    we know that under some change of basis, B is conjugate to the algebra
    of BLOCK UPPER TRIANGULAR matrices (with block sizes r_1, ..., r_s
    summing to q):

            [ T_1  *     *   ... * ]
        B = [ 0    T_2   *   ... * ]
            [ 0    0     T_3 ... * ]
            [ .    .     .   ... . ]
            [ 0    0     0   ... T_s]

    where T_i is any r_i x r_i matrix, and off-diagonal blocks are
    arbitrary. This "conjugate" is NOT KNOWN explicitly (the change of
    basis is the thing we want to avoid computing).

    THE QUESTION. From B alone, construct a rank-one idempotent in B.

    IMPORTANT: We need to construct it in the ORIGINAL basis, because
    the eventual lift to E requires knowing its coordinates in that
    basis.

    SOLUTION: THE TRACE PAIRING KERNEL.

    Define the bilinear form on B:

        (a, b)  ->  tr(ab)    (matrix trace).

    Compute the kernel N of this form:

        N = { b in B : tr(a b) = 0 for all a in B }.

    In block upper triangular coordinates, a product ab has DIAGONAL blocks
    matching the diagonal blocks of a and b:

        (ab)_ii = a_ii * b_ii.

    Therefore tr(ab) = sum_i tr(a_ii b_ii).

    The trace pairing on M_q(k) is nondegenerate (any two matrix units
    E_ij, E_kl pair to δ_il δ_jk, which is 1 only for i=l, j=k). Hence
    the pairing on each full matrix algebra of each diagonal block is
    nondegenerate.

    Now consider b in B. If b_ii = 0 for all i, then tr(a b) = 0 for
    all a in B, so b in N. Conversely, if some b_ii != 0: pick a in B
    with a_ii chosen so that tr(a_ii b_ii) != 0 (possible by nondegeneracy),
    and a_jj = 0 for j != i. Then tr(a b) = tr(a_ii b_ii) != 0. So
    b not in N.

    CONCLUSION: N = strictly block upper triangular part of B (diagonal
    blocks zero, off-diagonal arbitrary).

    Now consider the common kernel of all the matrices in N:

        U = intersection over n in N of ker(n).

    If N is JUST the strictly block upper triangular part, what is U?
    We need to figure out the common kernel.

    Claim: U is EXACTLY the space of column vectors of the form (v_1, 0, 0, ..., 0)
    with v_1 in k^(r_1) (arbitrary vector in the FIRST block position).

    Why? Consider a vector u = (u_1, u_2, ..., u_s). If any u_j != 0 with
    j >= 2, then some n in N can send it to a nonzero vector: pick n to be
    zero except in the (1,j) block, and choose the (1,j) block to be
    an arbitrary matrix (it's in the strictly-upper part), specifically
    one that sends u_j to a nonzero result. Then n u has a nonzero first
    block. Contradiction.

    If u is of the form (v_1, 0, ..., 0), then for any n in N,
    n u = (n_{11} v_1, 0, ..., 0) (since block columns beyond 1 kill zero
    entries), but n_{11} = 0 (it's a strictly-upper matrix, so its
    diagonal blocks are zero). So n u = 0. Contradiction.

    Hence U = first column block.

    CONSTRUCTING an idempotent from U.

    Pick any 0 != v in U. Pick any row vector w in k^q such that w v = 1
    (this just requires w v != 0 where v is a specific column vector;
    there are many such w, choose a canonical one).

    Define a_0 = v w, the outer product of a rank-one column and a
    rank-one row. This is a q x q matrix of rank one, and a_0^2 = v w v w
    = v (w v) w = v w = a_0 (an idempotent!).

    BEHAVIOR UNDER BLOCK COORDINATES: v has only its first-block entries
    possibly nonzero. The rank-one matrix a_0 = v w has only its first
    block ROW possibly nonzero. So a_0 lies in the FIRST BLOCK ROW of B,
    i.e. in B itself. Great.

    So we have constructed a rank-one idempotent a_0 in B, in the
    original coordinates.

    FINAL STEP: Lift a_0 (a matrix) to a global section b in E
    (an element of the matrix algebra over the function field) that
    evaluates to a_0 at infinity. This is a linear solve.

    Then compute e = b^q. Why b^q?

    Think in block coordinates. b evaluates to a_0 at infinity, which
    is a rank-one idempotent. It therefore has characteristic polynomial
    T^(q-1) (T - 1) at infinity (one eigenvalue 1, q-1 eigenvalues 0).
    So det(T - b) = T^(q-1) (T - 1).

    But b is a GLOBAL section of End(V), so the coefficients of det(T - b)
    are GLOBAL SECTIONS of O(P^1) = constants. So they are CONSTANTS,
    and the characteristic polynomial of b is IDENTICALLY T^(q-1)(T-1)
    (as a polynomial over K_0).

    Now b is a matrix over K_0 whose characteristic polynomial is
    T^(q-1)(T - 1). By the Chinese Remainder / primary decomposition, the
    algebra K_0[b] is isomorphic to K_0[T]/(T^(q-1)(T-1)) = K_0 x
    (nilpotent part). The idempotent projecting onto the eigenvalue-1
    eigenspace is b^q:

        b^q on T-1 eigenspace = 1^q = 1,
        b^q on T^(q-1) nilpotent = 0^q = 0.

    So e = b^q is a RANK-ONE IDEMPOTENT in E.

    THAT IS THE NORM SOLVER.

    THE REMAINING ENGINEERING. To actually implement all this, one must:

    (a) Construct maximal orders A_v at every closed point v of P^1. This
        is done by explicit local formulas:
        - Generic points: standard order.
        - Place at a root of F: total ramification, special local model.
        - Places where the norm G has valuation multiple of q: scaled
          order.
        - Place at infinity: model using z = X^{-1} and renormalized Y.

    (b) Compute the global sections (elements integral at EVERY place)
        as solutions to a finite linear system. The ansatz is a
        rational function with bounded numerator degree (bounded by
        coefficients of the orders), and the constraints are
        "divisibility by certain powers at each exceptional place".

    (c) Evaluate the map "sections at infinity -> matrices in B" via
        the local model at infinity.

    (d) Apply the trace-pairing computation described above.

    (e) Lift, solve V e = h e, return h.

    Each step is polynomial in q, N, D (where D bounds the numerator and
    denominator degrees of G). The paper proves precise bounds.

================================================================================
16.  Sizes: what is controlled and by how much
================================================================================

    The paper is careful about sizes because the entire pipeline is
    polynomial-time and we need to track that nothing blows up.

    Let N be the degree of F (which is odd here), D the max numerator/
    denominator degree of G, and let d_H <= N + 2D be the number of
    distinct "bad" places where the local model is nontrivial.

    Let M = 10 * q^2 * (N + D + 1) be a bound on the valuation of any
    element of any local order. The paper's Lemma "Bounds for the local
    models" proves that every nonzero entry of the matrices W_{ab} and
    their inverses has valuation between -M and +M. This follows by
    explicit formulas (Section 15.4 of the paper) which are polynomials
    in q, N, D.

    ANSATZ. Every global section of the order has the form

        b = sum_{a,b'=0}^{q-1} c_{ab'} W_{ab'}

    with c_{ab'} = P_{ab'}(X) / H(X)^M, where H = prod_i h_i is the product
    of the "bad" places and deg P_{ab'} <= M * (d_H + 1).

    The linear system has at most q^2 * (M(d_H + 1) + 1) unknowns, and
    about q^2 * M * (d_H + 1) scalar conditions (one per matrix entry at
    each place, at precision up to 3M + 3).

    All these numbers are polynomials in q, N, D. So all linear algebra
    in the norm solver is polynomial time.

    OUTPUT SIZE. The extracted h in L_curl has the form

        h = (1/d_h(X)) * sum_{j=0}^{q-1} a_j(X) Y^j

    with deg a_j, deg d_h <= some explicit polynomial bound (the paper
    proves it is bounded by, say, B^20 in the general bound, but with
    precise polynomial dependence on q, N, D). The absolute divisor degree
    of div(h) is bounded by a polynomial in q, N, D.

    THAT IS THE COMPLETE NORM-SOLVING MACHINERY.


================================================================================
16.5  Divisor arithmetic without knowing the points
================================================================================

    This is a crucial sidebar that must be included, because the algorithms
    in Part IV and V all manipulate divisors whose support includes the
    unknown ramification points P_i = (x_i, 0). We cannot enumerate
    these points. We must represent divisors IMPLICITLY.

    THE IDEAL REPRESENTATION.

    Let O be the affine coordinate ring: O = k[X, Y] / (Y^q - F(X)) =
    k[x, y]. Since C is smooth, O is a DEDEKIND DOMAIN: every ideal has
    a unique prime factorization. The fraction field of O is the function
    field L_curl = k(C).

    For a divisor D supported only on affine points, define the
    FRACTIONAL IDEAL:

        I(D) = { a in L_curl^* : v_P(a) >= v_P(D) for every affine P }
                 union {0}.

    This is a module over O. Every fractional ideal arises this way. It
    is free as a k[X]-module with basis 1, Y, ..., Y^(q-1).

    THE FINITE PART of a divisor is represented by this I(D). The
    INFINITY PART (a tuple of q integers, one for each infinity_j) is
    stored separately as a vector.

    SUM, NEGATIVE, MAX, MIN of divisors all translate into ideal
    operations:

        I(D + E)  = I(D) * I(E)     (ideal product)
        I(-D)     = I(D)^(-1)        (inverse ideal)
        I(max(D,E)) = I(D) ∩ I(E)   (intersection)
        I(min(D,E)) = I(D) + I(E)   (sum)

    Each of these can be computed with polynomial algebra over k. This is
    the essence of Proposition 4.1 in the paper.

    REPRESENTING THE IDEAL CONCRETELY.

    Choose a "bound polynomial" H in k[X] with

        H * O  ⊆  I  ⊆  H^(-1) * O.

    Then I is determined by the SUBSPACE (H*I)/(H*O) inside the quotient
    (H^(-1)*O)/(H*O), which has dimension 2*q*deg(H) over k. Store that
    subspace as a matrix of coefficients mod H^2. This is a finite
    linear-algebra object.

    CONSTRUCTING A BOUND H. Lemma 4.2 (compression) shows how to compute
    two monic polynomials h^+ and h^- such that H = h^+ h^- is a valid
    bound, and deg(h^+) = "total zero degree" of D, deg(h^-) = "total pole
    degree". These are the characteristic polynomials of multiplication-
    by-X on the integral ideals I ∩ O and I^(-1) ∩ O.

    An additional consequence (orbit-sum identity): if deg(D) = 0,
    then div(j) = sum over a=0..q-1 sigma^a D where j = h^+ / h^- in
    K_0 = k(X). This is the ORBIT SUM: it gives the pushforward of D
    to P^1 as a rational function j. That is exactly the j used in
    Section 14 step (i).

    RIEMANN-ROCH. For a divisor D on C and integer ell >= 0, let

        L(D + ell * infinity_0) = { h in L_curl^* : div(h) + D + ell
                                    * infinity_0 >= 0 } union {0}.

    This is a finite-dimensional k-vector space. The Riemann-Roch
    theorem guarantees it is NONEMPTY for large enough ell (specifically
    ell >= g where g is the genus). We compute a basis by:

        - Choosing an ansatz: every element has the form
                H^(-1) * sum_{j=0}^{q-1} a_j(X) Y^j
          with deg(a_j) <= deg(H) + |D| + ell.

        - Imposing membership in I(-D) (a subspace condition) as linear
          conditions on (a_j).

        - Imposing v_{infinity_j}(h) >= -v_{infinity_j}(D) - ell [j=0]
          via local Laurent expansion conditions.

    Solving the resulting linear system gives the space. This is used
    for REDUCTION: given a degree-zero divisor D, pick ell = g, find a
    nonzero h in L(D + g * infinity_0), and set D' = D + div(h). Then
    deg(D') = deg(D) = 0 and (D') + g*infinity_0 is EFFECTIVE of degree
    g, so the ABSOLUTE DEGREE of D' is at most 2g. This is the "reduce
    to canonical small form" operation.

    LOCAL EXPANSIONS. For the infinity conditions and for local
    residue computations used elsewhere:

        - At infinity_j: use z = X^(-1) and U = Y z^(N/q). Solve U^q =
          z^N F(z^(-1)) by Hensel lifting to enough precision.

        - At a ramification point P_i = (x_i, 0): use Y as uniformizer
          and solve F(X) = Y^q with X(0) = x_i via Hensel (derivative
          F'(x_i) != 0 because F is square-free).

    SIMULTANEOUS DIVISOR SUM. When we have N divisors, one for each root
    of F, computed over the algebra A = k[T]/F(T) via the Simultaneous
    Execution lemma (Section 8.1), we must sum them into a single divisor
    over k. This is done WITHOUT finding the components via the NORM:

        - Compute H_* = Norm_{A/k}(H) (product of the bound polynomials).
        - Compute w_a = Norm_{A/k} (sum_{j=0}^{b-1} a^j u_j) for a set of
          a in k (at least s(b-1)+1 distinct scalars).
        - The w_a generate the product ideal, and their norms give a valid
          bound H_*.

    This avoids enumerating the roots. See Lemma 4.3 in the paper.

    BINARY INFINITY COEFFICIENTS. In the final label test, the infinity
    divisors W_t have coefficients that can be EXPONENTIALLY large in
    binary (up to N*(2q)^m, where m is polynomial in n). We cannot
    represent W_t by its coefficient vector directly. Instead:

        - The divisor -W_t is written as a sum of q-1 signed multiples of
          (infinity_j - infinity_0) with binary-exponent coefficients.
        - We build a CIRCUIT: at each doubling or addition step, reduce
          (keeping the divisor small) and compose the resulting function
          with the current one.
        - The final h has a circuit representation whose leaves are
          SMALL functions (from reductions on small divisors).
        - At a ramification point, evaluate the circuit by propagating
          the leading coefficient and the valuation through each leaf.

    This keeps the actual object polynomial-sized. Lemma 4.5 in the
    paper proves the correctness and the size bound.

    That is the full "how to compute at unknown roots" story: implicit
    ideal representation + local expansions + Riemann-Roch reduction +
    norm for simultaneous operations + circuit representation for
    exponential infinity coefficients.

================================================================================
17.  The full splitting algorithm
================================================================================

    We are now ready to state the actual OddSplit procedure. It is the
    deterministic algorithm promised in Section 13.

    INPUT:
        - Prime p, prime q | N with N odd, N = deg(F).
        - Monic, square-free, totally split F in F_p[X], degree N.
        - The field K / F_p of degree at most q-1, containing:
            - primitive q-th root of unity ζ.
            - ω_K in K^* of order q^(v_q(|K|-1)).

    SETUP:
        - r = v_q(N).
        - m = 1 + (q-1) * r.
        - k = K[T]/(T^(q^r) - ω_K), [k:F_p] <= N(q-1).
        - ω = T (mod T^(q^r) - ω_K), full q-primary generator of k^*.
        - Curve C: Y^q = F(X) over k, ramification points P_i.
        - A = k[T_1]/F(T_1) = k^N (simultaneous execution over all roots).
        - e = (sigma - 1) * infinity_0 in the lattice Lambda.

    STAGE 1. LIFT EACH RAMIFICATION CLASS.

        Over A (simultaneously for all i), set:

            D_i := P_i - infinity_0

        as a divisor with variable point P_i in the i-th component.

        For t = 1, ..., m-1:
            D_i := LambdaDivide(D_i, P_i)
            (returns a divisor D' with (1-sigma)[D'] = [D_i])

        After these m-1 divisions, D_i = D_i^(m-1) satisfies
            lambda^t [D_i^(t)] = [e] for each t = 1, ..., m.

        If at any point LambdaDivide detects zero tests diverging between
        components, STOP and return the resulting factor of F. (This
        happens if the algorithm can split F sooner.)

    STAGE 2. SUM THE LIFTS.

        Use the simultaneous divisor sum operation (Lemma 4.3) to compute
        the sum:

            D_m := sum_{i=1}^N D_i^(m)

        This is a single degree-zero divisor over k satisfying

            lambda^m [D_m] = [N e].

        Reduce D_m using Riemann-Roch to get an equivalent divisor of
        absolute degree at most 2g.

        Now descend: for t = m-1, m-2, ..., 1:

            D_t := Reduce( (1-sigma) D_{t+1} )

        This computes d_t = lambda^(m-t) d_m iteratively.

        Also compute the integral infinity lattice divisors:

            W_t := lambda^(-(t-1)) [N e]  for t = 1, ..., m.

        These are guaranteed to exist (in Lambda) by (N e) being in
        lambda^(m-1) * Lambda but not lambda^m * Lambda.

    STAGE 3. LABEL TESTS.

        For each t = 1, 2, ..., m:

            Test invariant: lambda[d_t] = [W_t].

            Compute the function h_t satisfying

                div(h_t) = (1-sigma) D_t - W_t

            using the binary circuit method for W_t (which has potentially
            large coefficients).

            For each root index i, compute:
                - v_{P_i}(D_t) -- the coefficient of P_i in D_t
                - h_t(P_i) -- the value of h_t at P_i

            Compute the norm of h_t at P_i: this equals (h_t(P_i))^q,
            independent of i. Call it c_t in k^*.

            Compute a q-th root gamma_t of c_t using Lemma 8.2
            (UnitRoot, the primary subgroup extraction).

            Compute the LABELS:

                l_{i, t} := v_{P_i}(D_t) - log_ζ(h_t(P_i) / gamma_t)

            in F_q (log base ζ, since h_t(P_i)/gamma_t is a power of ζ).

            If the labels l_{i, t} are NOT ALL EQUAL, then:
                - Pick a target value a in F_q.
                - Compute the idempotent mask e_a in A that is 1 on
                  components where l_{i, t} = a.
                - Return gcd(F, e_a(T)) as a factor of F.
                  (This is a proper nontrivial factor because we detected
                   two distinct label values.)

            If the labels are all equal, continue to t+1.

    TERMINATION. By Proposition 13 (Section 13) applied with the class
    d_m just computed, at SOME t in 1, ..., m, the labels must differ.
    So the algorithm always terminates with a factor.

    COMPLEXITY. Each step is polynomial in n and L = log p, as follows:

        - Field k has degree at most N(q-1) <= n^2 over F_p.
        - Each LambdaDivide call costs a fixed polynomial (from Sections
          14-16), bounded by (say) B^2000 bit operations.
        - There are at most m <= B^2 divisions.
        - Simultaneous divisor sum costs poly(n, q, log|k|).
        - Label tests: at most q*m^2 log(N) * |k|^2 bit operations.

    Total bit cost is bounded by B^C for an absolute constant C. In the
    paper's final accounting the exponent is set generously at 10^12.

    CORRECTNESS. If OddSplit returns a factor, it is a proper factor of F
    by the gcd structure. If it runs all m tests without returning, that
    CONTRADICTS Proposition 13, because the labels must differ at some
    level. Hence OddSplit always returns a factor.

    THAT is the deterministic splitting procedure for odd degree.

18.  The auxiliary prime table
================================================================================

    Sections 14-17 assumed we had specific field data:
        - K / F_p containing ζ of order q.
        - ω_K of order q^(v_q(|K|-1)).
    For each prime q dividing N, we must construct this data explicitly.
    That is what the auxiliary prime table does.

    THE AUXILIARY PRIME. For each prime q <= n, we need a rational prime
    ℓ (call it the "auxiliary prime for q") such that:

        ℓ not in {2, 3, p, q},
        ℓ ≡ 1 (mod 12 q),
        p^((ℓ-1)/q) != 1 (mod ℓ).

    The last condition means: p is NOT a q-th power modulo ℓ. This will
    be the key input that makes the table work. The congruence mod 12q
    ensures ℓ has "enough roots of unity" for the construction.

    GIVEN ℓ, we build the table entry for q as follows.

    STEP 1. Build the "cyclotomic test algebra" R_ℓ:

        R_ℓ = F_p[T] / (T^(ℓ-1) + T^(ℓ-2) + ... + T + 1)

    This is the ℓ-th cyclotomic polynomial mod p, so R_ℓ encodes the
    ℓ-th roots of unity over F_p.

    Let Γ_ℓ = (Z/ℓZ)^* be the multiplicative group of integers mod ℓ.
    Let H_q = { a^q : a in Γ_ℓ } be the q-th-power subgroup.

    For a in Γ_ℓ, define τ_a: T -> T^a as a substitution automorphism of
    R_ℓ. Compute the H_q-invariant subalgebra:

        U_q = R_ℓ^(H_q) = intersection over a in H_q of ker(τ_a - id).

    This is F_p-linear algebra: stack the matrix representations of
    "τ_a - identity" for all a in H_q, find the common kernel.

    LEMMA. U_q is a field of degree q over F_p.

    WHY? R_ℓ (over an algebraic closure) has ℓ-1 primitive ℓ-th roots of
    unity as its "points". Γ_ℓ acts by raising roots to powers a; by
    regularity, the regular action gives a single orbit. H_q is a subgroup
    of index q (since Γ_ℓ is cyclic of order ℓ-1 and H_q is the q-th
    powers, so Γ_ℓ / H_q has order q). Frobenius acts on the orbit set by
    multiplying by p; the condition p^((ℓ-1)/q) != 1 mod ℓ means this is
    a NON-trivial element of Γ_ℓ/H_q. A cyclic group of prime order q
    has no proper nontrivial subgroup, so Frobenius acts transitively on
    the H_q-orbits. Hence U_q is a single field of degree q. QED.

    IMPORTANT: the computation of U_q NEVER calls for factoring the
    ℓ-th cyclotomic polynomial ζ_ℓ(X) = sum X^j, whose degree is ℓ-1
    (potentially very large). Everything is a single F_p-linear
    algebra problem in dimension ℓ-1.

    STEP 2. Factor the q-th cyclotomic polynomial over F_p.
    Compute a factor of Φ_q(Z) = 1 + Z + ... + Z^(q-1) over F_p, taking
    the first monic irreducible factor in a fixed coefficient order. Let
    K_q = F_p[Z]/(that factor), of degree d_q = ord_q(p), which divides
    q-1. Let ζ_q be the image of Z in K_q: a primitive q-th root of unity
    because the roots of Φ_q all have order q.

    STEP 3. Tensor with U_q.
    Form V_q = U_q (tensor over F_p) K_q. Since deg U_q = q and
    deg K_q = d_q divides q-1, the two degrees are coprime, so the
    tensor product is a field of degree q over K_q.

    STEP 4. Get a q-th root of unity on the q-primary side.
    Put Q_q = |K_q| = p^(d_q) and s_q = v_q(Q_q - 1). On V_q^*, the map
    u -> u^(Q_q - 1) has image exactly the norm-one subgroup (both have
    order (Q_q^q - 1)/(Q_q - 1)). The norm of ζ_q from V_q to K_q is
    ζ_q^(1+Q_q+...+Q_q^(q-1)) = ζ_q^q = 1, so ζ_q lies in that image.
    Therefore the linear system u^(Q_q - 1) = ζ_q u has a nonzero
    solution u in V_q.

    STEP 5. Extract the full q-primary generator.
    Let w = u^((Q_q^q - 1) / q^(s_q+1)) and ω_q = w^q. Equation
    v_q(Q_q^a - 1) = s_q + v_q(a) (LTE, for q odd) shows v_q(Q_q^q - 1)
    = s_q + 1, so w has order q^(s_q+1) and ω_q has order q^(s_q). That
    subgroup of order q^(s_q) sits in K_q^*, since q^(s_q) divides
    Q_q - 1. Hence ω_q lies in K_q^*, as required.

    STEP 6. Deliver (K_q, ζ_q, ω_q).
    These three objects are exactly what the odd splitter consumes: the
    field, the q-th root of unity, and the full q-primary generator of
    K_q^*.

    COST on factorization: the only polynomial-factoring call is Φ_q,
    of degree q-1, which is strictly smaller than q. So the recursive
    structure works: to build the entry for q we call Factor at degree
    q-1, which only needs table entries for primes at most q-1.

    q = 2 (even case). No auxiliary prime table needed. Just find a
    nonresidue in F_p^*: pick the first candidate a in {2, 3, 4, ...}
    that is not a square. Raise a^((p-1)/2^s) where 2^s || p - 1, and use
    that as ω_2. This gives the full 2-primary generator of F_p^*, exactly
    what Section 10 needs.

    HOW TO FIND ℓ IN THE FIRST PLACE. Search upward from 12q + 1 through
    integers ≡ 1 (mod 12q). For each candidate, test primality by trial
    division and test p^((ℓ-1)/q) != 1 mod ℓ by modular exponentiation.
    Each test costs poly(log ℓ + log p). The analytic input (Section 19)
    says that some auxiliary prime exists below c_0 * B^20000000 for an
    absolute constant c_0.

    So the full cost of building all table entries is bounded.

================================================================================
19.  The analytic input: Hecke L-functions
================================================================================

    We need ONE final input to close the loop: a guarantee that small
    auxiliary primes exist. That input comes from analytic number theory.

    THE STATEMENT (Theorem 1.2 in the companion paper
    "Primitive roots for every admissible integer base"). For every
    cyclotomic number field K containing the twelfth roots of unity,
    and every finite-order Hecke character χ of K, the Hecke L-function
    L_K(s, χ) has NO ZERO in the strip

        Re s > 1 - δ,    δ = 10^(-6)   (a fixed absolute constant).

    The principal character is included, with its pole at s=1 permitted.
    There is NO restriction on the conductor or imaginary part. The width
    δ is uniform across K and χ.

    WHY DOES THIS GIVE AUXILIARY PRIMES?

    For each prime q and each prime p (large), consider the cyclotomic
    field K = Q(ζ_12q) and the extension M = K(p^(1/q)). Since p is
    unramified in K (p is large), p^(1/q) is not in K and X^q - p is
    Eisenstein at p. So [M:K] = q and M/K is cyclic. By class field
    theory / abelian zeta factorization,

        ζ_M(s) = product over the q characters of K of L_K(s, χ).

    The assumed zero-free strip transfers to ζ_M(s) AND to ζ_K(s).

    Then a smoothed explicit formula (Lemma 1 in Section 10 of the paper)
    gives:

        Ψ_K(x) - (1/q) Ψ_M(x)
            = (1 - 1/q) * x * Φ(1) + O(x^(1-δ) * (log D + [K:Q]))

    where Ψ_K, Ψ_M are smoothed prime-counting functions (sums of log(N P)
    over prime ideals P, with a smooth cutoff), Φ is the Mellin transform
    of the smoothing function, and D is the discriminant.

    THE KEY OBSERVATION: Prime ideals P of K that SPLIT COMPLETELY in M/K
    are exactly those for which X^q - p splits mod P (i.e., p is a q-th
    power modulo P), and these contribute EQUALLY to Ψ_K and (1/q)Ψ_M
    (they cancel in the difference). Prime ideals of K that do NOT split
    (i.e., p is not a q-th power) contribute POSITIVELY to the difference.

    Hilbert-style argument on residue degrees: for a prime ℓ ≡ 1 (mod q)
    of K with residue field F_ℓ, p^((ℓ-1)/q) = 1 mod ℓ iff p is a q-th
    power mod ℓ. And it splits completely in M iff p is a q-th power
    mod ℓ. So the first-power degree-one primes of K that are CANCELLED
    are exactly the ones with p a q-th power mod ℓ.

    Meanwhile, the main term (1 - 1/q) x Φ(1) can be compared to the
    error term O(x^(1-δ) * B^3). At x = T B^20000000 (with T an absolute
    constant), the error is a small fraction of the main term. So:

        Ψ_K(x) - (1/q) Ψ_M(x) is LARGE (of order x).

    But the total contribution of primes UP TO x with p a q-th power
    modulo those primes is at most the error term. And the contribution
    of ALL primes up to 2x with p a q-th power is at most some O(x^(1-δ))
    amount. So: if NO prime ℓ <= x ≡ 1 mod q existed with p not a q-th
    power mod ℓ, we would have a contradiction. Hence there MUST exist
    such a prime ℓ below c_0 B^20000000.

    That is the analytic input in a nutshell. Its full proof is in the
    companion paper. It uses standard explicit-formula machinery:
    the smoothed explicit formula for ζ_M(s), a uniform zero-density
    estimate (from Hasanalizade-Shen-Wong 2022), Mellin transform decay,
    and the cyclotomic discriminant bound. All of it is standard
    analytic number theory, though precise and careful.

    IMPORTANTLY: the analytic input is ISOLATED. Everything else in this
    paper is elementary algebra and geometry. Only this section uses
    L-functions. Moreover, GRH for finite-order Hecke L-functions implies
    the same strip, so the algebraic reduction ALREADY gives polynomial-time
    factorization under GRH (independent of the analytic theorem).
    The companion paper removes the GRH assumption by proving the strip
    unconditionally.

================================================================================
20.  The complexity bound
================================================================================

    Let us put all the pieces together and account for the running time.

    THE MASTER PARAMETER. Set

        B = 20 + (n + 1) * (L + 1)

    where n = deg f is the input degree and L = ceil(log_2 p) is the number
    of bits in the prime. Then B is a constant multiple of the bit length
    of the input. Every polynomial bound in the paper is expressed in
    terms of B.

    THE TOP-LEVEL DRIVER.

    Algorithm Factor(f):
        (1) Read degree n, leading coefficient c. If n = 0, return
            (c, empty list).
        (2) Let h = f / c, so h is monic.
        (3) Compute h' = formal derivative of h (coefficients at
            positions 0, 1, ..., n-1 scaled by their index, mod p).
        (4) If h' = 0: then h(X) = g(X^p) for some polynomial g whose
            coefficients are the p-th roots of h's nonzero coefficients
            (which equal themselves in F_p). Recursively Factor(g),
            multiply every multiplicity by p, return.
        (5) Else: let d_0 = gcd(h, h'). If d_0 != 1:
            recursively Factor(d_0) and Factor(h / d_0) and merge the
            two result lists by adding multiplicities. Return.
        (6) Now h is square-free. Compute Berlekamp algebra B_h.
            Let r = dim B_h (r = number of distinct irreducible factors).
        (7) If r = 1: return (h, multiplicity 1).
        (8) Else: split h into two or more factors using one of the
            following, then return the merged recursive Factor on each
            piece:

              (a) SMALL p (p <= B^(200000)). Brute force: find a nonscalar
                  element b in B_h. For a in F_p (enumerated), test
                  gcd(h, b - a). One of them must be nontrivial. Split.

              (b) LARGE p, EVEN N: use EvenSplit (Section 10).

              (c) LARGE p, ODD N: use OddSplit (Section 17), which needs
                  the auxiliary table entry for the odd prime q dividing N.
                  If q is the smallest odd prime factor of N, we can use
                  that q.

    All the work is in (8b) and (8c). The engineering (1)-(7) is standard.

    THE SMALL/LARGE SPLIT. The value B^200000 is chosen so that:
        (a) For p <= B^200000, the small-prime brute-force search in
            (8a) uses at most B^200000 scalar tests, bounded by B^201000.
        (b) For p > B^200000, the analytic input (Section 19) guarantees
            auxiliary primes ℓ_q <= c_0 * B^20000000. Then the
            characteristic hypotheses of the norm solver and the divisor
            trace are satisfied (they demand p > B^6 or so).

    THE COMPLEXITY ACCOUNTING.

    For the large-p branch, one OddSplit call costs a fixed polynomial
    in B, bounded by (say) B^20000 (this comes from the norm solver's
    internal linear algebra, the divisor arithmetic, and the label
    tests). There are at most O((n+1)^2) OddSplit calls across the
    recursion. Total splitting work: B^20004.

    Building the auxiliary table for each prime q <= n costs:
        - Φ_q factorization by recursion at degree q-1: included in the
          recursion cost.
        - Everything else: poly(q, log p) <= B^40 per entry, times at
          most B^41 for the whole table.
    Total table work: B^41 E^10, where E is the largest auxiliary prime
    actually built. With E <= c_0 B^20000000, this is at most B^400000100.

    The small-p branch (p <= B^200000) costs B^201000.

    Summing: the total is at most B^400000100 in the large-p case, and
    B^201000 in the small-p case. The paper rounds this up to the
    headline exponent:

        bit cost of Factor(f) = O(((n+1) L)^(10^12))

    with an absolute implied constant.

    THAT IS THE POLYNOMIAL-TIME BOUND.

    Note what is and is not claimed:
        - We do NOT claim a small exponent. 10^12 is far larger than
          practical algorithm design would want. That is fine. The
          theorem says only "a fixed polynomial exists".
        - We do NOT claim this is fast in practice. Practical factoring
          uses Cantor-Zassenhaus on top of Berlekamp, usually with
          randomness.
        - We DO claim: for EVERY prime p and EVERY nonzero polynomial f
          over F_p, the result of Factor(f) is the complete factorization
          of f, and the running time is at most B^10^12 bit operations.

    The polynomial bound is the real result. The efficiency comes later.

21.  History and the wider landscape
================================================================================

    A few signposts in the story of this problem.

    BERLEKAMP (1967, 1970). Introduced the Berlekamp algebra, turning
    factorization into existence of a nonscalar element in B_f. Proved
    polynomial-time (in p) factorization by exhaustive search for such
    an element.

    CANTOR-ZASSENHAUS (1981). Randomized expected polynomial-time
    factoring. The workhorse algorithm for decades. Requires a random
    choice of a in F_p to separate factors.

    SHOUP (1990). Deterministic algorithm with running time polynomial
    in n and p^(1/2+o(1)). This is the first deterministic algorithm
    that does better than "polynomial in p"; its characteristic
    dependence is roughly the square root.

    RONYAI (1988, 1992). Under GRH, deterministic polynomial-time
    factoring when the number of irreducible factors is bounded, or
    when the splitting field degree is bounded. Also introduced
    deterministic factorization of totally-split polynomials given a
    quadratic nonresidue.

    EVDOKIMOV (1994). Under GRH, deterministic subexponential-time
    factoring in general, specifically (n^(log n) log p)^O(1), which is
    quasipolynomial in n.

    SCHOOF (1985). Independent geometric machinery: computes elliptic
    curve Frobenius in polynomial time, providing polynomial-time
    computation of square roots mod p when they exist. Uniform bound
    depends on the "height" of the input in a specific way.

    PILA (1990). Frobenius computations on general abelian varieties,
    giving deterministic computation of roots of unity of fixed prime
    order under suitable conditions.

    ALTMAN (2025). Handles reductions modulo many primes together:
    total time pi(X) (m log H)^O(1) log^5 X, "avearged" over primes,
    where m can be as large as the factorial of the polynomial's degree.

    THE PRESENT PAPER (2026). Combines:
        - geometric ideas (curves Y^q = F(X), the infinity lattice)
        - algebraic ideas (norm equations, idempotents, vector bundles)
        - the analytic input (a zero-free region for Hecke L-functions)
    into a fully deterministic polynomial-time algorithm with a
    uniform bound in (n+1) L.

    The innovation is the ODD-DEGREE splitter: the geometric + algebraic
    construction of a separator that cannot exist by elementary means
    (because a regular tournament on an odd number of roots has no
    intrinsic distinguishing element). The infinity-lattice obstruction
    is what FORCES the geometric construction to produce a nontrivial
    factor.

    The input from the companion paper (uniform zero-free strip for
    Hecke L-functions) is the last non-algebraic ingredient. Under GRH,
    this strip is automatic, so the algebraic reduction gives
    polynomial-time factoring under GRH. The companion removes the GRH
    assumption.

    WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT PROVE. This is a theoretical result, not
    a practical algorithm. The bound's exponent (10^12) is enormous. The
    real algorithmic machinery for everyday use remains Cantor-Zassenhaus
    plus optimizations. What the paper does is establish that
    "deterministic polynomial time" is possible AT ALL, settling a
    longstanding question about the relationship between randomization
    and factoring.

22.  A small worked example
================================================================================

    Because the parameter p must be quite large for the odd splitter to
    engage its full machinery (p > B^200000), we cannot trace the ENTIRE
    algorithm on a small example. But we can walk through the STRUCTURAL
    STEPS on a small example to see how the pieces fit together.

    SETUP. Take p = 7, and consider

        f(X) = X^3 - 3X^2 + 2X = X(X - 1)(X - 2).

    The roots in F_7 are x_1 = 0, x_2 = 1, x_3 = 2. So f is
    monic, square-free, totally split, of odd degree N = 3.

    This is EXACTLY the input OddSplit expects. In practice p = 7 is
    small, so the driver would use EvenSplit's cousin for small p
    (brute force). But the STRUCTURAL steps of OddSplit still make sense
    and we can trace them symbolically.

    PARAMETERS.
        q = 3 (the only odd prime dividing N = 3).
        N = 3 = 3^1 * 1, so r = v_3(N) = 1.
        m = 1 + (q-1) * r = 1 + 2 = 3.

    WORKING FIELD. p = 7. We need a q-th root of unity. In F_7, the
    element 2 satisfies 2^3 = 8 = 1 and 2 != 1, so ζ_3 = 2 is a
    primitive cube root of unity. And F_7 already contains it (since
    3 | 7-1). So we take K = F_7, ζ = 2.

    For the q-primary generator: v_3(7 - 1) = v_3(6) = 1. Take
    ω_2 = ... wait, q = 3 here, so we need ω_3 in F_7^*. The full
    3-primary subgroup of F_7^* (order 6) has order 3. Find a g with
    order exactly 3: 2^3 = 8 = 1, so ord(2) = 3. Then ω_3 = 2 works.
    (In general for q=3 and p=7, K = F_7 and ω_3 = 2.)

    BUILD k. k = K[T]/(T^3 - ω_3) = F_7[T]/(T^3 - 2). Does T^3 - 2 have
    a root in F_7? Check T = 1: 1-2 = -1 = 6 != 0. T = 2: 8-2 = 6 != 0.
    T = 3: 27-2 = 25 = 4 != 0. T = 4: 64-2 = 62 = 6 != 0. T = 5:
    125-2 = 123 = 4 != 0. T = 6: 216-2 = 214 = 4 != 0. So T^3 - 2 is
    irreducible over F_7. Hence k = F_7^3 = F_343. Good.

    In k, ω = T mod (T^3 - 2) generates the full 3-primary subgroup of
    k^*, which has order 3^2 = 9. Check: T^3 = 2 (order 3 in F_7^*),
    T^6 = 4, T^9 = 8 = 1, so ord(T) = 9. Good, ω = T has order 9 in
    k^*.

    THE CURVE. C: Y^3 = F(X) where F(X) = X^3 - 3X^2 + 2X, over k = F_343.

    Ramification points:
        P_1 = (0, 0),
        P_2 = (1, 0),
        P_3 = (2, 0).

    Genus: g = (3-1)(3-2)/2 = 2*1/2 = 1. So C is an ELLIPTIC CURVE.
    (This is a coincidence of small parameters.)

    Infinity points: q = 3 points. From U^3 = z^N F(z^-1) = z^3 (z^-3 -
    3 z^-2 + 2 z^-1) = 1 - 3z + 2z^2. At z = 0: U^3 = 1. So
    U(infinity_j) = ζ^j for j = 0, 1, 2:
        U(infinity_0) = 1,
        U(infinity_1) = 2,
        U(infinity_2) = 4 = 2^2.

    So three infinity points: infinity_0 = [0:1:1], infinity_1 = [0:1:2],
    infinity_2 = [0:1:4] in some projective coordinates.

    THE AUTOMORPHISM. sigma: (X, Y) -> (X, ζ Y) = (X, 2Y). Then:
        sigma(P_i) = P_i (Y = 0),
        sigma(infinity_j) = infinity_{j+1} (indices mod 3).

    THE OPERATOR lambda = 1 - sigma. And e = (sigma - 1)(infinity_0) =
    infinity_1 - infinity_0 in the lattice Lambda.

    STAGE 1. LIFT CLASSES (symbolically). Set D_i := [P_i - infinity_0].

        Compute D_i' such that (1 - sigma)[D_i'] = [D_i].
        Since sigma is a nontrivial automorphism, (1-sigma) has
        nontrivial action on the Jacobian. We compute LambdaDivide
        three times (for each i). Each division is a class division,
        done via the norm solver (Section 15). Over the product algebra
        A = k[T_1]/F(T_1), all three run simultaneously.

        After Stage 1, we have D_i^(t) for t = 1, 2, 3 and i = 1, 2, 3,
        satisfying lambda^t [D_i^(t)] = [e].

    STAGE 2. SUM THE LIFTS. Compute D_3 = sum over i=1,2,3 of D_i^(3).
    Then:

        lambda^3 [D_3] = [N e] = [3 e].

    The lattice element 3e lies in lambda^(m-1) Lambda = lambda^2 Lambda
    but NOT lambda^3 Lambda, because N = 3 = 3^1 * 1 with r = 1 and
    m = 1 + (q-1)r = 3, so m - 1 = 2.

    Descend: D_2 = Reduce((1-sigma) D_3), D_1 = Reduce((1-sigma) D_2).

    Compute infinity divisors W_t = lambda^(-(t-1)) (3e) for t = 1, 2, 3.

    STAGE 3. LABEL TESTS.

        Test t=1: compute h_1 with div(h_1) = (1-sigma) D_1 - W_1.
        Evaluate h_1(P_i) for i=1,2,3.
        Compute norm c_1 = h_1(P_i)^3 (independent of i).
        Extract gamma_1 in k^* with gamma_1^3 = c_1.
        Compute labels l_{i, 1} = v_{P_i}(D_1) - log_2(h_1(P_i)/gamma_1) in F_3.

        If labels differ: gcd(F, e_a) gives a factor. Done.
        If not: t = 2 (test again).
        ...
        Guaranteed: at some t in {1, 2, 3} the labels differ.

    The actual numerical values of h_1, gamma_1, etc. require running
    the full norm solver and divisor arithmetic, which is too involved
    for a small hand-calculation. But the STRUCTURE is exactly the
    above. The key insight to appreciate is:

        - We never actually compute the roots x_i = 0, 1, 2 (we know them
          here because it is a toy example, but the algorithm does not
          use them).
        - Instead, we compute everything in the algebra A = k[T_1]/F(T_1),
          doing SYMBOLIC arithmetic with the variable T_1 that represents
          "some unspecified root".
        - If a computation's outcome differs between two roots, a GCD
          immediately splits F. That is what the label tests detect.
        - The geometric argument GUARANTEES that at some level the
          labels differ, without knowing which roots they are.

    This is the miracle the paper constructs. On this example, all the
    geometric machinery (curve C, ramification P_i, infinity points,
    sigma, lambda) is EXPLICIT: an elliptic curve over F_343, three
    specific points, one specific automorphism. The general case works
    the same way, with larger q, N, and genus.

    p                     the prime characteristic (input parameter)

    g                     genus of C, (q-1)(N-2)/2
    P_i                   ramification point (x_i, 0), i = 1, ..., N
    infinity_j            q points at infinity on C, j = 0, ..., q-1
    1 - sigma             1 - sigma (also written lambda)
    e                     (sigma - 1)(infinity_0), generator of Lambda

    Div(C)                divisors on C, formal integer sums of points
    div(h)                principal divisor of h
    Pic(C)                divisor class group of C

    d_{i,t}               division chain classes, lambda^t d_{i,t} = [e]
    d_m                   sum over i of d_{i,m}, lambda^m d_m = [N e]
    d_t = lambda^{m-t} d_m   the descend chain, chain of "how far down"
    W_t                   lambda^{-(t-1)}(N e), integer lattice divisor

    h_t (in some sections)   distinguished function defining div = (1-sigma) D_t - W_t

    l_{i,t}               labels in F_q at test t; l_{i,t} differing

    Norm(h)               product of conjugates sigma^j(h), j = 0,...,q-1
