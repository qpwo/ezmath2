================================================================
PART 1. WHAT A GAS IS, BRIEFLY
================================================================

A gas is a lot of tiny particles flying around inside a box.

Each particle has a position x and a velocity v, so each
particle lives in a six-dimensional "phase space"
    (x, v) in R^3 x R^3.
The position part is 3D; the velocity part is 3D again.

If the particles are all the same (say, all argon atoms), the
physics is determined by:
  - Newton's laws. Each particle is accelerated by the forces
    from the others.
  - The force between two particles at positions x_i and x_j
    is written as a gradient of a potential Phi:
        force on i from j = -grad Phi(x_i - x_j).
  - The potential Phi is often taken to be radial, meaning it
    depends only on the distance r = |x_i - x_j|. That is true
    for atoms and molecules, because atoms are roughly
    spherical.

Microscopically, everything is deterministic: given the
positions and velocities of all particles at time 0, Newton
plus the potential uniquely specifies them at any later time.

That sounds nice and complete. But it is completely useless for
a gas, because a gas at room temperature has around
10^23 particles per cubic centimeter. No one can store that,
let alone solve 10^23 coupled ODEs at every tick.

So a gas needs a different description. That description is
called kinetic theory, and the main object of kinetic theory
is a probability density.


================================================================
PART 2. TWO DESCRIPTIONS OF A GAS
================================================================

There are two descriptions of the same gas.

  MICROSCOPIC (Newtonian)
    A list of positions and velocities for each particle:
        (x_1, v_1), (x_2, v_2), ..., (x_N, v_N).
    Deterministic. In principle exact. In practice impossible
    to compute for real gases.

  KINETIC (probabilistic)
    A function f(t, x, v), called the phase-space density.
    It is a probability density on R^3 x R^3:
    it integrates to 1 over all positions and velocities.
    The actual expected number of particles per unit of
    phase space is mu times f, where mu = eps^-2 is the
    density scale from Part 4. So
        mu * f(t, x, v) dx dv
    is the expected number of particles in a volume dx
    around x, with velocity in dv around v, at time t.
    f is a continuous function.

The question of kinetic theory, in one sentence:

    In what sense does the microscopic description reduce, in
    the appropriate limit, to the kinetic description?

If you believe Newton is exactly right, then the kinetic
description is an approximation. The whole point is to say
what the approximation is, when it holds, and how good it is.


================================================================
PART 3. THE BOLTZMANN EQUATION, BRIEFLY
================================================================

In 1872 Ludwig Boltzmann proposed an evolution equation for f
that has stood up remarkably well:

    (d/dt + v . grad_x) f = Q(f, f).

Let us unpack the left and right sides.

THE LEFT SIDE (FREE TRANSPORT)
    If particles did not interact, each would move in a
    straight line: x -> x + v t, v constant. The kinetic
    density would just drift along those straight lines.
    The operator (d/dt + v . grad_x) is exactly the derivative
    of f along such a straight-line trajectory. It says
    "how does f change as you ride along a particle that is
    not colliding with anything".

THE RIGHT SIDE (COLLISIONS)
    Q(f, f) is the collision term. It says how collisions
    change the distribution.

For a gas with an interaction potential Phi, Q has a specific
formula. For a pair with incoming velocities (v, v*), a
collision produces outgoing velocities (v', v*'). The
transformation (v, v*) -> (v', v*') is the two-body scattering
map for Phi. It conserves momentum v + v* and kinetic energy
|v|^2 + |v*|^2.

The Boltzmann collision operator is then:

    Q(f, f)(x, v)
      = integral over v* and impact parameter b of
            [f(x, v') f(x, v*') - f(x, v) f(x, v*)]
            * |v - v*| * db dv*.

INTERPRETATION:
  - The first term inside the bracket is a "gain": pairs
    arriving with velocities (v', v*') happen to scatter
    INTO velocities (v, v*). That ADDS to f(v).
  - The second term is a "loss": pairs at velocities (v, v*)
    scatter AWAY, into some other pair. That REMOVES from
    f(v).
  - |v - v*| is the relative speed. Faster pairs collide more
    often, so the rate is bigger.
  - db is the infinitesimal "impact parameter" (how much the
    trajectories are off-center). Its range is fixed by the
    interaction range.
  - v' and v*' are the OUTPUT velocities of a two-body
    scattering of pair (v, v*) with impact parameter b.

The equation is nonlinear because Q involves f twice. Two
particles have to meet for a collision.

For the usual hard-sphere gas, the transformation and the
integral simplify to the classical Boltzmann equation you
might see in a textbook. For a smooth potential, the same
formula applies with a different scattering map.

This equation is a MIRACLE, in the sense that it says
something simple about a complicated many-particle system.
The whole mathematical subject of this paper is: can we
actually prove, starting from Newton, that f really does obey
this equation?

For a full history of that question, see Part 11. For now,
the answer is the paper's theorem.


================================================================
PART 4. THE BOLTZMANN-GRAD SCALING:
        THE ONLY INTERESTING REGIME
================================================================

Imagine you have N particles in a box. You can vary two
things:
  - How densely packed they are.
  - How strong and how long-range each pairwise interaction
    is.

If particles are dense and interactions are strong, they are
coupled all the time, and no simple kinetic equation is
true. If particles are sparse and interactions are weak,
they never collide, and there is no dynamics either. There
is only one regime where something interesting can happen:

    The mean free path is comparable to the size of the box.

The mean free path is the average distance a particle travels
between two collisions. If a particle rarely collides in the
time it takes to cross the box, it is effectively free. If
it collides many times, it is part of a dense liquid.

The Boltzmann-Grad scaling is the specific way to keep the
mean free path finite as the interaction range shrinks. Here
is how it goes.

SETUP.
  Let eps > 0 be a small number -- "the interaction range".
  Scale the potential so that:
      Phi_eps(q) = Phi(q / eps).
  That is, Phi_eps is supported in |q| < eps. Outside that,
  particles do not feel each other.
  Let mu be the "activity":
      mu = eps^-2.
  mu is the expected number of particles per unit volume (up
  to constants).  In other words, the number density is on
  the order of eps^-2.

WHY THIS IS THE INTERESTING CHOICE.
  Two particles interact when their distance is less than
  eps. Their "cross-sectional area" for a collision is roughly
  pi eps^2. So the collision rate per particle is roughly:

      (number of particles per unit volume)
         x (cross-sectional area)
         x (relative speed)
      = mu * eps^2 * (typical relative speed).

  With mu = eps^-2, the collision rate is finite as eps -> 0.
  A particle collides at a rate of order one: it has a
  fixed mean free path. Meanwhile the interaction range eps
  shrinks to zero, so each individual collision takes
  vanishing time.

  That is exactly the "hard point of zero size" idealization,
  but stated for smooth potentials. So the setting is:

      Infinitesimally short-range collisions,
      at a finite, order-one rate.

IN 3D, THAT IS THE SAME AS:
  - Particle density going to infinity like eps^-2.
  - Interaction length scale going to zero like eps.
  - Collisions still happening at O(1) rate per particle.

More generally, in d spatial dimensions the Boltzmann-Grad
activity is mu = eps^-(d-1). In d = 3 that gives
mu = eps^-2. Any other exponent makes the limit either
trivial (rare gas, no collisions) or singular (dense gas,
no kinetic equation). Only the d-1 choice keeps the mean
free path finite. That is why Boltzmann and Grad both
picked it.

Now we can state the theorem in the language of the previous
parts.


================================================================
PART 5. THE MAIN THEOREM
================================================================

Let us first fix the model carefully. Three ingredients:
the potential, the initial state, and the dynamics.

--------------------- THE POTENTIAL ---------------------

The potential Phi satisfies:

  A1. Phi is radial: Phi(q) = phi(|q|) for some function phi.
  A2. Phi has finite range: Phi(q) = 0 for |q| >= 1.
      (So after scaling, the range is eps.)
  A3. Phi is C^2 away from the origin, and either also C^2
      at the origin, OR has a repulsive singularity at the
      origin (phi(r) -> +infinity as r -> 0). Never an
      attractive blowup.
  A4. Phi is STABLE in the sense of David Ruelle:
      there is a constant B >= 0 such that for ANY finite
      configuration of distinct positions,
          sum_{i<j} Phi(q_i - q_j) >= -B * (number of particles).

Assumption A4 is the only sign condition on Phi. It is
slightly stronger than saying "no bound state of infinitely
many particles" and slightly weaker than saying "no
attractive well". In practice it is very mild. It excludes
potentials that would let infinitely many particles collapse
to a single point and win an arbitrarily deep energy well,
because that would make no sense thermodynamically.

------------------- THE INITIAL STATE --------------------

At time 0, the gas is sampled from a specific random law.

The overall law is GRAND-CANONICAL: the number of particles N
is random, and the probability of a specific state with
N particles at positions and velocities
    (x_1, v_1), ..., (x_N, v_N)
is weighted by:

    mu^N / N!  *  f_0(x_1, v_1) * ... * f_0(x_N, v_N)
      *  (indicator: all pairwise distances |x_i - x_j| > eps)
      *  (overall normalizing constant).

Here f_0(x, v) is a fixed one-particle phase-space density,
the "initial distribution" out of which particles are
sampled independently. mu^N/N! is the standard
grand-canonical weight for unordered particles.

The indicator (all |x_i - x_j| > eps) is an EXCLUSION: no
two particles may start within distance eps of each other.
This is a convention that mirrors the "hard core" of the
interaction; in particular, initial potential energy is
exactly zero.

The law is normalized so that it is a proper probability.
It is a well-defined probability distribution because the
partition function is finite: 1 <= Z_eps <= exp(mu).

----------------------- THE DYNAMICS -----------------------

Once we have a set of N particles, they evolve by the
scaled Newton equations:

    d x_i / dt = v_i
    d v_i / dt = - (1/eps) * sum_{j: j != i}
                       grad Phi( (x_i - x_j) / eps )

This system has a UNIQUE GLOBAL SOLUTION for any finite
initial energy, because of the stability assumption A4
plus the finite range. It also conserves phase-space
volume and total energy (potential + kinetic).

This is not trivial: without stability you could get
collapsing to a point in finite time. With it, the
particles are guaranteed not to blow up.

----------------- THE FACTORIAL MARGINALS -----------------

Given the above dynamics, we define the s-particle
distribution:

    F_s^eps(t, z_1, ..., z_s)
    = mu^(-s) * E [ sum over ordered distinct labels
                    i_1, ..., i_s of
                    delta at (z_{i_1}(t), ..., z_{i_s}(t)) ]

In words: F_s^eps(t, z_1, ..., z_s) dz_1 ... dz_s is the
expected number of ORDERED DISTINCT s-tuples of particles
you would find with the s-th one near z_s, etc. The
mu^(-s) normalizer ensures that as eps -> 0, F_s^eps is
finite.

The main result:

---------------- MAIN THEOREM (RESTATEMENT) ----------------

Fix Phi satisfying A1-A4 above and a beta > 0.

Suppose:
  (H1) f_0 >= 0 is a probability density on R^3 x R^3.
  (H2) f_0 is C^1 with spatially summable bounds:
           ||f_0||_Bol,2beta
             + ||grad_x f_0||_Bol,2beta < inf.
       (The Bol norm is essentially a supremum over spatial
       unit cubes with a Gaussian weight exp(2beta |v|^2) in
       velocity. It is finite for any well-behaved probability
       density with Gaussian velocity tails.)
  (H3) On the time interval [0, T], the classical Boltzmann
       equation with initial data f_0 has a
       nonnegative C^1 solution f(t, x, v) with a uniform
       Gaussian bound:
           sup_{0 <= t <= T, x, v} exp(2beta |v|^2) f(t, x, v)
           < infinity.

Then the factorial marginals of the Newtonian gas satisfy:

    For every fixed integer s >= 1,

        lim_{eps -> 0}  sup_{0 <= t <= T}
            ||F_s^eps(t) - f(t)^{tensor s}||_L1
        = 0.

WHAT THIS SAYS IN WORDS:
    As the interaction range goes to zero and the density
    goes to infinity at the Boltzmann-Grad scaling, the
    s-particle phase-space distribution converges, in L^1
    and uniformly on any time interval where Boltzmann is
    well-defined, to the s-fold product of the true
    Boltzmann solution f(t, x, v).

That last phrase is what people call "propagation of chaos"
or "molecular chaos": the s-particle joint distribution
becomes the s-fold independent product of the one-particle
distribution. In other words, distinct low-dimensional
particles behave independently in the limit.

If you are comfortable with tensor products: the "s-fold
product" f(t)^{tensor s}(z_1, ..., z_s) is just the function
f(t, z_1) * f(t, z_2) * ... * f(t, z_s).

The norm ||.||_L1 is the usual L^1 norm of the difference,
i.e., the total variation of the difference of the two
distributions. Convergence in L^1 is about as strong as it
gets for this kind of object. It is a density-level
convergence, not just an observable-level one.

----------------------- WHY IT MATTERS -----------------------

Several words in the theorem are load-bearing. Let us call
them out one by one.

"UNIFORMLY ON THE WHOLE INTERVAL [0, T]".
  Not just for short times. The classical Boltzmann equation
  has a short-time solution on any reasonable class, so a
  short-time result is less impressive than it sounds. This
  theorem is for as long as Boltzmann itself works well
  (regularity plus Gaussian tail). If Boltzmann has a global
  solution on that class, this gives a global result.

"ANY STABLE RADIAL POTENTIAL".
  Not just hard spheres. Not just positive finite-range.
  The class includes attractive wells.

"CLUSTERS ARE ALLOWED".
  The microscopic dynamics is not stopped or modified to
  forbid simultaneous three-body and higher encounters, or
  particles sticking together in bound clusters. The
  conclusion is about the actual Newtonian orbit.

"NO RESTRICTION ON THE CROSS-SECTION".
  The scattering map for Phi can be extremely singular and
  even non-injective: several impact parameters could give
  the same outgoing angle, or the differential cross-section
  could be infinite on a set of positive measure. The proof
  does not require any sort of monotonicity or regularity of
  the angle-to-velocity map beyond what Newtonian mechanics
  guarantees.

These four points make the theorem substantially stronger
than what was available before this paper. See Part 9 for
the precise comparison.


================================================================
PART 6. UNPACKING THE HYPOTHESES:
        WHY THE CLASS IS WIDE
================================================================

The theorem has only three real hypotheses: on the potential,
on the initial data, on the existence of a classical
Boltzmann solution. Let us be honest about how restrictive
each one is.

---------------------------- A1-A4 ----------------------------

The potential class was already discussed. Let me stress
what it does NOT rule out:

  - ATTRACTIVE WELLS. Phi can be negative somewhere. It
    just cannot be negative so much that unboundedly many
    particles can collapse to save energy.
  - SINGULARITIES AT THE ORIGIN. Phi(r) -> +infinity as
    r -> 0 for a repulsive core, or a finite limit, both
    allowed. Only an attractive singularity is excluded,
    and that is excluded by stability anyway.
  - ARBITRARILY COMPLICATED SCATTERING. Two particles with
    a nice potential very often produce a highly non-injective
    scattering map: two different impact parameters can give
    the same outgoing angle. The theorem does not care.

Possibly the biggest "no" here: it does not require a
MONOTONE potential. Potentials with both attractive and
repulsive parts are fine as long as stability holds.

------------------------------- H1-H2 -------------------------

The initial data class: nonnegative C^1 probability density
with a Gaussian supremum in velocity and a Gaussian bound on
the spatial gradient. Practically, this excludes only
densities that have velocity tails much fatter than
Gaussian. It includes every "physically reasonable" initial
state you might hope to write down.

The "spatially summable" part of the Bol norm is about the
spatial density being in a weighted L^infinity sense over
unit cubes. It is slightly weaker than being bounded, and
strictly stronger than being merely L^1.

------------------------------- H3 ------------------------

The most important hypothesis, and the most honest one:
the classical Boltzmann equation itself has to have a
regular solution on [0, T] with a Gaussian tail.

Why is this not circular? Because Boltzmann is a much
simpler equation than the N-particle Newton system. It is
a single integro-differential equation for one function
f(t, x, v). There is a large classical theory of local
existence in suitable weighted C^1 space, and of global
existence on various physically meaningful classes. Any
interval [0, T] on which Boltzmann is well-behaved is a
legitimate target for the theorem.

The word "regular" in the paper title -- "on regular kinetic
intervals" -- refers exactly to this. It does not mean the
interval is short, or the data is small, or we are near
equilibrium. It means:

    On this interval, the kinetic description exists,
    is classical, nonnegative, and has a Gaussian tail
    in velocity.

That is the weakest possible "well-posedness" condition you
would want.

----------------- A THEOREM WITH THIS SHAPE IS OFTEN ----------------
                 CALLED "CONDITIONALLY GLOBAL"

A common pattern in mathematical kinetic theory: prove the
limit for short times, then eat the rest by a bootstrapping
argument. This paper does not do that. It proves the limit
for any regular interval directly, using a single long-time
estimate.

That estimate -- the "single energy budget" -- is the crown
jewel of the paper. Part 8, Step 4, explains what it does.


================================================================
PART 7. THE COROLLARY: WHAT THIS MEANS FOR
        TIME-AVERAGED OBSERVABLES
================================================================

The main theorem (Part 5 of this document) is about the
s-particle marginal, which is a complete distribution.
Very often in practice we care about observables: a smooth
function a(x, v) with compact support, and the question
"what is the average of a over all particles at time t?".

For a good initial state, the theorem has a clean corollary.

---------------------- COROLLARY ----------------------

Under the hypotheses of the main theorem, let a(x, v) be any smooth
test function of compact support, and let eta > 0 be any
positive number. Define:

    A_eps(t) = (1/mu) sum_{i=1}^N a(x_i(t), v_i(t)),

    A_kin(t) = integral of a(x, v) f(t, x, v) dx dv.

Then:

    lim_{eps -> 0} sup_{0 <= t <= T} P( |A_eps(t) - A_kin(t)| > eta ) = 0.

WHAT THIS MEANS.
  The empirical average, over all particles, of any smooth
  phase-space observable, converges IN PROBABILITY to the
  Boltzmann prediction, at every deterministic time t in
  [0, T], uniformly.

This is what people usually want from a kinetic theorem: a
concrete statement about what a measurement would show, with
an error that goes to zero.

HOW IT IS PROVED.
  From the first two factorial marginals F_1 and F_2. The
  variance of A_eps is a variance of the second factorial
  marginal minus the square of the mean, computed against a.
  The main theorem makes ||F_1^eps - f||_1 and ||F_2^eps - f x f||_1
  both go to zero. A Chebyshev bound finishes the job.

Nothing complicated. Once you have L^1 convergence of the
first two marginals, the empirical-measure conclusion is a
short and standard computation.


================================================================
PART 8. HOW THE PROOF WORKS
================================================================

This is the meat. The proof has six major steps. Each one
tackles one of the "why is this hard" obstacles.

Before we start, one paragraph about WHY IT IS HARD.

The difficulty is not subtle. If you take two particles and
watch them for a long time in a real gas, they interact,
separate, come back many times (due to being bounced around
by other particles). Two particles that started independent
become correlated by their history, and the "s" particles
you are tracking have their own history of prior interactions
that involves all the OTHER particles in the gas. These
shared histories are what makes the problem hard.

For hard spheres, Lanford's original 1975 proof handled this
on a short time interval, but the error term GROWS
exponentially with time. Naive short-time theorems are stuck
at T of order 1 or smaller. The modern proof has to defeat
this exponential growth over the whole interval.

The paper does this via a very careful layering of scales.
Let us take them one at a time.

==========================================================
STEP 1. LAYERS AND CELLS
==========================================================

Take the whole interval [0, T] and split it:

  - Macro-layers: split [0, T] into L layers, each of length
        b = T/L.
    L is a large fixed integer, chosen once and for all.

  - Micro-cells: split each layer into J cells, each of
    length b/J = T/(LJ).
    J is a very large, eps-dependent number, chosen as a
    fixed power of (1 + |log eps|). Say J = (1+|log eps|)^Q
    for a fixed Q.

L gives the time-resolution of the long-time argument. J is
a technical cutoff: it must be much larger than any
polynomial in the number of particles, so that accidental
near-boundary events cost negligibly.

The reason to have TWO resolutions is:
  - L big and b small is what makes the energy budget work
    (see Step 4).
  - J big and cells even smaller is what makes the exposure
    argument uniform in eps.

You can think of it as: L is a physics-scale number, J is a
mathematical-fine-scale number.

==========================================================
STEP 2. EXACT WHOLE-COMPONENT EXPANSION
==========================================================

Fix a cell [a, b] of length delta. In that cell, we want to
express the s-particle marginal at the top of the cell in
terms of what happened at the bottom.

Lanford's classic technique: split the s particles into a
binary tree of pairwise collisions. Each collision is
represented by a "gain" or "loss" term from the Boltzmann
equation.

That technique has a specific problem. If THREE or more
particles are simultaneously interacting (or worse, if
some particles form a bound cluster and stay together),
Lanford's binary-tree expansion of the current cell only
counts what happens outside the cluster. Inside the cluster,
the binary-tree structure is wrong: they are not colliding
one pair at a time, they are moving as a single object.

The paper sidesteps this by grouping particles into WHOLE
INTERACTION COMPONENTS.

DEFINITION: In a cell [a, b], define a "contact graph" whose
vertices are the labels (particles), with an edge between
two labels if that pair comes within distance eps at any
moment during [a, b]. The CONNECTED COMPONENTS of this graph
are called interaction components.

Each interaction component is the correct unit of the
microscopic dynamics in the cell:
  - Different components have strictly no interaction
    during the cell.
  - Within a component, forces are handled exactly by
    Newton.

The expansion is then a sum over "cell diagrams". A cell
diagram is:
  - Pick a root component A that contains the s tracked
    particles at the top of the cell.
  - Decompose the rest of the labels into "detached groups"
    that touched A or each other at different "generation"
    times.
  - For each detached group, add a sign that comes from
    finite boolean inclusion-exclusion on the initial
    polymer partition.

The important consequences:
  - EXACTNESS. The sum of the cell diagrams, over all
    possible diagrams, equals the true s-particle marginal
    at the top, given the marginal at the bottom. No
    approximation.
  - NO STOPPING. The expansion keeps all clusters and all
    many-body interactions. It does not artificially stop
    the dynamics to force a binary structure.
  - ENERGY CONTROL. Since each component is a genuine
    Newtonian subsystem, its energy is exactly conserved
    (not just approximately), which is what makes the
    Gaussian estimates in later steps work.

The cost: many diagrams. The paper bounds the number of them
by (C(1+m))^{Cm} in the number m of added labels.

This is the first key trick: keep whole components, not
individual collisions.

==========================================================
STEP 3. CENTERING AT LAYER CUTS
==========================================================

At the end of each macro-layer, we subtract off independent
factors f(t_{j-1}) to isolate the remaining correlation
between the s tracked particles and everything else.

The point: at the top of layer j, we have an exact formula
for F_s^eps(t_j) as a sum of terms. Layer by layer, we
want to compare it to f(t_j)^{tensor s}. So we insert
zero -- by adding and subtracting the independent-product
expression -- to write:

    F_s^eps(t_j)
      = sum over "centered" terms
      + sum over "uncentered" terms.

The centered terms vanish by cancellation. The uncentered
terms are what we have to bound.

The cancellation is delicate because it has to happen
INSIDE each interaction component. You cannot break a
bound cluster to make cancellation local; that would use
information the actual cluster does not have. The paper's
exact "componentwise centering identity" shows that the
cancellation can be performed without splitting any
component.

Cost: a factor 2^U at a cut, where U is the cap on the
number of upper roots. This 2^U is what shows up later as a
"cut cost".

This is the second key trick: cancel at the level of
components, not binary trees.

==========================================================
STEP 4. THE SINGLE ENERGY BUDGET
==========================================================

Here is the crown jewel.

NAIVE APPROACH (FAILS).
  Bound each layer's exponential growth separately, multiply
  over L layers, take a root. The base of the exponential
  grows like Cb per label, and after L layers the total is
  (Cb)^{L*p} for p labels. That is exponential in p and
  also in L, hopeless.

NEW APPROACH (WORKS).
  Use a SINGLE augmented energy budget across all L layers.

IDEA. Imagine a "God's-eye view" of the whole history of a
history term:
  - Every label has a "source" time when it is created
    (from an independent factor f).
  - Every label has a "departure" time when it disappears
    (or the observation time, for surviving top labels).
  - The system is not the same as the physical system at
    any single time. It is the "augmented" system that
    freezes each label before its source and after its
    departure.

In this augmented system:
  - The number of distinct labels is p.
  - The system starts with h_0 "top" labels (the s
    observed particles plus other top labels) at time 0.
  - Additional labels are injected over time from f-factors.
  - Labels depart over time as their force-systems end.

By a "chain of Hamiltonians" argument (tracking energy
across all cuts and injections), the SUM of squared
velocities across all p labels -- past, present, and future
-- is bounded by:

    S + 2B p,

where S is the sum of squared velocities at the sources
(fixed from the source-factor f's).

This is a single bound, independent of how many layers we
have and independent of what happens in individual cells.
It is the sole source of the "velocity is controlled"
estimate, used everywhere later.

CONSEQUENCES.
  - Time factors: for each layer, the ordered-time simplex
    contributes (b)^(n_j) / n_j! for n_j additions. Over L
    layers, this gives b^n / (product n_j!) times an
    integral over the total simplex.
  - Repeatedly carried velocities: each label that persists
    across layers of its formal lifetime pays a factor of
    sqrt(L). Over all p labels, this multiplies to L^(p/2).
  - Net effect: the total bound per history with h_0 top
    roots is
        (C * T / sqrt(L))^p  *  (some correction factor
                                    for h_0),
    where CT/sqrt(L) is < 1 for L large.

There it is. We are not just "small on a short time"; we
have a base < 1 for L large, on an arbitrarily long interval
[0, T]. That is what makes long-time results possible.

The (T/L)^p timing is what generates the base CT/sqrt(L).
The L^(p/2) is the L-part that the L-splitting creates. Their
product is (T^p) / L^(p/2) = (T/sqrt(L))^p. Choose L > C T
and the base is exponentially small.

This is the third key trick: single augmented energy budget,
not per-layer.

==========================================================
STEP 5. CAUSAL EXPOSURE
==========================================================

The energy budget handles a subtle point: it produces
estimates that are small on AVERAGE over all histories, but
some highly crowded correlation histories still survive.

For those, we need a second mechanism to make them small.
The paper uses a beautiful trick called "exposure".

IDEA: ROTATIONAL SYMMETRY GIVES SMALL ANGULAR FACTORS.

Collisions are two-body. A two-body collision is described by:
  - Incoming relative velocity g.
  - Impact parameter b (a vector in the plane perpendicular
    to g).
Equivalently, in "spherical" coordinates:
  - Relative speed s = |g|.
  - Impact radius rho in (0, 1).
  - A rotation matrix R in SO(3) that describes the overall
    orientation of the pair.

The measure in these coordinates is:

    8 pi^2 s^3 rho ds d rho dR.

Here dR is the standard Haar measure on rotations SO(3). Since SO(3) is compact, R has a uniform distribution: no direction is preferred.

Now consider: before a particle undergoes a scattering with
impact radius rho, it is following a straight-line free path.
Its future path depends on the scattering direction, which
depends on the rotation R.

KEY OBSERVATION: BEFORE THE FIRST CONTACT WITH ANOTHER
PARTICLE, THE SCATTERING DIRECTION IS COMPLETELY FREE. The
angular variable R can be anywhere on the sphere of
directions, with uniform measure.

If you need this free rotation to hit a specific target
(another particle within distance eps at a specific time
tau after launch, with relative speed s), the set of
directions that succeeds has spherical measure at most

    C * eps / (s * tau)   (roughly: the tube sweeping out).

This is the paper's Lemma exp:tube. It is a small quantity
whenever tau >> eps. It is linear in eps, not quadratic.
The exact form involves the Lipschitz speed of the target
curve and a lower bound on s, but the key point is that it
goes to zero as eps shrinks and elapsed time grows.

Multiply this over many such "exposures" and you get
exponential suppression in the number of exposures. That
is the mechanism that kills highly correlated histories.

WHY THIS IS ELEGANT.
  - It does NOT need any regularity of the differential
    cross-section. It only uses that the rotation R enters
    linearly, and the uniform measure gives a uniform
    spherical marginal for the outgoing direction.
  - It handles non-injective scattering. Even if the
    scattering map is many-to-one, each preimage has its
    own measure, and the small-target argument is unaffected.
  - It handles genuine scattering and passage on the same
    footing: the difference is that genuine scattering
    hides BOTH descendant branches (the parent's velocity
    changes), while force-free passage hides only the new
    branch.

The figure in the original paper visualizes this: a
genuine scattering "hides" two branches and a passage
"hides" one branch, and the whole remaining construction
can be computed without the hidden parameters up to first
exposure.

The paper's tube lemma makes this rigorous, using a "tube"
estimate: a ray hitting a target at distance delta within
time T has angular measure at most C * delta / (time).
Multiply over many selected histories.

This is the fourth key trick: rotational symmetry as a
source of small angular factors.

==========================================================
STEP 6. CLOSURE AND INDUCTION
==========================================================

Now we have all the pieces, but they need to be assembled
in the right ORDER. This is the most technical part of the
paper, and the part where careful bookkeeping matters.

The thresholds.
  For layer j, define four integers:
    K_j = the particle-count cap for the physical stop
          in layer j.
    D_j = the "ancestry" cap for trial components, i.e.,
          the maximum size of an ancestry that can be
          ignored.
    U_j = the particle-count cap for the whole expanded
          upper block above layer j.
    W_j = the "witness size" cap for a stopped-cloud
          witness in layer j.

The schedule is set up by starting at j = L and working
DOWN to j = 1. At step j we already have U_j either from
the previous step (the U_{j-1} of step j+1) or from the
initial U_L = ceil(P). At step j we set:

    K_j     = ceil(P^3 U_j)
    D_j     = ceil(P^3 K_j)
    W_j     = ceil((1+U_j+K_j+D_j)^nu)   for a fixed nu >= 4
    U_{j-1} = ceil((1+U_j+W_j+ sum_{ell>j} W_ell)^nu)

where P = 1 + |log eps|. The last line is skipped at j = 1
(there is no layer 0).

Why this order?
  - Start at the LAST layer (top of time). We need U_L to
    be at least the s particles being tracked, so give
    U_L a small but nonzero value.
  - K_j must dominate U_j so the physical stop at layer j
    is bigger than the current upper cap. (Otherwise you
    could not detect any removal.)
  - D_j must dominate K_j so the trial-component ancestry
    cap covers the physical stop. (Otherwise you would
    detect a giant polymer before you have any way to
    account for it.)
  - W_j must dominate K_j and D_j so witness costs are
    bounded.
  - U_{j-1} must dominate U_j, W_j, and all future witnesses
    so the induction hypothesis holds across the previous
    layer.

The INDUCTION (forwards in time).
  We prove, for each layer j = 1, 2, ..., L:

    (I_j-a)   P(B_j(D_j))  <=  exp(-8 D_j)
    (I_j-b)   R_j          <=  exp(-8 K_j)

  where:
    B_j(D_j)  = event "some ancestry size exceeds D_j
                within the first j layers".
    R_j       = max(Q_j, Z_j), the largest of the
                L^1 norms of the physical remainder and
                the combined centered remainder in layer j.
                Q_j alone is the physical remainder;
                Z_j includes the reference-cutoff mismatch
                after centering.
    P(...)    = probability under the actual process.

The induction goes like:
  - ORDER MATTERS. The proof makes three separate claims,
    in this order:
      a) Bound the reference-cutoff mismatch.
      b) Bound the probability of a layer-j witness.
      c) Bound the physical remainder.
  - Each claim uses only the bounds for LOWEST-index layers
    (< j), which the induction hypothesis already gives.
  - Then all three combine to prove (I_j-a) and (I_j-b).

Why the ORDER matters. The reference-cutoff mismatch uses
only layers < j (in the fully-expanded form). The probability
of a layer-j witness uses the induction hypothesis on
layers < j. The physical remainder uses the probability
just proven for layer j. If you tried to use the layer-j
bound to prove the layer-j bound, you would be circular.
The proof is written explicitly to avoid this.

CONSEQUENCES.
  - R_L goes to 0 at rate exp(-8 K_L). K_L is a large fixed
    power of P = 1 + |log eps|, so this is a very strong
    smallness: faster than any fixed power of eps.
  - For the FINAL theorem, take s <= U_L, expand the
    centered singleton array of size s. The error is a
    finite sum of bounded terms, each going to zero.
    Fixed-size terms of the physical remainder go to zero
    by Part 5, Step 6's finite-size theorem.
  - Take m large (larger than the finite constants from
    summability). Terms with more than m labels are
    exp(-m). The finitely many remaining terms go to zero
    by the finite-size result. Let eps -> 0 first, then
    m -> infinity. Both sides go to zero.

This is the fifth key trick: an explicit, non-circular
induction that uses layer j-1 to prove layer j.

----------------- THE ROLE OF "COMPLETED" TERMS -----------------

There is one more concept worth naming, because it recurs.
A "completed history" is a term that has been fully
expanded through all layers it enters, with all the
simple-family cancellations performed at centered cuts.
Its inputs are:
  - independent copies of f(t), from the short-layer residual
    estimate;
  - one-body residual factors from the residual estimate;
  - initial centered factors from the initial-data estimate.

These are the terms the summability result controls, and
these are the terms the final theorem sums.

There are also "mixed uncentered upper blocks" -- finite
upper blocks that you have to leave uncentered because you
are not sure what the cancellation should do to their
roots. Those are handled separately, via the reference
mismatch.

A subtle point: the residual-estimate and summability-
estimate use DIFFERENT notions of "f" at different levels.
The residual r_{u,t}^eps is a small deviation of a short
interval of the actual f from its reference-tree
approximation. The summability of completed terms uses the
actual f as its source factors. These are not in conflict:
the residual is a separate small thing that appears in the
statement of exact-centering, not in the summability
itself.

------------------- WHAT GOES IN b* -------------------

The final constant b* is chosen after all the estimates,
so that:

    C * b * sqrt(L) < 1

with room to spare, where C is the fixed constant from
the short-layer residual estimate and the
energy-budget estimate. This is the "choose L big enough"
step: b = T/L is set by choosing L, then residual estimates
are checked for that b. All the choices are fixed as eps -> 0.


================================================================
PART 9. WHAT IS ACTUALLY NEW
================================================================

It is worth being precise about what was already known and
what this paper adds.

----------------------------- LANFORD (1975) -------------

Lanford proved the Boltzmann-Grad limit for hard spheres on
a short time interval. "Short" means T of order 1 over the
collision rate, with the error growing exponentially in T
so that beyond T of order 0.1 or so the bound is useless.

Lanford's proof introduced the idea of the collision-tree
expansion: writing the marginal as a sum over binary
collision trees.

--------------------- GALLAGHER-SAINT-RAYMOND-TEXIER -----------------

A detailed geometric analysis of "recollisions" (particles
re-colliding after having been bounced by others), including
convergence rates in the hard-sphere case.

--------------------- PULVIRENTI-SAFFIRIO-SIMONELLA (2014) -----------------

A short-time theorem for the full class of stable radial
potentials, INCLUDING attractive potentials. Their result
shows that the class of potentials in this paper is
naturally handled by two-body scattering, without needing
a monotone potential.

--------------------- ILLNER-PULVIRENTI (1986, 1989) -----------------

Global validity of Boltzmann for a very RARE gas in a
vacuum: f decaying in space so that particle number is very
small. Global-in-time, but for a special class of data
(no recollisions lead to real correlations).

--------------------- PULVIRENTI-SIMONELLA (2017) -----------------

Quantitative propagation of chaos over many short times,
by tracking correlation errors removed at cut times.

--------------------- BODINEAU-GALLAGHER-SAINT-RAYMOND-SIMONELLA (2022) -----------------

Cluster expansion directly on physical trajectories,
distinguishing genuine collisions from geometric overlaps.
The physical intuition here is closest to the present paper.

--------------------- DENG-HANI-MA (2025) -----------------

Long-time Boltzmann-Grad limit for HARD SPHERES, using a
layered expansion that retains the earlier histories of
correlated remainders. The strategy of "expand the physical
remainder, extract the factors approximated by f, retain
the rest as correlations" is theirs.

--------------------- THIS PAPER -----------------

Extends Deng-Hani-Ma's framework to the FULL stable radial
class of SMOOTH potentials, at the cost of a more involved
expansion. The main conceptual changes:

  1. Whole-component expansion. DHM25 uses binary collision
     trees. For smooth potentials, bound clusters can form
     and cannot be un-bundled into binary collisions without
     losing energy control. The paper keeps whole
     interaction components throughout.

  2. Stability-based energy budget. Since each component's
     Hamiltonian is exactly conserved, the augmented
     energy is exactly bounded (not just approximately,
     which would not survive the long-time limit). The
     stability assumption A4 is what makes this work
     uniformly in p.

  3. Rotational exposure without an inverse cross-section.
     DHM25 handles hard spheres where the differential
     cross-section is essentially constant. Smooth
     potentials do not require any differential
     cross-section formula at all; the paper uses
     rotational symmetry directly and works even for
     non-injective scattering.

  4. Explicit regularity hypotheses on the kinetic solution.
     The paper makes the Gaussian bound on f a named
     hypothesis, not something bootstrapped.

The breakthrough idea is that you can combine a DHM-style
long-time framework with the PSS14-style "whole component"
treatment if you use a single augmented energy budget across
all layers and a symmetric exposure argument. This is a
genuinely new synthesis.


================================================================
PART 10. WHAT IS *NOT* PROVED
================================================================

Honest scope. The paper does not prove:

  1. Existence of the Boltzmann solution. That is a separate
     (classical) matter. The theorem is conditional on
     classical existence on [0, T] with a Gaussian bound.

  2. Anything about non-Gaussian velocity tails. The
     theorem requires f_0 and f(t) to have a Gaussian
     envelope in velocity (the Bol norm, with exponent
     2 beta). For initial data with slower-decaying tails,
     the classical Boltzmann equation itself is less
     understood and the estimates in this paper would
     need to be redone. The Newtonian side of the theorem,
     unlike the kinetic side, has no such restriction.

  3. Convergence of higher-order correlations than fixed
     order. The theorem is for fixed s as eps -> 0. Not
     for s = s(eps) growing.

  4. Uniform control over "exceptional times". The theorem
     is uniform on [0, T], but the empirical-measure
     corollary is about deterministic times, not about
     supremum over t in a sample path.

  5. A quantitative rate. The proof gives qualitative
     convergence; explicit convergence rates are not
     derived, though the proof certainly suggests that
     ones exist and are power-of-eps or better.

  6. Infinite-range potentials. The finite-range assumption
     A2 is used critically in several places (energy budget,
     scaling). It would be a substantial extension to
     remove it.

These are natural next steps but they are not claimed.


================================================================
PART 11. WHERE THIS FITS IN THE LINEAGE
================================================================

The question "can we derive the Boltzmann equation from
Newton?" has been asked since Boltzmann himself in 1872.
The rigorous proofs fall into a lineage:

  1949   Grad: formal Boltzmann derivation for hard spheres.
  1958   Grad: book treatment, with formal limits.
  1969   Ruelle: stability as the fundamental assumption.
  1975   Lanford: first rigorous short-time proof, hard spheres.
  1975   King: PhD thesis, positive finite-range potentials.
         (Short-time, similar strategy to Lanford.)
  1986   Illner-Pulvirenti: global validity for a rare gas in
         vacuum.
  1987   Pulvirenti: same, in 3D.
  1989   Illner-Pulvirenti: erratum and improved result.
  2014   Gallagher-Saint-Raymond-Texier: geometric analysis
         of recollisions. Detailed for hard spheres and a
         class of repulsive potentials.
  2014   Pulvirenti-Saffirio-Simonella: short-time theorem
         for the stable radial class (with attractive
         potentials). Establishes that two-body scattering
         is enough.
  2017   Pulvirenti-Simonella: quantitative propagation of
         chaos, tracking correlation errors.
  2022   Bodineau-Gallagher-Saint-Raymond-Simonella: cluster
         expansion on physical trajectories.
  2025   Deng-Hani-Ma: long-time for HARD SPHERES.
  2026   Bourbaki seminar summary of DHM25.
  2026   THIS PAPER: long-time for the FULL STABLE RADIAL
         class, using a whole-component expansion with a
         single energy budget.

The trajectory is: from short-time + hard spheres, to
long-time + hard spheres, to long-time + smooth stable
radial. The endpoint -- full regularity + long time +
physically interesting potential class -- is what this
paper achieves.

The "long time" part is the deep one. A short-time theorem
gives you a proof of the Boltzmann-Grad limit that is
correct but irrelevant to physics, because gases have
collections of collisions at every timescale. To say
something about an actual gas between observations, you
need uniform-in-time results. That is what long-time
kinetic theory is about. This paper is one of the very
few long-time derivations of Boltzmann for smooth
potentials in full generality.


================================================================
GLOSSARY
================================================================

(Definitions in roughly the order they appear.)

ACTIVITY, mu
    mu = eps^-2. The expected number of particles per unit
    volume (up to factors equal to pi). In the Boltzmann-
    Grad scaling, the density of a gas that stays "collision-
    active" while its interaction range shrinks to zero.

BOL NORM
    ||h||_Bol,gamma := sum over k in Z^3 of
         sup { exp(gamma |v|^2) |h(x, v)| :
               |x - k| <= 1, v in R^3 }.
    Roughly: for each unit cube k+[-1,1]^3 in position,
    take the sup of the Gaussian-weighted |h| over that
    cube and all velocities, then sum over all cubes. It
    is the natural norm for phase-space functions with
    Gaussian velocity tails and bounded weighted spatial
    envelope. The "Bol" stands for Boltzmann.

CELL (of a layer)
    One of the fine time subintervals into which a macro-
    layer is subdivided. Length b/J.

CLUSTER
    A group of particles that stays close enough to
    interact over an extended period of time. Bound clusters
    are groups with negative total energy relative to
    separation-at-infinity; they never break apart. Clusters
    are the main obstacle to the naive "tree of pairwise
    collisions" picture.

COLLISION KERNEL
    Q_Phi in the Boltzmann equation. For a pair
    (v, v*) and impact parameter b, it gives the rate at
    which that pair turns into (v', v*') via two-body
    scattering, and back. Depends only on the two-body
    scattering map of Phi, not on any many-body information.

CONTACT GRAPH
    A graph on a set of labels, with an edge between i
    and j if |x_i(t) - x_j(t)| <= eps for some t in the
    given time interval. Connected components are the
    "interaction components".

FACTORIAL MARGINAL
    The rescaled expectation of ordered distinct tuples
    of s particles. See F_s^eps in Part 5.

FINITE-RANGE POTENTIAL
    Phi(q) = 0 for |q| >= 1 (before scaling). After
    scaling, only pairs within distance eps interact.
    This makes "contact" a finite-time event, and it
    makes the energy budget work.

GAUSSIAN BOUND
    A bound of the form sup_{x,v} exp(alpha |v|^2) |h| < inf.
    It says the velocity distribution has at most Gaussian
    tails. Non-Gaussian initial data would require a very
    different proof.

GRAND-CANONICAL LAW
    A probability law on the number of particles N together
    with their joint state, weighted by mu^N/N! times a
    product of one-particle factors. Standard in statistical
    mechanics. In this paper it gives an exactly known
    initial state, which is crucial for the summability
    estimates.

INJECTION
    The act of adding a label to the current history at
    some time t. The label's position and velocity at
    injection are given by an independent input from f.
    Its subsequent motion is part of the augmented system.

MEAN FREE PATH
    Average distance a particle travels between collisions.
    Boltzmann-Grad scaling keeps it O(1) as eps -> 0.

MICRO-CELL
    See CELL.

NON-INJECTIVE SCATTERING
    Multiple different impact parameters give the same
    outgoing velocity pair. Common for smooth potentials.
    The paper's proof does not presume injectivity.

PATTERN
    A partition of a label set into connected components
    of the contact graph at a given cut. Used in the
    "patternwise centering" identity.

POTENTIAL, Phi
    The pairwise interaction energy. Phi_eps(q) = Phi(q/eps)
    so that the interaction range is eps. Assumed radial
    (depends only on |q|) and finite-range.

PROPAGATION OF CHAOS
    The phenomenon that initial correlations between
    distinct particles decay over time, so that joint
    distributions become products of one-particle
    distributions.

RECOLLISION
    Two particles interact, separate, and then interact
    again after being redirected by other particles. The
    main source of the "exponentially growing error" in
    Lanford's short-time result.

SCALING
    The relationship between the small parameter eps and
    the rest of the parameters (particle number, density,
    potential range). See Part 4.

STABILITY (Ruelle)
    The assumption that the sum of pairwise potential
    energies is bounded below by -B times the number of
    particles, for some fixed B. Excludes unbounded
    collapse.

WHOLE-COMPONENT EXPANSION
    The paper's main technical construction: partition the
    labels in a cell by connected components of the contact
    graph, and treat each component as a whole. Exact,
    energy-controlling, and cluster-safe.

WITNESS
    A positive finite whole-cell history constructed from a
    "bad event" in the actual dynamics. Used to bound the
    probability of a bad event by a sum of positive
    witness-kernel integrals.
