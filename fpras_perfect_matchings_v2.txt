0.1 What is a perfect matching?

Take a graph: dots called vertices, lines called edges joining pairs of
dots. A matching is a set of edges that never share a dot. It is called
perfect if every dot is touched by exactly one chosen edge. So a perfect
matching pairs everybody up, with no dot left alone.

Examples.
  - Four dots in a square (the cycle C4). The two ways to peel off two
    opposite edges are the perfect matchings.
  - The complete graph K4: every pair of dots is joined. Label the dots
    1,2,3,4. The perfect matchings are {12,34}, {13,24}, {14,23}. Three.
  - The complete graph K6 has 15 perfect matchings. In general K_n (n
    even) has (n-1)!! = 1*3*5*...*(n-1). Reason: dot 1 picks one of the
    n-1 partners, then recurse on the remaining n-2 dots.
  - An odd number of dots: no perfect matching, count is zero.

Write Z(G) for the number of perfect matchings of a graph G.

0.2 Three different jobs: find, count, sample.

FINDING one perfect matching is easy in principle for any graph. Jack
Edmonds gave a polynomial-time algorithm in 1965. "Polynomial" means the
running time grows like a fixed power of the input size, not like 2^n.

COUNTING all perfect matchings is hard. Leslie Valiant proved in 1979
that exact counting is #P-complete, even for bipartite graphs (graphs
whose dots split into two sides with edges only crossing between sides).
Nobody expects a fast exact algorithm, and none is known.

SAMPLING one perfect matching, roughly uniformly at random, is a third
job. It turns out that counting and sampling are almost the same problem:
if you can sample you can usually count, and vice versa. This paper builds
a sampler, and the sampler produces the count.

0.3 What is an approximation scheme, and what is an FPRAS?

Because exact counting is hopeless, we settle for approximation. Fix a
small number eps, say 0.01. We want a number Zhat that is guaranteed to
land between (1-eps)Z(G) and (1+eps)Z(G). That is relative error eps.

The algorithm is random, so it may occasionally fail. We allow it to fail
with probability at most some delta we choose. The point is that the
running time must only grow like the LOGARITHM of 1/delta, so making
failure extremely unlikely costs almost nothing.

FPRAS = Fully Polynomial Randomized Approximation Scheme. "Fully
polynomial" means the time is polynomial in the input length, in 1/eps,
and in log(1/delta). The next section states the main theorem, which is
exactly this guarantee for perfect matchings in arbitrary graphs.

IN PLAIN WORDS: we cannot count exactly and quickly, but we can count
within any accuracy eps with any confidence 1-delta, and the cost grows
only gently with 1/eps and barely at all with 1/delta. That is the best
kind of approximation result.

0.4 Why "general graphs" is the hard case.

For bipartite graphs, counting perfect matchings is counting the
permanent of a 0-1 matrix. Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda solved that in
2004 with an FPRAS. Their method keeps track of the two unmatched dots
("holes") and learns how to weight them so that a random walk mixes fast.

A plain copy of that trick fails for general graphs. Stefankovic, Vigoda
and Wilmes built graphs where any such "holes-only" walk either puts
almost no mass on the perfect matchings or moves so slowly it will not
finish. So a new idea is needed. This paper supplies one: it enlarges the
graph with harmless helper edges, arranges a balanced weight scale, and
proves a fresh inequality for a two-copy random walk. The general-graph
question had been open since 1989.

PART I. THE PROBLEM, THE HISTORY, AND THE PROOF AT A GLANCE
================================================================

1. The main theorem.

Throughout, G = (V,E) is a finite simple undirected graph: finitely many
dots, edges joining distinct pairs, no loops, no repeated edges. Let n =
|V|. When n is odd, Z(G) = 0 automatically.

THEOREM 1.1 (Main theorem).
There is a uniform classical randomized algorithm which, given G and
rational parameters 0 < eps < 1 and 0 < delta < 1/2, returns a
nonnegative rational number Zhat such that

    Pr[ (1-eps) Z(G) <= Zhat <= (1+eps) Z(G) ]  >=  1 - delta.

If Z(G) = 0, the output is zero with certainty. The worst-case bit
running time is a polynomial in the input encoding length, in 1/eps, and
in log(1/delta).

"Uniform" means one algorithm handles every input; there is no per-size
special-casing or precomputed advice.

Consequences, proved in Part X: we also count edge subsets with prescribed
vertex degrees, and matchings of a prescribed size, with the same
guarantees, and we can produce a nearly uniform sample of such an object.

2. History and significance.

Perfect matchings are the standard example that separates finding from
counting.

  - Edmonds (1965) finds a maximum matching in polynomial time in general
    graphs. Finding is easy.
  - Valiant (1979) proves exact counting is #P-complete even bipartite.
    Counting is hard.
  - Kasteleyn (1963) shows exact counting is polynomial for planar graphs,
    by connecting matchings to Pfaffians. So geometry helps.

For APPROXIMATION, three problems are distinct and must not be confused:
  (a) counting all matchings, of every size;
  (b) counting bipartite perfect matchings (the permanent);
  (c) counting perfect matchings in general graphs.

  - Jerrum and Sinclair (1989) gave an FPRAS for (a), the total number of
    matchings summed over all sizes.
  - Their perfect-matching method for (c) worked only when the ratio of
    near-perfect matchings (two holes) to perfect matchings was bounded by
    a fixed polynomial. Without that, perfect matchings can carry too
    little mass. Approximating the total for (a) does NOT isolate the
    perfect-matching contribution with relative accuracy.
  - Jerrum, Sinclair, Vigoda (2004) removed the restriction for (b), the
    permanent of every nonnegative matrix. They weight the two-hole states
    by their unmatched pair and learn suitable weights while slowly
    changing edge activities.
  - The general-graph question (c) was posed by Jerrum and Sinclair in
    1989 and stayed open. Stefankovic, Vigoda, Wilmes (2018) showed that
    the natural bipartite-style walk ("JSV type": perfect-and-near-perfect
    states, Broder proposals, Metropolis acceptance, arbitrary hole-based
    weights) has an unavoidable obstruction on some graphs: either tiny
    stationary mass on perfect matchings or exponentially slow mixing.

Theorem 1.1 answers (c) affirmatively. The proof does not reuse the
holes-only state space. Instead it uses a product of perfect-matching
spaces on a larger colored graph, with a move that swaps alternating
cycles between copies. A new energy comparison is proved for that state
space and those moves. This is what escapes the Stefankovic-Vigoda-Wilmes
barrier.

Two recent developments in the bipartite world are worth knowing. Chen,
Vigoda, Yang (2026) and Chen, Guo, Vigoda, Yang (2026) improve the
permanent running time via restricted Poincare inequalities and coupled
flows. These are bipartite results. For nonbipartite graphs under a
density promise, Yi (2026) gives deterministic relative approximation of
hafnians when min degree is at least (1/2 + gamma)n and weights lie in
[theta,1], with polynomial exponent depending on the fixed gamma, theta.
Theorem 1.1 makes no density promise: it handles sparse graphs and even
sets of isolated vertices.

3. The proof at a glance: six layers.

The counting algorithm never counts matchings of G directly. It walks
through a chain of weighted graphs, from one we can count easily to our
target. At each step it estimates the ratio of two consecutive partition
functions from random samples, then multiplies the ratios. This is
"annealing with a sampler". Here is the chain; memorize this table.

  Object         Vertices   What changes and why
  --------------------------------------------------------------------
  input G        n          Target: the unweighted count Z(G).
  complete graph n          Start with all edges weight 1; then slowly
                            shrink nonedges by a factor b<1 per stage,
                            so nonedge matchings fade out.
  vertex scaling n          Multiply activity of edge {i,j} by s_i s_j.
                            This shifts mass between rows WITHOUT
                            changing the normalized matching law, and it
                            keeps every row balanced.
  real paths     N          Replace each edge by a weighted path of
                            helper vertices. Real perfect matchings stay
                            in bijection with the old ones, with a common
                            weight multiplier.
  colored bags   N          Add weak "clique" helper edges inside tree
                            bags. If balanced, these inflate the partition
                            function by at most a factor two.
  weight tiers   N          Clamp all activities to [c^-t, c^t] and climb
                            from t=0 (uniform, exactly countable) to the
                            bag law. A product of many independent copies
                            mixes fast.
  --------------------------------------------------------------------

Three separate ideas keep the drift under control:
  (i)   a hole bound (Part III) that limits how much weight extra edges
        can attach to unmatched dots;
  (ii)  a balanced vertex scale (rederived every stage from observed
        two-hole ratios) that keeps the drift exactly where the hole
        bound can see it;
  (iii) a replicated-tier sampler (Parts V-VII) whose energy inequality
        turns a two-copy comparison into a full spectral gap.

The last two layers are the novel ones. Everything before them is
classical weighted-matching machinery, made explicit with constants.

A note on rigor and constants. All constants below are chosen for clear
estimates, not efficiency. Every polynomial has an absolute degree, and
the final time bound holds on every random execution, not just typical
ones.

================================================================
PART II. TOOLS
================================================================

Notation cheat sheet used everywhere.
  n        number of vertices of the input, always even in the interesting
           case (otherwise return zero).
  wt_a(M)  product of activities a_e over edges e in matching M.
  Z_a      sum of wt_a(M) over perfect matchings.
  Z_a(-U)  same, but after deleting the hole set U (so matchings leave the
           vertices of U uncovered).
  g_a(U)   hole ratio Z_a(-U)/Z_a.
  pi_a(M)  probability wt_a(M)/Z_a.
  B_ij     bottleneck similarity between vertices i,j (Sec 6).
  Phi_B(U) pairing capacity of hole set U (Sec 6).

4. Weighted matchings and holes.

We allow parallel edges distinguished by color. A matching names the
actual colored edges it uses, so two edges on the same two vertices but
different colors are different objects.

Fix positive rational activities a = (a_e). Define
    wt_a(M)  = prod_{e in M} a_e,
    Z_a(-U)  = sum_{M a perfect matching of H-U} wt_a(M),
    Z_a      = Z_a(empty set).
Here H is the underlying colored graph, and H-U deletes the vertices in
U. The empty graph has exactly one perfect matching (the empty one), of
weight one. Whenever Z_a > 0 put
    g_a(U)   = Z_a(-U)/Z_a,
    pi_a(M)  = wt_a(M)/Z_a.
The set U is a set of "holes": vertices deliberately left unmatched. The
number g_a(U) is the probability that a random weighted matching leaves
U unmatched. For distinct vertices we abbreviate g_a({i,j}) = g_a(ij).

We construct these weighted colored graphs as intermediate spaces from
the simple input. They are stored explicitly: vertices, colored edges,
rational activities listed one by one. The main theorem keeps the finite
simple undirected input model; it does not claim anything for compressed
edge multiplicities or for loops.

Collapsing parallel activities into their sum keeps every Z, hence every
g, unchanged. Conditional on a collapsed edge being used, its original
color can be recovered with probability proportional to its share.

5. Two matchings at once: alternating paths, cycles, and the switching trick.

IN PLAIN WORDS: lay one matching on the graph and a second matching on top
of it in a different color. Where they overlap you see a doubled edge.
Follow the colors and you alternate. The picture breaks into simple
pieces: alternating paths with two loose ends, and alternating cycles.
Swapping the two colors on a whole piece never changes the product of the
two weights. That is the engine behind almost every proof below.

Formally, the union of two matchings (keeping common edges twice) has
maximum degree two. Its nontrivial connected components are alternating
paths and even alternating cycles. Swapping the layers along a component
preserves the product of their weights. In the union of two PERFECT
matchings, common identical edges can be dropped, leaving disjoint
alternating cycles; different colors on the same two vertices form a
2-cycle.

To avoid any ambiguity later we fix once and for all a total order on
vertices and, for colored graphs, on colors. Every "choose the least" or
"the first such" step is decided by these orders.

The single most used fact is the following injection.

LEMMA 5.1 (Switching is injective for a fixed endpoint).
Overlay a matching M that misses a vertex a with a perfect matching P (or
more generally a matching that misses a prescribed set). The alternating
path that starts at a has some far endpoint z. Label the output by that z.
Switching the path between layers produces two new matchings with the same
total weight; the map is injective once the endpoint z is fixed, because
in the output you can follow the path back from a and switch it the other
way.

Proof. A path in a max-degree-two graph is determined by one endpoint.
Switching along it moves edges between layers without changing the
multiset of edges, so the product of weights is unchanged. Given the
output layers and the label of the far endpoint, the path from a is
recovered uniquely, and un-switching returns the input. QED.

This injection is why, in the next sections, "sum over inputs" comparisons
are legitimate: an input is charged to at most one output for each fixed
endpoint or case label.

6. Bottleneck similarity and pairing capacity.

Two vertices are strongly connected if some route between them uses only
high-activity edges. We care about the WORST edge on the best route.

For a positive weighted connected graph define, for i != j,
    B_ij = max( 1 , max over simple paths P from i to j of
                        min over edges e in P of a_e ).
Simple paths suffice. Values are at least 1 by the floor. This B is a
symmetric "similarity" and satisfies the ultrametric-style inequality
    B_ij >= min(B_ik, B_kj):
concatenate a route i->k with a route k->j and erase loops; the worst
edge is at least the smaller of the two worst edges.

For a threshold t > 1, the classes where B_ij >= t are exactly the
connected components of the graph that keeps only edges of activity at
least t (singletons included). At t = 1 we take one class containing every
vertex, consistent with the floor.

For an even hole set U define its pairing capacity
    Phi_B(U) = max over perfect pairings P of U of prod_{ij in P} B_ij,
    Phi_B(empty) = 1.
This number is used only in proofs; the counting algorithm never
enumerates pairings or computes Phi_B(U) for a general U. Think of it as:
how strongly can I pair up the holes, in the bottleneck sense.

7. Tree bags.

IN PLAIN WORDS: we group the vertices into a hierarchy of "bags" laid out
as a rooted tree. Neighbouring bags overlap in a controlled way, and we
only add helper edges inside a bag. Because bags overlap along interfaces,
the helper graph has a tree-like backbone, which sums of counts can exploit.

DEFINITION 7.1 (Tree-bag graph).
A tree-bag graph consists of a finite rooted tree of labels together with
a finite even vertex set. A label l at level k has height h_l = 2^k. The
root is at level zero; a child label has level one more than its parent.
Each vertex belongs to either one label or two ADJACENT labels (parent and
child). The bag at l is the set of vertices that belong to l. For each
unordered pair of distinct vertices and each common label l, there is one
edge of color l; there are no other edges. For an integer D >= 1 the
reference activities obey
    h_l / D  <=  W_{xy,l}  <=  3 h_l .
We also consider activities obtained by clamping all W_e to one common
interval [a,b] inside (0, infinity).

Two consequences to keep in mind.
  - A vertex pair has at most two edge colors (one or two shared labels).
  - At any bag, its interface with the parent and its interfaces with
    different children are DISJOINT: a vertex in two such interfaces would
    belong to at least three labels, which the definition forbids. This is
    what makes the later dynamic program cheap.
Empty bags and repeated underlying vertex sets are allowed (the tree and
the vertex set are separate data).

Clamping is monotone and never increases the ratio of a larger activity to
a smaller one; nearby labels stay comparable after clamping (proved in
Part V). Clamping lets us slide all activities into one bounded range
without breaking the bag structure.

8. Markov chains: energy, Poincare, Metropolis, total variation.

A Markov chain on a finite set with transition matrix P and stationary law
mu is reversible if mu(x)P(x,y) = mu(y)P(y,x) for all x,y. For a reversible
kernel define the Dirichlet energy
    E_{mu,P}(f) = (1/2) sum_{x,y} mu(x) P(x,y) (f(x)-f(y))^2 .
The variance is Var_mu(f) = sum_x mu(x) f(x)^2 - (sum_x mu(x) f(x))^2.
A Poincare inequality
    Var_mu(f) <= (1/gamma) E_{mu,P}(f)   for all f
says the spectral gap is at least gamma. A larger gap means faster mixing.

Metropolis rule. Given a symmetric proposal Q and target mu, propose y
from x with probability Q(x,y) and accept with probability
min(1, mu(y)/mu(x)), otherwise stay at x. The resulting kernel is
reversible with stationary mu, and its off-diagonal capacity is
    mu(x) P(x,y) = Q(x,y) min( mu(x), mu(y) ) .
We treat an inapplicable proposal as a stay-put move.

Total variation distance between laws mu,rho on the same finite set is
    ||mu - rho||_TV = (1/2) sum_x |mu(x)-rho(x)| .
Two useful facts.
  - A deterministic map, or a common randomized postprocessing step,
    cannot increase total variation (triangle inequality).
  - Two finite laws at distance eta admit a coupling that disagrees with
    probability exactly eta: first match the shared mass
    min(mu(x),rho(x)) on each point, then couple what is left. Iterating
    this bounds the disagreement probability of an adaptive simulation by
    the sum of its one-step errors.

Closure used later: every state space below contains at least one perfect
matching (we test that first), so positive activities give full-support
probability laws and every chain is well defined.


================================================================
PART III. THE LOGICAL HOLE BOUND
================================================================

Goal of this part: prove that if edge activities are "balanced" (every row
maximum of the two-hole ratios lies in [1/4,1]), then for ANY even hole set
U the mass of matchings leaving U unmatched is small, measured against the
bottleneck pairing capacity. This is the theorem that later keeps the
helper edges of the enlarged graph from overwhelming the answer. It uses
only alternating paths and differentiation of a finite partition function.

9. Setting and the statement.

Let V have even size n >= 2. Give every edge of the complete logical graph
K_V a positive activity lambda_ij. Use the weighted notation of Part II,
abbreviating M(K_V - U) to M(-U). For any positive array a on this graph
define the row maxima
    r_i(a) = max_{j != i} g_a(ij).
Call a BALANCED if 1/4 <= r_i(a) <= 1 for every i.

Let B be the bottleneck similarity of lambda (Section 6) and Phi_B its
pairing capacity.

THEOREM 9.1 (Logical hole bound).
Let lambda be balanced, B its bottleneck similarity, and set
    D0 = 10^8 (n+1)^4 .
Then for every even set U contained in V,
    g_lambda(U) * Phi_B(U)  <=  2 * D0^(|U|/2) .        (9.1)

Why this shape? Balance only controls two holes directly. The theorem
extends that to every even hole set. The factor Phi_B charges each pair of
holes its best bottleneck, so holes that are only weakly connected to the
graph are charged less.

Strategy. Temporarily add to every pair {i,j} a small parallel edge of
activity t*B_ij/D0 for a sliding parameter t in [0,1]. Collapse parallel
edges to get activities mu_ij(t) = lambda_ij + t*B_ij/D0. We show:
  (a) the partition function grows by a factor below e^eta < 2 over
      t in [0,1] (inflation bound), and
  (b) at t=1, matching up the holes U along the best pairing Q and
      filling the rest with the added edges turns each lambda-matching of
      H-U into a matching of the enlarged graph.
Dividing (b) by (a) gives (9.1). Part (a) is where balance and the next two
lemmas are used.

10. The four-hole inequality.

IN PLAIN WORDS: take a matching that leaves four dots a,b,c,d free, and a
perfect matching. Overlay them. The alternating path from a has to end at
one of b,c,d. Switching that path converts the pair of matchings into a
new pair where a is matched to that endpoint and the other two of {b,c,d}
are left free. Three possible endpoints give three injections. Count them
and divide.

LEMMA 10.1 (Four-hole inequality).
For any positive activities mu and four distinct vertices a,b,c,d,
    g_mu(abcd) <= g_mu(ab)g_mu(cd) + g_mu(ac)g_mu(bd) + g_mu(ad)g_mu(bc).
Consequently, if every row maximum is at most 4, then
    g_mu(empty) = 1,   g_mu(U) <= 4 (|U|=2),   g_mu(U) <= 48 (|U|=4).   (10.1)

Proof. Overlay M in M(-{a,b,c,d}) and P in M(-empty). The alternating path
that starts at a ends at exactly one of b,c,d; call it the endpoint. Switch
that path between the layers. The two new layers miss {c,d} and {a,b}
respectively (if the endpoint is b), and the product of weights is
unchanged. This map is injective for a fixed endpoint b: in the output
overlay, follow the path from a and switch it back. The same argument
applies to endpoints c and d. Summing the three injective comparisons and
dividing by Z_mu^2 gives the inequality. The small-set bounds follow since
g_mu(ab) <= r_a <= 4 and then g_mu(abcd) <= 3*(4*4)=48. QED.

11. Relocating a hole along a strong path.

The previous lemma controls small hole sets with row maxima. We also need an
extra factor when two holes are joined by a STRONG path (all edges of high
activity). Normalizing by the row maximum at the moving hole makes the
successive relocation errors ADD instead of multiply along the path. This
is the technical trick that makes the whole hole bound work.

The lemma is stated with mu >= lambda so it keeps applying while we add
activities. Its bottleneck similarity is always the one defined from
lambda.

LEMMA 11.1 (Relocating a hole along a strong path).
Let B be defined from lambda, and suppose mu_e >= lambda_e for every edge
and
    1/8 <= r_i(mu) <= 4    for every vertex i.
If |U| is 2 or 4 and i,j in U are distinct, then
    g_mu(U) <= 10^5 n / B_ij .      (11.1)

Proof. Drop the subscript mu from g and r. Write B = B_ij. If B <= 8,
then 10^5 n/B >= 10^5 n/8 >> 48 >= g_mu(U), so (10.1) already gives the
claim. Suppose B > 8 and pick a simple path from i to j whose lambda-,
hence mu-, activities are all at least B.

One relocation step. Let a be the hole currently on the path, S the current
hole set, and z the next vertex. So a in S and mu_az >= B.

Case z in S: inserting edge az in a matching missing S is injective and
multiplies its weight by mu_az >= B. Hence
    g(S)/r_a <= g(S  {a,z}) / (B r_a) <= 32/B,       (11.2)
using |S| <= 4, (10.1), and r_a >= 1/8.

Case z not in S: choose p != z with g(zp) = r_z. Inserting az gives
g(az) <= 1/mu_az <= 1/B < 1/8, so p != a. Overlay M in M(-S) and P in
M(-{z,p}). The path from a starts with an edge of P, and its endpoint falls
into three exhaustive cases:
  (i)  it ends at z. Switching gives layers missing (S{a}) u {z} and
       {a,p}. Product weight unchanged.
  (ii) it ends at p (only possible if p not in S). Switching gives layers
       missing (S{a}) u {p} and {a,z}. Insert az into the second; product
       weight grows by mu_az >= B.
  (iii) it ends at x in S  {a,p}. Switching gives layers missing
       (S{a,x}) and {z,p,a,x}. Insert az into the second; remaining holes
       are {p,x}; product weight grows by at least B.
The cases are exhaustive because degree-one vertices of an overlay are
exactly the symmetric difference of the two hole sets; if p in S then p is
isolated and cannot be a path endpoint.

The edge az is in neither input layer (first misses a, second misses z),
and switching cannot create an edge absent from the overlay. Each map is
injective once its case and endpoint are fixed: for (i) switch the output
path from a; for (ii),(iii) first remove the inserted edge az from the
second output layer, then switch the path from a. Summing over inputs,
using g(ap) <= r_a, and dividing by Z_mu^2 r_z yields
    g(S) <= (r_a/r_z) g(S{a} u {z})
            + (1/(B r_z)) [ 1_{p not in S} g(S{a} u {p})
              + sum_{x in S{a,p}} g(S{a,x}) g(px) ].   (11.3)
The bracket is at most 96: for |S|=4 the first term is at most 48 and the
at most three product terms are each at most 16; for |S|=2 the bound is 8.
After division by r_a,
    g(S)/r_a <= g(S{a} u {z})/r_z + 6144/B .        (11.4)

Starting at a=i, apply (11.4) along the chosen path, keeping all holes in
U{i} fixed, and stop just before reaching the first of these fixed holes;
such a hole exists because the path ends at j. The path is simple, so there
are at most n-2 steps, then one application of (11.2). The normalization by
the current row maximum makes the leading terms telescope. Since r_i <= 4,
    g(U) <= 4( 6144(n-2) + 32 ) / B  <= 10^5 n / B .    QED.

12. Proof of the logical hole bound (Theorem 9.1).

Temporarily add a distinguished parallel edge of activity t*B_ij/D0 to every
pair {i,j}, 0 <= t <= 1. Collapse parallel edges, giving
    mu_ij(t) = lambda_ij + t*B_ij/D0.
Write Z(t) = Z_{mu(t)} and g_t = g_{mu(t)}; B stays fixed. All partition
functions involved are polynomials in t, and Z(t) > 0. Differentiating the
finite sums gives
    d/dt log Z(t) = (1/D0) sum_{i<j} B_ij g_t(ij),           (12.1)
    d/dt g_t(xy) = (1/D0) sum_{ {i,j} disjoint from {x,y} } B_ij g_t(xyij)
                   - g_t(xy) * d/dt log Z(t).                 (12.2)

On any initial interval where all row maxima lie in [1/8,4], Lemma 11.1
bounds every summand B_ij g_t(ij) and every summand B_ij g_t(xyij) by
10^5 n. Since
    C(n,2) * 10^5 n / D0  <  eta,   eta = 10^-3,
we obtain, throughout that interval,
    0 <= d/dt log Z(t) <= eta,
    -eta g_t(xy) <= d/dt g_t(xy) <= eta.
Integrating gives
    e^{-eta t} g_0(xy) <= g_t(xy) <= g_0(xy) + eta t.

For the lower row bound, fix a neighbor maximizing a row at time zero; the
moving row maximum is at least that one evolving entry. So balance at time
zero gives
    (1/4) e^{-eta} <= r_i(mu(t)) <= 1 + eta.
Both bounds lie strictly inside [1/8,4]. By continuity the row maxima
cannot first leave [1/8,4] before t=1: at any proposed first exit, the
displayed strict bounds still hold and persist on a neighborhood. Thus all
preceding estimates hold on [0,1], and
    Z(1)/Z(0) <= e^eta < 2.         (12.3)

Now choose a pairing Q of U attaining Phi_B(U). At t=1 keep the original
and added parallel edges distinguished. To each original matching in
M(-U), add the edges of Q using their ADDED copies, of activities
B_ij/D0. This is injective into perfect matchings at time 1, and it
multiplies every weight by Phi_B(U)/D0^(|U|/2). Hence
    Z_lambda(-U) * Phi_B(U)/D0^(|U|/2)  <=  Z(1).
Divide by Z(0) = Z_lambda and apply (12.3):
    g_lambda(U) * Phi_B(U) <= 2 * D0^(|U|/2) .
This is (9.1). QED.


================================================================
PART IV. THE ENLARGED GRAPH AND ITS CONTROLLED INFLATION
================================================================

Goal: build a larger "colored bag" graph whose perfect matchings are easy
to sample, that is at most twice as big as the real one, and that exposes
the two-hole ratios as simple observable events. Then apply Part III's hole
bound to prove the factor two.

Input to this part: the complete logical graph on an even n >= 2 vertices,
with positive rational activities lambda_uv <= 4^K for an integer K >= 0.
Write B for its bottleneck similarity and Phi_B for its pairing capacity.
We first subdivide every edge into a path with a carefully chosen profile.

13. Parameters.

Set
    H_u = max_{v != u} B_uv,        (each H_u <= 4^K)
    p   = 2K + 2,
    N   = n + 4p * C(n,2),          (vertex count of the enlarged graph)
    D0  = 10^8 (n+1)^4,             (Part III constant)
    D   = 100 * N^2 * D0.
The number 4p+1 is the number of edges in each subdivided path.

14. The path profile.

Orient each logical edge e = uv by the fixed vertex order (say u < v), and
replace it by a path with real edges
    x_0 = u, x_1, x_2, ..., x_{4p}, x_{4p+1} = v .
Internal vertices of different paths are distinct. Let t_j = t_j^e be the
activity of edge x_{j-1} x_j. For 1 <= r <= p set
    t_{2r-1} = t_{2r}  = max{ B_uv , H_u * 2^{-(r-1)} },
    t_{4p+3-2r} = t_{4p+2-2r} = max{ B_uv , H_v * 2^{-(r-1)} },
    t_{2p+1} = lambda_uv .
The first line fills the left leg toward u, the second the right leg toward
v, and the central edge carries the ORIGINAL activity. We call these the
REAL edges and write Z' and g' for their partition function and hole
ratios. Let B' be their bottleneck similarity.

IN PLAIN WORDS: the two halves of the path decay geometrically from the
terminals inward by factors of two, and the very middle edge is the real
lambda. Equal activity pairs on symmetric positions make the two "tilings"
of the path (even-indexed edges vs odd-indexed edges) differ by exactly lambda,
because everything else cancels in equal pairs. That is the engine of the
next lemma.

15. Subdivision lemma.

LEMMA 15.1 (Subdivision).
The real graph has N vertices. Its perfect matchings are in bijection with
logical perfect matchings, and
    Z'  =  C_0 * Z_lambda ,
    C_0 = prod_e prod_{1 <= j <= 4p+1, j even} t_j^e .        (15.1)
On a noncentral real edge, its effective strength B' equals its activity.
On the central edge of the path for uv, B' equals B_uv. Incident effective
strengths differ by a factor of at most two. For an internal vertex x_j,
its max incident effective strength is
    T_j = t_j      if 1 <= j <= 2p,
          t_{j+1}  if 2p+1 <= j <= 4p.
Its HOME terminal is u in the first case and v in the second. Every edge on
its leg to its home has activity at least T_j.

Proof.
(a) Bijection and weight multiplier. An intact path has exactly two tilings
of its internal vertices: the even-indexed edges (which touch neither
terminal) and the odd-indexed edges (which touch both). The ratio of the
latter weight to the former is lambda_uv, because the noncentral factors
cancel in equal adjacent pairs on both legs. A real perfect matching picks
the odd tiling precisely on the paths corresponding to a logical perfect
matching. This proves the bijection and (15.1).

(b) Effective strength on real edges. Every noncentral edge has an
equal-activity twin sharing an internal vertex of degree two. Any path
connecting its endpoints that avoids that edge must use the twin, so its
bottleneck cannot exceed the edge activity; the direct edge attains it.

(c) Effective strength of the central edge. The minimum activity on the
full path replacing e is lambda_e. Hence for every t > 1, real edges of
activity at least t connect two terminals exactly when logical edges of
activity at least t do (a simple terminal-to-terminal path must traverse
whole subdivided paths). In particular B' restricted to terminals equals B.
Let a = x_{2p} and b = x_{2p+1} be the ends of the central edge. The legs
from a,b to u,v have minimum activity B_uv. If B_uv > max{1, lambda_uv}, a
logical path attaining B_uv avoids uv, and its subdivision together with
these legs connects a to b with bottleneck B_uv. If
B_uv = max{1, lambda_uv}, the central edge and the floor at one give the
lower bound. For the upper bound, any alternative path from a to b must
first use an edge on a leg of activity B_uv. Thus B'_{ab} = B_uv.

(d) Factor-two and home terminal. Along each half, the displayed profile is
nonincreasing toward the center and successive values differ by at most two.
The effective strength at the center agrees with both innermost pairs. At a
terminal u, every first edge has activity H_u. These give all remaining
assertions. QED.

16. Building the tree bags.

For each integer 0 <= k <= floor(log2 max_u H_u), take one label for every
connected component of the enlarged graph whose retained edges have
effective strength at least 2^k (singleton components included). A label at
level k > 0 has as parent its containing component at level k-1; at level
zero there is one component, so the labels form a rooted tree. Components
recurring at different levels are still different labels.

Label each real edge xy by the component containing it at level
floor(log2 B'_xy). A vertex belongs precisely to the labels of its incident
real edges. Equal-level incident labels are identical, and the factor-two
bound in Lemma 15.1 implies there are at most two incident levels. If there
are two, they are consecutive and their labels are parent and child. Thus
the memberships satisfy the tree-bag definition (Part II, Def 7.1). A bag
is the set of vertices with that membership; it need not be the entire
threshold component defining its label.

Within every bag of label l and height h_l, add a virtual edge of activity
h_l/D for each vertex pair. Collapse any real edge of color l into the
virtual edge with the same endpoints and color, adding its activity. This
gives one colored edge per common label, of activity
    W_{xy,l} = h_l/D + ( t_xy  if xy is a real edge with label l,
                         0     otherwise ) .
Since a real edge assigned to l has t_xy <= B'_xy < 2 h_l, these weights
satisfy the bag weight range (Part II, Def 7.1):
    h_l/D <= W_{xy,l} <= 3 h_l .
Also 1/D <= W_{xy,l} <= 3 * 4^K.

Probe edges. For each logical edge e, split off activity 1/D from the
virtual activity on its central colored edge, and mark that portion as the
PROBE for e. This is possible because h_l >= 1. After sampling a colored
matching, independently reveal the portion of each used edge in proportion
to its real, probe, and remaining virtual activities; zero portions are
omitted.

(The gentle reader may picture one path: the two real tilings of the path
side by side, and a third row showing the sole probe on the central edge.
The probe forces the outer legs to use both terminals; their combined
weight equals the even-tiling baseline, so what remains is a logical
matching with u and v deleted.)

17. Marked matching laws.

Let Z_bag be the colored partition function of the enlarged graph, and put
    I = Z_bag / Z'   (the "inflation" of the auxiliary edges).

PROPOSITION 17.1 (Marked matching laws).
For arbitrary positive logical activities, sample from the bag matching law
and reveal edge portions as above. The all-real event has probability 1/I.
Conditional on this event, the decoded logical matching has law pi_lambda.
For each logical edge uv,
    Pr[ probe for uv used and all other edges real ]
        = g_lambda(uv) / (D * I) .        (17.1)
Conditional on this event, the residual logical matching has the
lambda-weighted perfect-matching law on the vertices other than u,v.

Proof.
Revealing portions recovers the weighted law before real and virtual
activities were collapsed. The all-real matchings have total weight
Z' = C_0 Z_lambda (Lemma 15.1), and their normalized law is transported to
pi_lambda.

Now require the probe on the path for uv and real edges elsewhere. The
probe matches x_{2p} to x_{2p+1}. The remaining vertices on its path have
unique real tilings: the left one uses u, and the right one uses v. Equal
adjacent activities in the path profile show their combined weight equals
the even-edge baseline. Other paths incident to u or v must use their even
tilings. The remaining paths keep their even/odd alternatives, with ratio
lambda_e. So these marked matchings are in bijection with logical matchings
missing u,v, and every weight is multiplied by C_0/D. Their total mass is
C_0 Z_lambda(-uv)/D. Dividing by Z_bag = I C_0 Z_lambda proves (17.1); the
same constant multiplier proves the conditional-law assertion. QED.

18. Bounded inflation.

PROPOSITION 18.1 (Bounded inflation).
If the logical activities are balanced (Part III), then
    1 <= I <= 2 .        (18.1)

The rest of this part proves this. A set of virtual edges leaves a set U of
endpoints that must be removed from a real matching; its added weight must
therefore be compared with g'(U). We transfer the logical hole bound to
these enlarged hole sets in two steps: exact accounting for deleted internal
vertices, then charging the pairing capacity.

19. Exact accounting for deleted vertices.

A path is BROKEN if at least one of its internal vertices is deleted. Let
E_br(U) be the set of logical edges whose paths are broken by a hole set U.

Consider the ordered internal holes on a broken path. If a real completion
exists, successive hole indices alternate in parity, because the run
between them must have even order. A first hole of EVEN index forces the
prefix to match the terminal u; we say it CONSUMES u and map it to u. A
last hole of ODD index likewise consumes and maps to v. Remove all consumed
holes (zero, one, or two) from the list. The remaining holes split, in their
order, into pairs (j,k) with j odd and k even; call these NEUTRAL PAIRS.
There is no ambiguity when the list has one hole (it cannot be both even
and odd indexed).

Let R be the set of explicitly deleted terminals together with all consumed
terminals. Feasibility requires these terminals to be distinct. To each
consumed hole x_j assign the factor
    f_j = 1                         if it maps to its HOME terminal,
          lambda_e / T_j            if it maps to the OTHER terminal.
For a neutral pair (j,k) assign
    f_jk = T_j^{-1}                 if j,k <= 2p,
           T_k^{-1}                 if j,k >= 2p+1,
           lambda_e/(T_j T_k)       if j <= 2p < k.        (19.1)
Let F = F(U) be the product of all these factors on all broken paths; an
empty product is one. Both R and F depend only on U and the fixed path
profiles, not on a choice of completion.

For a set E_0 of logical edges, write Z_lambda(-R; E_0 forbidden) for the
partition function after deleting R and forbidding those edges.

LEMMA 19.1 (Deletion accounting).
If an even hole set U has a real completion, then R is even, |R| <= |U|, and
    Z'(-U) = C_0 F(U) Z_lambda(-R; E_br(U) forbidden) .     (19.2)
In particular g'(U) <= F(U) g_lambda(R).

Proof.
Fix a broken path. A prefix ending immediately before a first hole of index
j has j-1 internal vertices. If j is odd it uses the even tiling and leaves
u unused; if j is even it uses the odd tiling and consumes u. The suffix has
the analogous alternatives. Every run between consecutive holes has a unique
tiling, since its order is even. These parity conditions prove the
description of consumed holes and neutral pairs. On each path the number of
consumed holes has the same parity as the number of internal holes. Adding
explicit terminal holes gives |R| = |U| (mod 2). Distinctness and |R| <= |U|
follow from feasibility and from charging each consumed terminal to a
different hole.

For completeness all factors follow from one prefix ratio. For even j define
    P(j) = prod_{i <= j, i odd} t_i / prod_{i <= j, i even} t_i,  P(0)=1.
The profile gives P(j) = 1 for j <= 2p and P(j) = lambda_e/T_j for
j >= 2p+2. This is the factor for a first even-indexed hole consuming u;
reflection gives the factor for a last odd-indexed hole consuming v. For a
neutral pair (j,k) its shifted tiling relative to the even baseline
contributes
    prod_{i=j+2,j+4,...,k-1} t_i / prod_{i=j+1,j+3,...,k} t_i
        = P(k) / (P(j-1) t_j) .
If j is odd on the left its denominator is T_j; if j is odd on the right its
denominator is lambda_e (also when j = 2p+1). Substitution proves (19.1).
Prefixes and suffixes that don't consume a terminal, and runs outside these
shifted intervals, keep their baseline even edges. Thus F accounts for all
changes on broken paths.

Every completion now determines a logical matching on the intact paths,
using exactly the terminals outside R. Conversely, take any logical perfect
matching on the vertices outside R that uses no broken edge. Give its
selected intact paths their odd tilings, the others their even tilings, and
fill all broken paths by the uniquely forced runs above. Distinctness
ensures no terminal is used twice or both used and deleted. This
reconstructs a real completion and is inverse to the first map, with weight
C_0 F times the logical weight, proving (19.2). Dropping the forbidden-edge
condition and dividing by (15.1) gives the inequality. QED.

20. Pairings in a hierarchy.

To transfer the logical bound we must compare Phi_{B'}(U) with Phi_B(R). The
next lemma writes both capacities as integrals over threshold classes.

LEMMA 20.1 (Pairings in a hierarchy).
Let B >= 1 be a symmetric similarity on a finite set X with
B_ij >= min(B_ik, B_kj). Let C_t be the equivalence classes of B >= t,
including singletons. For every even U contained in X,
    log Phi_B(U) = integral_1^inf sum_{C in C_t}
                     floor(|C cap U| / 2) dt/t .      (20.1)
There is one pairing attaining the maximum number of internal pairs in
every threshold class simultaneously.

Proof. For any pairing Q,
    log prod_{ij in Q} B_ij
        = integral_1^inf |{ij in Q : B_ij >= t}| dt/t .
Inside a threshold class C there are at most floor(|C cap U|/2) pairs of Q,
giving the upper bound.

The threshold classes form a laminar family: any two are disjoint or one
contains the other. Process its finitely many distinct classes from smaller
to larger, starting with singletons. In each class, pair its currently
unpaired elements of U arbitrarily until at most one remains. When class C
has been processed, exactly floor(|C cap U|/2) pairs lie inside it.
Subsequent processing cannot create another pair inside C, because at most
one element of C cap U remains unpaired. The final class is X, and |U| is
even, so the procedure produces a pairing; it attains every floor count, and
therefore attains the integral upper bound. QED.

21. Lifting the hole bound.

LEMMA 21.1 (Lifted hole bound).
If the logical activities are balanced, then for every even set U of
enlarged vertices,
    g'(U) Phi_{B'}(U) <= 2 D0^(|U|/2) .
More precisely, for every U with a real completion, the deterministic
quantities satisfy
    F(U) Phi_{B'}(U) <= Phi_B(R) .        (21.1)

Proof. The assertion is immediate if U has no completion. For a threshold
t > 1 and any set A of enlarged vertices let
    S'_t(A) = sum_C floor(|A cap C| / 2),
where C ranges over the threshold classes of B'. Define S_t on logical
vertices similarly using B. These make sense even for odd |A|. Threshold
partitions stay those of the undeleted graphs; only the holes change.

By Lemma 15.1, an internal vertex x_j is isolated in the threshold partition
precisely when T_j < t; otherwise it is connected to its home terminal. Call
it ACTIVE in the latter case. The threshold relation induced on terminals is
exactly the logical relation.

Remove the neutral pairs one at a time from the current hole set. For a
same-side pair, its active vertices lie in one class. Removing it reduces
S'_t by at most
    1_{max(T_j,T_k) >= t} .
Indeed two active holes are removed from one class, one active hole reduces
a class floor by at most one, and an inactive hole is in a singleton class
and contributes zero. On the left max(T_j,T_k) = T_j; on the right it is
T_k. For a cross pair the loss is at most
    1_{T_j >= t} + 1_{T_k >= t} - 1_{lambda_e >= t} .
The subtracted term: when lambda_e >= t both vertices are active and
connected by the full path, so their removal reduces a single class floor by
one; otherwise the bound is the sum of the separate active-hole bounds. These
arguments apply to the current set after any preceding removals.

Next consider each consumed hole mapping AWAY from home. If it is active and
lambda_e < t, discard it. The floor loss is at most
    1_{T_j >= t} - 1_{lambda_e >= t} ,
which is nonnegative since T_j >= B_e >= lambda_e. Every remaining active
consumed hole can be mapped to its consumed terminal within its threshold
class: a home mapping always has this property, and an away mapping retained
by the rule has lambda_e >= t. Together with explicitly deleted terminals
these maps are injective, by the distinctness in Lemma 19.1. Inactive
internal holes lie in singleton classes and contribute no floor. Hence the
floor sum remaining after removals and discards is at most S_t(R).

Integrate these bounds against dt/t on (1,inf). By Lemma 20.1 the integral
of S'_t(U) is log Phi_{B'}(U) and that of S_t(R) is log Phi_B(R). For a
same-side neutral pair the integrated charge is exactly -log f_jk. For a
cross pair it is
    log T_j + log T_k - log max{1, lambda_e} <= -log f_jk .
For an away-mapped hole it is
    log T_j - log max{1, lambda_e} <= -log f_j .
Home mappings cost zero. Summing proves
    log Phi_{B'}(U) <= log Phi_B(R) - log F ,
which is (21.1). Finally Lemma 19.1 and Theorem 9.1 give
    g'(U) Phi_{B'}(U) <= g_lambda(R) Phi_B(R)
        <= 2 D0^(|R|/2) <= 2 D0^(|U|/2) .  QED.

22. Proof of bounded inflation (Proposition 18.1).

Expand the partition function before collapsing real and virtual portions. A
nonempty partial matching Q of a colored virtual edges, with endpoint set U,
contributes relative to Z' the mass
    g'(U) prod_{xy,l in Q} h_l / D .
Vertices sharing l lie in the same threshold component at h_l, so
h_l <= B'_{xy}. Their pairing therefore gives
    g'(U) prod_{xy,l in Q} h_l/D
        <= D^{-a} g'(U) Phi_{B'}(U)
        <= 2 (D0/D)^a
by Lemma 21.1. A vertex pair has at most two common labels, so there are
fewer than N^2 colored virtual edges and at most N^{2a} choices for Q. The
empty Q contributes one. Consequently
    1 <= I <= 1 + 2 sum_{a >= 1} (N^2 D0 / D)^a
            = 1 + 2/(99)  <  2 ,
since D = 100 N^2 D0. QED.


================================================================
PART V. A QUADRANGULATION ADAPTED TO THE LABELS
================================================================

The sampler will compare two perfect matchings that differ on a cycle. To
do that locally we need to cut the cycle's polygon into quadrilateral
"cells", each side standing for an arc of the cycle. The cells must respect
the labels of the bag graph: neighbouring sides must be able to close with a
chord of a shared color. This part builds such a quadrangulation and proves
the activity comparability used later. No balance assumption is needed here.

23. Odd arcs and their two matchings.

Let C be a simple even cycle of length m >= 4 in the colored bag graph. Each
edge keeps its color, a node of the label tree. For a vertex v of C define
    S_v = the set of labels of the two edges of C incident to v.
So S_v is a singleton or two adjacent tree nodes. These sets always refer to
the ORIGINAL full cycle; adding a diagonal never changes them. The cyclic
sequence of edge labels is a walk on the tree, with repetitions allowed.

An ARC is one of the two paths in C between two distinct vertices, specified
with its endpoints. Its length is its number of edges. We only use ODD arcs.
An arc is ADMISSIBLE if its endpoints u,v satisfy S_u cap S_v nonempty; then
the clique property supplies an edge joining them with any chosen label from
this intersection.

For an admissible odd arc J = (v_0,...,v_s), choose a colored endpoint chord
e_J with a label in S_{v_0} cap S_{v_s}, and set
    T_J = { v_0v_1, v_2v_3, ..., v_{s-1}v_s },        (through pattern)
    N_J = { v_1v_2, v_3v_4, ..., v_{s-2}v_{s-1} },    (interior pattern)
    C_J = N_J union { e_J } .                          (closed pattern)
All path edges keep their cycle colors. T_J and C_J both PERFECTLY match the
vertices of J; N_J leaves only its endpoints unmatched. Call J SHORT if s=1
and LONG otherwise. On a short arc choose its actual cycle edge as e_J, so
T_J = C_J. When a diagonal represents two complementary arcs, use the same
colored chord in both patterns.

A good long arc. Besides closing an arc with a chord, we sometimes need to
free its endpoints while starting from its through pattern. Removing the
first and last edges of a long arc from T_J uncovers its endpoints and its
two LEAVES v_1, v_{s-1}. Call J GOOD if it is short, or if
    S_{v_1} cap S_{v_{s-1}} nonempty.
For a good long arc choose an edge between its leaves with a label in this
intersection, a LEAF REPAIR. After removing the two boundary edges, inserting
this repair matches all internal vertices and leaves both endpoints free.
For a short arc, deleting its sole edge does the same. The BOUNDARY EDGES of
an arc mean only its first and last edges (which coincide for a short arc).

24. The quadrangulation lemma.

Realize the vertices of C in cyclic order as a convex polygon. A
QUADRANGULATION subdivides this polygon into quadrilaterals by noncrossing
diagonals. A SIDE of a cell represents the arc of the original cycle from one
corner to the next corner, containing no other corner of that cell. The four
sides of each cell represent four consecutive arcs partitioning the whole
cycle. An interior diagonal has two occurrences as a cell side, and their
arcs are complementary in the original cycle.

LEMMA 24.1 (Label-adapted quadrangulation).
The polygon of C has a quadrangulation such that every cell side represents an
admissible odd arc and the following hold in every cell:
  (i)  some opposite pair of sides consists of good arcs;
  (ii) if both arcs in an opposite pair are long, both arcs in the other
       opposite pair are good.
There are (m-2)/2 cells and (m-4)/2 interior diagonals. The construction runs
in time polynomial in m and the encoding length of the label tree.
Each diagonal may be assigned any common label from the fixed sets at its
endpoints, used consistently in its two occurrences. For a short side, use the
color of its actual cycle edge. These choices need not match the auxiliary
labels used in the construction.

Proof. Recursive splitting rule. A SUBPROBLEM is an admissible odd arc
J = (v_0,...,v_s); its EXTERIOR is its complementary arc in the full cycle.
Choose a label P in S_{v_0} cap S_{v_s}, and write a_i for the label of
v_i v_{i+1}, 0 <= i < s. Adjacent terms of this sequence are equal or
adjacent in the tree. If s=1 no cell is needed. For s >= 3 find indices
    0 < p1 < p2 < s,   p1 odd,   p2 even,           (24.1)
such that the three subarcs with successive endpoint pairs
    (v_0, v_p1), (v_p1, v_p2), (v_p2, v_s)
are admissible. They and the exterior arc form the four sides of the new
cell: first, middle, third, exterior.

Elementary tree fact. If a contiguous part of the label walk avoids P and its
first and last labels are adjacent to P, these two labels coincide: the walk
lies in a single component of the tree with P removed, and that component has
just one node adjacent to P.

Case A: the exterior is good. It suffices to make either the middle side
short or both the first and third sides short; either immediately gives (i)
and (ii) because the exterior is good.
First ensure some a_i equals P. If none does, a_0 and a_{s-1} are adjacent to
P (the endpoint sets contain P), and the tree fact gives a_0 = a_{s-1} = Q
for a neighbor Q of P. Replace P by Q, which also lies in both endpoint sets.
Now split into:
 (a) a_i = P for an odd i. Set p1=i, p2=i+1. Middle side short. First and
     third sides' endpoints contain P. All admissible.
 (b) otherwise all P's have even indices. If first index i>0, set p1=i-1,
     p2=i. Prefix a_0..a_{i-1} avoids P and its end labels are adjacent to P,
     so they equal one neighbor Q. First side's endpoints contain Q, third
     side's contain P, middle side short.
 (c) first occurrence at 0, last at j < s-1. Set p1=j+1, p2=j+2. Suffix
     a_{j+1}..a_{s-1} avoids P, its end labels adjacent to P, so equal Q.
     First side's endpoints contain P, third's contain Q, middle short.
 (d) a_0 = a_{s-1} = P. Set p1=1, p2=s-1. Both outer sides short, middle
     side's endpoints contain P.
All indices satisfy (24.1) (e.g. in (b) the even i >= 2; in (c) the even j <=
s-3).

Case B: the exterior is not good (hence long). At least one of its leaves
lacks P in its fixed set; reverse the indexing of J so this leaf is the
exterior neighbor of v_0. The exterior edge at v_0 cannot have label P; since
P in S_{v_0}, the first edge of J has label a_0 = P.
If also a_{s-1} = P, choose p1=1, p2=s-1; both outer sides short, middle
admissible. Otherwise let t-1 be the last index with a_{t-1}=P, so
1 <= t <= s-1. The suffix a_t..a_{s-1} avoids P; its first label is adjacent
to P, and its last is adjacent to P because P in S_{v_s}; hence
a_t = a_{s-1} = Q for the same neighbor Q of P.
 - t even: set p1=1, p2=t. First side short; middle endpoints contain P;
   third endpoints contain Q; third side good because if long both boundary
   edges have label Q, so both leaves contain Q.
 - t odd: set p1=t, p2=s-1. Third side short; first endpoints contain P;
   middle endpoints contain Q; first side good because if long both boundary
   edges have label P.
The strict inequalities follow from t >= 2 (even) and t <= s-2 (odd). In
either case first and third sides are good and at least one is short, giving
(i); the only opposite pair that can contain two long sides is middle vs
exterior, and its other pair is good, giving (ii).

Recursion and consistent diagonals. Start with any arc of length m-1, whose
exterior is the remaining single cycle edge (endpoints share that edge's
label). Apply the rule and recurse on each subarc of length > 1, always using
the original sets S_v and the complement in the original cycle as exterior.
The three subpolygons have disjoint interiors, so all diagonals are
noncrossing. Each child has odd length and admissible endpoints. The splitting
label P is only an auxiliary witness; it does not constrain the color of the
closing diagonal, so each diagonal can be colored once and used on both sides.

Count. For an odd arc of length s the result has (s-1)/2 cells: zero for s=1,
and a split into odd lengths s_1+s_2+s_3=s gives
1 + sum_i (s_i-1)/2 = (s-1)/2. The root gives (m-2)/2 cells. Each nonroot
subproblem that creates a cell has one closing diagonal, so one fewer diagonal
than cells. A direct implementation scans at most m labels in each of the
(m-2)/2 nontrivial subproblems; the recursion also has m-1 short leaves.
Fixing total orders makes the construction deterministic, with O(m^2) label
comparisons and polynomial bit cost. QED.

For each cell choose its colored side chords as in the lemma; for each good
long side choose one leaf repair. These are exactly the extra edges the local
two-edge switches in Part VI need.

25. Comparability within a cell.

LEMMA 25.1 (Comparability within a cell).
Consider the four colored side chords of a cell, the first and last cycle edges
of each of its four arcs, and one leaf repair for each good long arc. The
labels of any two edges in this collection have tree distance at most 6.
Consequently their activities lie within a factor
    A_0 = 1000 D
of one another. The same holds after clamping every edge activity to any
common interval [a,b] in (0, infinity).

Proof. Two consecutive side chords have labels in the same fixed set S_v at
their common cell corner, so their labels are equal or adjacent. Any two of
the four side-chord labels are therefore at distance at most 2. A boundary
edge of an arc and its side chord have labels in the same S_v at the relevant
endpoint, distance at most 1. A leaf repair and the boundary edge at either
of its leaves have labels in that leaf's fixed set, again distance at most 1.
Thus each edge is at distance at most 2 from its side chord, and the diameter
is at most 2+2+2 = 6.

Adjacent labels have heights differing by a factor 2. If edges e,f have labels
ell,ell' at distance at most 6, the assumed activity bounds give
    W_e / W_f  <=  3 h_ell / (h_{ell'}/D)  <=  3D 2^6 = 192 D  <=  A_0 .
The same bound holds with e,f exchanged.

Clamping. The clamp psi(x) = max{a, min{b,x}} satisfies
    1 <= psi(x)/psi(y) <= x/y    for x >= y > 0,
because log psi(e^u) is the clamp of u to [log a, log b], a nondecreasing
1-Lipschitz function. Clamping therefore keeps every factor bound. QED.


================================================================
PART VI. THE TWO-COORDINATE ENERGY INEQUALITY
================================================================

This is the technical heart. The sampler runs on a product of many copies
of a bag-matching law. To prove it mixes fast we need a Poincare inequality
for a pair of copies, driven by simple local moves. This part proves that a
variance of one coordinate is controlled by the Dirichlet energy of the SUM
of two coordinates, with an explicit constant L. Part VII turns this
two-copy control into a full spectral gap on the product.

Throughout, fix a tree-bag graph on N >= 2 vertices with at least one
perfect matching, and let w be either its reference activities or any common
clamp of them. Write pi(M) = wt_w(M)/Z_w, and call M the set of its colored
perfect matchings. Put
    A_0 = 1000 D,    Tcal = (10N)^30,    L = (10^4 N D)^100 .    (26.1)

26. The pair kernel.

On a pair (A,B) in M x M choose each of the following proposal types with
probability 1/3.

 (1) Two-edge switch. Choose an unordered pair of distinct edges of A
     uniformly, then either alternative pairing of their four endpoints with
     probability 1/2. For each proposed edge, choose index 1 or 2 with
     probability 1/2 from the ordered list of common labels of its endpoints.
     If an index is absent, stay put. If A has fewer than two edges, stay put.

 (2) Color change. Choose an edge of A uniformly and choose index 1 or 2
     from the common-label list with probability 1/2. An absent index gives a
     holding move.

 (3) Cycle swap. Choose uniformly one discrepancy cycle of A,B and
     interchange its two layers. If there is no discrepancy cycle, stay put.

Accept each proposal by the Metropolis rule for pi (x) pi (the product law on
the pair). Denote the resulting kernel by P and its Dirichlet energy by E.
The proposal is symmetric: for (1),(2) the reverse choice has the same edge,
pairing, and color-index probabilities; the holding choices do not change
this. For (3) the discrepancy cycles and their number are unchanged by the
swap, so a cycle swap has acceptance probability one (product weight is
unchanged).

THEOREM 26.1 (Pair energy inequality).
For every two real functions f1,f2 on M,
    Var_pi f1  <=  L * E( (A,B) |-> f1(A) + f2(B) ) .        (26.2)
The same constant L works for every common-interval clamp of the reference
activities.

In the proof write F(A,B) = f1(A)+f2(B) and E = E(F). A two-edge switch is
SAFE if its four removed or inserted colored edges have activities within a
factor A_0 of one another. Let S(A) be its possible safe destinations, and
K(A) its possible destinations by changing one edge color. Consecutive labels
have comparable heights, so every color change has activity ratio in
[A_0^{-1}, A_0] (Lemma 25.1).

27. Elementary energy bounds.

LEMMA 27.1 (Elementary energy bounds).
With the notation above,
    sum_A pi(A) sum_{A' in S(A)} (f1(A)-f1(A'))^2
        <= 60 N^2 A_0^2 E ,                       (27.1)
    sum_A pi(A) sum_{A' in K(A)} (f1(A)-f1(A'))^2
        <= 12 N A_0 E ,                           (27.2)
    sum_{A,B} pi(A)pi(B) sum_{C in C(A,B)}
        (F(A,B) - F((A,B)^C))^2
        <= 6 N E .                                (27.3)
Here C(A,B) denotes the discrepancy cycles, and (A,B)^C is the pair after
swapping C.

Proof. Every specified legal two-edge switch has proposal probability at
least 1/(30N^2); its Metropolis acceptance is at least A_0^{-2} when safe.
Every specified color change has proposal probability at least 1/(6N) and
acceptance at least A_0^{-1}. These moves change only the f1 summand of F, so
summing the second-coordinate law and using the factor 1/2 in the energy
gives (27.1),(27.2). There are at most N discrepancy cycles; each specified
swap has proposal probability at least 1/(3N) and is accepted, giving (27.3).
QED.

28. An exact identity on a cycle.

Fix a pair of demand matchings (I1,I2) and a simple discrepancy cycle C of
length at least four. All orientations and choices below are deterministic,
using the fixed orders. Let R be the edges of I1 outside C, and let O0 = I1
and O1 be the matching obtained by toggling C in I1. Both orientations use R
outside C. Put
    D_C = f1(O0) - f1(O1) .

Apply the quadrangulation (Part V) and fix the colors of its diagonals
consistently. A cell's four sides always mean the four odd arcs of the
ORIGINAL whole cycle, not paths formed from earlier diagonals.

A side J is THROUGH in O_eps if T_J is contained in O_eps. Exactly one
orientation O_eps contains T_J; define
    H_J = (O_eps  T_J) union C_J ,
    g_J = f1(O_eps) - f1(H_J) .                    (28.1)
For a short side, the chord is its actual cycle edge, so g_J = 0.

Why the original cycle on every cell side. Take a six-cycle
C = (v0,v1,...,v5,v0) and
    O0 = R u { v0v1, v2v3, v4v5 },
    O1 = R u { v1v2, v3v4, v5v0 },
with an admissible diagonal e = v0v3. In the two cells it represents the
complementary arcs J = (v3,v4,v5,v0) and Jbar = (v0,v1,v2,v3). Both
conversions give the SAME matching
    H_J = H_Jbar = H = R u { v1v2, e, v4v5 } .
Thus g_Jbar - g_J = f1(O0) - f1(O1) = D_C: the shared diagonal contributes
one whole-cycle difference, not zero. This isolates the complementary-gain
identity. Those cells have only one long side each, so the context errors
below vanish; cells with two opposite long sides need a further estimate.

In a cell, let P be its opposite pair of sides through in O0, and Q the pair
through in O1. Let A_P use N_J on every side and the chords of the two sides
of P, together with R. Define A_Q analogously and put
    Delta = f1(A_P) - f1(A_Q) .
These are legal perfect matchings: the interior vertices are matched by the
N_J's, and the two chosen chords match the four corners. Their difference is
a SAFE two-edge switch by Lemma 25.1.

Order each opposite pair as (Y,X). For the orientation in which both are
through, first convert Y and then X. Write A^(0) = O_eps and A^(1) = H_Y for
the two pre-conversion contexts of X. For a matching R' on the vertices
outside X define
    d_X f(R') = f(T_X union R') - f(C_X union R') .        (28.2)
The context error of the ordered opposite pair is
    E_{Y,X} = d_X f1(A^(1)  T_X) - d_X f1(A^(0)  T_X) .   (28.3)
It is zero if either side is short. Denote these errors by E_P,E_Q for the
two ordered pairs of the cell.

LEMMA 28.1 (Cell identity and cancellation).
The quantities just defined satisfy
    D_C = Delta + sum_{J in P} g_J - sum_{J in Q} g_J + E_P - E_Q     (28.4)
for each cell. Consequently,
    D_C = sum_{cells of C} (Delta + E_P - E_Q) .                       (28.5)

Proof. The first conversion in an opposite pair has its canonical gain. The
second has its canonical gain plus the error (28.3). So
    f1(O0) - f1(A_P) = sum_{J in P} g_J + E_P ,
    f1(O1) - f1(A_Q) = sum_{J in Q} g_J + E_Q .
Subtracting proves (28.4).

Consider an interior diagonal with complementary arcs J,Jbar. They are
through in opposite whole-cycle orientations, and their converted matchings
coincide:
    H_J = H_Jbar = R union N_J union N_Jbar union { e_J } .
So the two signed canonical gains contributed by this diagonal sum to
f1(O0)-f1(O1) = D_C. The signs are exactly those in (28.4): the arc through
in O0 has positive sign, the other negative. Boundary sides have zero gain.
Summing (28.4) over the cells therefore gives one copy of D_C per cell on the
left and one per interior diagonal on the right, plus the local terms. The
number of cells is one more than the number of diagonals, proving (28.5).
QED.

29. Encoding the demands.

A SWITCH DEMAND specifies (I1,I2), a simple discrepancy cycle C, and one cell
of its fixed quadrangulation. Its value is Delta above. An ERROR DEMAND
specifies these data and one opposite pair with two long sides, in its fixed
order (Y,X). Its value is E_{Y,X}. The weight of either demand is
    rho(d) = pi(I1) pi(I2) .
Repeated demands from different cells or different cycles are counted
separately in all sums.

LEMMA 29.1 (Encodings with a bounded number of preimages).
Every switch demand has an encoding (A_P,B), and every error demand has
encodings (A^(j),B^(j)) for j=0,1, with:
  (i)  for each encoding (A,B), rho(d) <= A_0^4 pi(A) pi(B);
  (ii) for each encoding type and each fixed output pair there are at most
       Tcal demand preimages, including the choices of cycle, cell, opposite
       pair, and context;
  (iii) for an error demand both guide matchings satisfy C_X is contained in
       B^(j).

Proof. Repair operation. Start with the union of the four through patterns
T_J in a cell. Each corner is covered twice, once by each incident side.
Select a good opposite pair of sides. For each selected short side delete its
sole edge. For each selected long side delete its first and last edges and
insert its chosen leaf repair. Each corner loses exactly one incident edge.
On a long selected side the only newly uncovered internal vertices are its
two leaves, which the repair matches. So the resulting edges form a perfect
matching of all cycle vertices. This is legal also for a side of length
three: its two leaves are distinct and its repair is an ordinary colored edge
between them.

Switch demands. Use A_P as the first output. In the second output retain the
edges of I2 outside C, start with all four through patterns, and apply the
repair to a good opposite pair (exists by quadrangulation (i)). Call the
result B. Before these modifications the original multiset union contains
exactly one T_J and one N_J on each side. The output union adds the two
P-chords and the leaf repairs and drops the selected boundary edges. If
r in {0,1,2} selected sides are long, the numbers added and dropped are both
2+r <= 4.

Error demands, context zero. First output is A^(0) = O_eps, the canonical
orientation in which X,Y are through. Start the second output with the
complementary cycle orientation and the edges of I2 outside C. It has the N
patterns on X,Y. Insert their two chords. The other opposite pair is good by
quadrangulation (ii), because X,Y are both long. On that other pair delete
boundary edges and insert leaf repairs as above. The result B^(0) is a
perfect matching containing both C_X and C_Y. Again the multiset union differs
from the original by equally many added and dropped edges, at most four each.

Error demands, context one. Interchange the patterns T_Y and C_Y between
A^(0),B^(0). Each pattern perfectly matches the vertex set of Y, so the
interchange is legal and gives A^(1)=H_Y and a guide B^(1). The arc sets X,Y
are disjoint, so B^(1) still contains C_X. The multiset union and product
weight are unchanged.

In every case all added and dropped edges are among the chords, boundary
edges, and good-side leaf repairs of this one cell. They have pairwise
activity ratio at most A_0 by Lemma 25.1. Since the two lists have equal
length at most four, the input product weight is at most A_0^4 times the
output product weight. Division by Z_w^2 proves (i).

Inverse. Attach a tag: the four cell corners in cyclic order, lists of the
added and dropped colored edges padded to length four each, one orientation
bit, and fixed flags for the encoding type, opposite pair, and order. An edge
in these lists is an occurrence in the multiset union; coincident colored
edges keep their multiplicities. Given the output pair and tag, remove the
added occurrences and reinsert the dropped occurrences in its multiset union.
This recovers the original colored union I1 (u) I2. Its component containing
the first tagged corner is the active simple cycle C. The orientation bit
says which of the two alternating classes at that corner belongs to I1, so
alternation determines both original layers on all of C. Outside C the output
layers are unchanged. The fixed quadrangulation can now be reconstructed; the
tagged corners identify the cell, and the flags identify the term and its
ordered pair. This is an inverse on every produced tag.

Tag count. There are at most 2N^2 possible colored edges. Allowing a padding
symbol, the tags have at most
    128 N^4 (1+2N^2)^8 <= 128*3^8 N^20 < (10N)^30 = Tcal
possibilities for N >= 2 (factor 128 covers the orientation bit and flags;
lists can be stored in the fixed colored-edge order). This proves (ii), and
the constructions proved (iii). QED.

First application to switches. For a fixed encoded pair (A,B) there are at
most Tcal demands, each with destination A_Q in S(A). Therefore
    sum_{d switch} rho(d) Delta_d^2
        <= A_0^4 Tcal sum_{A,B} pi(A)pi(B)
             sum_{A' in S(A)} (f1(A)-f1(A'))^2
        <= 60 N^2 A_0^6 Tcal E .               (29.1)

30. Controlling context errors by cycle swaps.

Here an IDENTITY X means a directed, colored, simple odd path of length at
least three together with a specified colored edge joining its endpoints. It
determines T_X, C_X, and (28.2). We may sum over all such identities: this is
a finite proof device, not something the algorithm enumerates. Put
    p_X = pi{ B : C_X is contained in B } .
We omit identities with p_X = 0. Every identity from an error demand has
p_X > 0, by the guide of Lemma 29.1. For any first matching A containing T_X
and either j=0,1, that lemma gives the CONDITIONAL DEMAND-MASS BOUND
    sum_{d error : X(d)=X, A^(j)(d)=A} rho(d)
        <= A_0^4 Tcal pi(A) p_X .              (30.1)
Indeed, for each guide B containing C_X there are at most Tcal preimages of
(A,B); sum its product weight over these guides. The factor p_X is essential
and cancels the conditioning below.

For a fresh guide B ~ pi define
    mu_X = E[ d_X f2(B  C_X) | C_X is contained in B ] .
For an error demand (a-b)^2 <= 2(a-c)^2 + 2(b-c)^2 (with a,b the two context
derivatives and c = mu_X) and (30.1), then Jensen to the conditional
expectation defining mu_X, give
    sum_{d error} rho(d) E_d^2
        <= 4 A_0^4 Tcal sum_X sum_{A contains T_X} pi(A) p_X
             ( d_X f1(AT_X) - mu_X )^2
        <= 4 A_0^4 Tcal Sigma_swap ,          (30.2)
where
    Sigma_swap = sum_X sum_{A contains T_X, B contains C_X} pi(A)pi(B)
        ( d_X f1(AT_X) - d_X f2(BC_X) )^2 .  (30.3)
In detail, for fixed X,A Jensen bounds the squared difference from mu_X by
    (1/p_X) sum_{B contains C_X} pi(B)
        ( d_X f1(AT_X) - d_X f2(BC_X) )^2 .
Multiplication by the p_X in (30.1) removes this denominator exactly; no
lower bound on p_X is used.

LEMMA 30.1 (Swap sum).
The sum in (30.3) satisfies Sigma_swap <= 12 N^2 E.

Proof. For a pair in (30.3), T_X and C_X cover exactly the same vertex set,
and their union is one alternating cycle: the path X together with its
closing chord. Their edges are disjoint since X is long and simple. So this is
an entire discrepancy component of A,B, not a portion of a larger one.
Swapping it changes F by exactly d_X f1(AT_X) - d_X f2(BC_X). For a fixed
fresh pair (A,B) and one of its discrepancy cycles, an identity X is
determined by marking the closing chord in the second matching and choosing a
direction around that cycle (the rest of the path is read from the pair).
There are at most 2N such choices. Consequently
    Sigma_swap <= 2N sum_{A,B} pi(A)pi(B)
        sum_{C in C(A,B)} (F(A,B)-F((A,B)^C))^2 <= 12 N^2 E
by (27.3). QED.

31. Completing the energy proof.

PROOF OF THEOREM 26.1.
Take independent I1,I2 with law pi. Toggle their discrepancy cycles in the
order of their least vertices, converting I1 into I2. Cauchy-Schwarz over the
at most N cycles gives
    (f1(I1)-f1(I2))^2 <= N sum_C (the f1 difference at the step for C)^2 .
For each selected C, toggle all earlier cycles in BOTH demand matchings. This
preserves their product weight, their union, and the selected cycle; it is its
own inverse. The term at that step becomes D_C^2 for the new demand pair.
Since Var_pi f1 = (1/2) E (f1(I1)-f1(I2))^2, we get the convenient slightly
weaker bound
    Var_pi f1 <= N sum_{I1,I2} pi(I1)pi(I2) sum_C D_C^2 .    (31.1)

A length-two discrepancy cycle is a color change in I1. For a fixed I1 and a
specified change, summing the mass of I2 that supplies the other color costs
at most one. Its total contribution to (31.1) is at most 12 N A_0 E, by
(27.2).

For a simple cycle there are fewer than N cells, so (28.5) and Cauchy-Schwarz
give
    D_C^2 <= 3N sum_{cells} (Delta^2 + E_P^2 + E_Q^2) .
Sum over all such cycle demands. Errors with a short side vanish; all others
are precisely the error demands already bounded. Using (29.1),(30.2) and
Lemma 30.1, the simple-cycle contribution to (31.1) is at most
    3N ( 60 N^2 A_0^6 Tcal + 48 N^2 A_0^4 Tcal ) E .
Combining the two cycle types in (31.1), and using A_0,Tcal >= 1,
    Var_pi f1 <= ( 12 N^2 A_0 + 324 N^4 A_0^6 Tcal ) E
              <= 1000 N^4 A_0^6 Tcal E .
Finally
    1000 N^4 A_0^6 Tcal = 10^51 N^34 D^6 <= (10^4 N D)^100 = L .
All comparisons used only Lemma 25.1 and the adjacent-label color comparison;
both hold under any common clamp, proving the final assertion. QED.


================================================================
PART VII. A SAMPLER FROM REPLICATED ADJACENT TIERS
================================================================

The two-copy inequality (Part VI) controls an additive function of a pair,
but it is not yet a Poincare inequality for ALL functions of that pair. Here
we turn it into a full spectral gap on a larger product space. The idea:
make many independent copies ("slots") at each of a ladder of adjacent weight
tiers. If a pair of slots exchanges alternating cycles, a slot can borrow
variation from its neighbour. Replicating many times per tier and averaging
over guides makes the leftover residual terms small enough to absorb. The
bottom tier has all activities equal to one, which we can count and sample
exactly by a tree dynamic program. Then we slide up the ladder to the tier
where no activity is clamped, i.e. the real bag law.

Throughout: the tree-bag graph has N >= 2 vertices, b_tree labels, maximum
level at most 2K, parameter D >= 1, positive rational reference activities,
and a common nonempty set M of colored perfect matchings. By the bag weight
range (Part II),
    D^{-1} <= W_e <= 3 * 4^K .        (32.1)

32. An exact refresh at unit activities.

LEMMA 32.1 (Base refresh).
The unit-activity partition function is computable by a dynamic program on
the label tree. With exact rational categorical choices, its tables also
produce an exact sample. The tables use O((b_tree+1)(N+1)^3) arithmetic
operations on counts of O(N log(N+1)) bits. Label and loop indices use
O(log(b_tree+1)+log(N+1)) extra bits. Once the tables exist, a sample uses
polynomially many integer operations and at most 4(b_tree+N+1) rational
categorical choices, each with at most N+1 outcomes. A positive table also
yields a matching by deterministic backtracking.

Proof. A colored matching assigns each vertex to the color of its matched
edge. Conversely, assign each vertex to one of its one or two bags, and pair
the vertices assigned to each bag; these two operations are inverse. If a_l
vertices are assigned to bag l, the number of matchings for that assignment
is prod_l J(a_l), where
    J(0)=1,  J(a)=(a-1)!! for even a>0,  J(a)=0 for odd a.
J counts pairings of a complete graph.

For a nonroot bag C write S_C for its interface with its parent and
s_C = |S_C|; for the root use S_C empty. Let u_C be the number of vertices
belonging to C alone. All interfaces incident to a bag are disjoint. For a
fixed subset of k vertices of S_C assigned to C, let F_C(k) count the
assignments and pairings within the subtree of C (other S_C vertices are
assigned to the parent). The answer depends only on k. If C_1,...,C_d are the
children of C, set s_i = s_{C_i}. Choosing l_i vertices of the i-th interface
to be assigned downward gives
    F_C(k) = sum_{l_1=0}^{s_1} ... sum_{l_d=0}^{s_d}
        J( u_C + k + sum_i (s_i - l_i) )
        prod_i C(s_i,l_i) F_{C_i}(l_i) .        (32.2)
The required partition function is F_root(0). There is no exponential
enumeration in evaluating this. Form the polynomial
    H_C(z) = prod_i ( sum_{l=0}^{s_i} C(s_i,l) F_{C_i}(l) z^{s_i-l} ).
Its degree is at most N, and
    F_C(k) = sum_{a=0}^N [z^a] H_C(z) J(u_C+k+a),
with impossible indices contributing zero. Naive convolutions suffice. The
counts enumerate assignments and partial pairings on at most N vertices.
There are at most 2^N assignments, and for any one assignment at most N!
such pairings, so 2^N N! bounds the counts, giving the bit bound.

Sampling. Begin at the root with k=0. At a visited bag choose a with
probability proportional to [z^a] H_C(z) J(u_C+k+a). Backtrack the stored
convolutions to choose the individual l_i, then choose uniformly the
corresponding interface subsets. Recurse on each child with its chosen subset,
and independently choose a uniform pairing of the vertices assigned to the
current bag. Every choice is proportional to its number of completions in
(32.2), so induction gives the uniform law on colored matchings. A uniform
subset is generated by sequential inclusion and a uniform pairing by pairing
the least remaining vertex with a uniformly chosen other vertex. The total
number of interface vertices is at most N. There are at most N/2 partner
choices, b_tree total-size choices, and b_tree-1 convolution choices. Choosing
any positive-count branch instead yields a deterministic matching. QED.

33. The product chain.

Set
    c = 1 + N^{-2} ,
    T = min{ t in Z_{>=0} : c^t >= max(D, 3*4^K) } ,
    q = 32 L ,        (33.1)
with L the constant of Theorem 26.1. At tier t in {0,...,T}, clamp every
reference activity to [c^{-t}, c^t] and denote the matching law pi_t. Thus
pi_0 is the uniform colored matching law and pi_T is the desired law.
Moreover,
    1/2 <= pi_{t-1}(A)/pi_t(A) <= 2  (A in M, 1 <= t <= T).     (33.2)
Indeed an individual activity changes by a factor in [c^{-1},c], so
unnormalized weights and partition functions each change by factors in
[c^{-N/2}, c^{N/2}]. The probability ratio is in [c^{-N}, c^N], and
c^N <= exp(1/N) < 2. Also log(1+N^{-2}) >= (2N^2)^{-1}, so
    T <= 1 + 2 N^2 log max(D,3*4^K) = O(N^2 (K+log D+1)) .      (33.3)

Use q slots at each tier, with product stationary law
    nu = tensor over t=0..T of pi_t^{tensor q} .
An ALLOWED PAIR (x,y) is a slot x at tier t >= 1 together with any slot y at
tier t-1. On this pair use exactly the symmetric proposal of Theorem 26.1,
with local proposals acting on x, and accept by Metropolis for
pi_t (x) pi_{t-1}. Across unequal tiers a cycle swap uses this Metropolis
acceptance; the acceptance-one observation for two identical laws does not
apply. For each base slot there is also the update that refreshes it from
pi_0 using Lemma 32.1. There are
    M = T q^2 + q        (33.4)
updates. The chain P holds with probability 1/2; otherwise it chooses one of
these M updates uniformly. Every update preserves nu and is reversible, so
the same is true of P.

Write E_xy for the energy of one allowed-pair update with all other
coordinates integrated against nu, and E_i for the energy of a refresh of
base slot i. Then
    E_{nu,P}(f) = (1/(2M)) ( sum_{xy} E_xy(f) + sum_{i base} E_i(f) ) .  (33.5)

The two-coordinate inequality also applies across adjacent tiers. The pair
state space and symmetric proposal are identical for the targets
pi_t (x) pi_t and pi_t (x) pi_{t-1}. By (33.2) the latter density is at least
half the former at every pair state. The Metropolis capacity formula then
shows the unequal-tier energy dominates half the equal-tier energy.
Consequently, for any functions a,b of the two slots,
    Var_{pi_t} a <= 2L E_xy( a(x)+b(y) ) .        (33.6)
Here the energy is on the two slots alone; it also applies conditionally on
any fixed values of other slots.

LEMMA 33.1 (Replica gap).
The lazy product chain has spectral gap at least 3/(8M), and hence at least
1/(20LM). The dependence on L in the stronger bound is carried by q = 32L,
and hence by M.

Proof. Order the slots from highest tier to lowest, ties broken arbitrarily.
Every allowed guide y then follows its upper slot x. For each slot x let U_x
be the set of its predecessors and put
    h_x = E[f | U_x,x] - E[f | U_x] .
These are the orthogonal martingale increments, so
    Var_nu f = sum_x ||h_x||^2 .        (33.7)
Fix an allowed pair (x,y), write U = U_x, and define
    k_xy = E[f | U,y] - E[f | U],
    g_xy = E[f | U,x,y],
    r_xy = g_xy - E[f|U] - h_x - k_xy .        (33.8)
For fixed U, h_x is centered over x and k_xy over y. Applying (33.6) and
averaging over U gives
    ||h_x||^2 <= 2L E_xy( h_x + k_xy ) .
Conditional expectation onto U,x,y decreases E_xy (the pair kernel is
independent of the other coordinates; Jensen). For any stationary Markov
kernel, the energy is at most 2||v||^2 via (a-b)^2 <= 2a^2+2b^2. Using (33.8)
and that functions of U have zero pair energy,
    ||h_x||^2 <= 4L E_xy(f) + 8L ||r_xy||^2 .        (33.9)

The residuals are orthogonal over all allowed pairs. To verify, use the
orthogonal product (ANOVA) decomposition of Efron and Stein, written
    f = sum_S f_S,   f_S = sum_{A subset S} (-1)^{|S|-|A|} E[f|A],
S ranging over subsets of the slot set. Each f_S depends only on S and is
centered in each coordinate of S, so distinct f_S are orthogonal and
E[f|A] = sum_{S subset A} f_S. Substitution into (33.8) gives
    r_xy = sum_{ S subset (U_x union {x,y}), x,y in S } f_S .    (33.10)
Every S in this sum has y last and x penultimate in the fixed slot order; all
its other coordinates lie in U_x. These two positions determine the allowed
pair uniquely. Thus even pairs sharing a slot use disjoint sets of orthogonal
components, and since every component here is nonconstant,
    sum_{xy} ||r_xy||^2 <= Var_nu f .        (33.11)

For a base slot i, conditional expectation onto U_i,i decreases its refresh
energy, and the refresh energy of h_i equals ||h_i||^2 (h_i is centered over i
given U_i). Hence ||h_i||^2 <= E_i(f). Average (33.9) over the q guides of
each nonbase slot and sum over slots. Using (33.7),(33.11),
    Var_nu f <= (4L/q) sum_{xy} E_xy(f)
              + (8L/q) Var_nu f
              + sum_{i base} E_i(f) .
Since q = 32L, absorption gives
    Var_nu f <= (1/6) sum_{xy} E_xy(f)
              + (4/3) sum_{i base} E_i(f)
              <= (8M/3) E_{nu,P}(f) .
This proves the first gap bound; the second follows from L >= 1. QED.

34. A quantitative sampling guarantee.

THEOREM 34.1 (Bag sampler).
Define T,q,M by (33.1),(33.4) and put
    Lambda = 10 N q (T+1) ( K + ceil(log2(N D + 2)) + 1 ) ,   (34.1)
    s(xi)  = ceil( 20 L M ( Lambda + ceil(log2(1/xi)) + 1 ) ) ,
              for 0 < xi < 1 .                                 (34.2)
Initialize all slots to any one colored perfect matching and run the exact
chain for s(xi) steps. The resulting product law is at total variation
distance at most xi from nu. In particular any top-tier slot has law within
xi of the desired weighted matching law.
All tables, states, and transition probabilities are computable by rational
arithmetic; their sizes, operation counts, and bit lengths are polynomial in
N,D,K,b_tree, log(1/xi), and the bit lengths of the reference activities.

Proof. Every colored matching consists of N/2 vertex pairs, each with at most
two color choices, so |M| <= (2N^2)^N. At every tier all activities remain in
the interval (32.1). For any A in M this gives
    pi_t(A) >= 1 / ( (2N^2)^N (3 D 4^K)^{N/2} ) .
Taking the product over q(T+1) slots and using N>=2,D>=1 shows
    log(1 / min nu) <= Lambda .        (34.3)
(With a = ceil(log2(ND+2)), the log of the denominator is at most
N(K + (5/2)a + 2) <= 10N(K+a+1).)

Finite-state spectral bound. Reversibility makes P self-adjoint on L^2(nu).
Laziness makes its eigenvalues nonnegative, and Lemma 33.1 bounds every
eigenvalue on the centered subspace above by 1-gamma, gamma = 1/(20LM).
Starting from a point z, the centered initial density has squared norm
1/nu(z) - 1. By diagonalization, reversibility, and (34.3),
    ||P^s(z,.) - nu||_TV <= (1/2)(1-gamma)^s sqrt(1/nu(z)-1)
        <= (1/2) exp(-gamma s + Lambda/2) .
Substitution of (34.2) makes this at most xi. Projection onto a chosen
top-tier coordinate cannot increase total variation.

Computation. The unit tables come from Lemma 32.1; a positive table supplies
an initial matching. There are at most N^2 colored edges and q(T+1) matching
coordinates. Precompute the clamped activities at every tier using rational
powers of c; the bound (33.3) is polynomial and these powers have polynomial
bit length. A scan choice is an integer in {1,...,M} decoding either a base
slot or an upper tier and two slot indices. A pair proposal inspects two
matchings; the alternating components are listed by following the union of
edges, and local switch and color proposals need only finite lists of edges
and at most two color choices per new edge. Metropolis acceptance uses a
ratio of products of at most 2N activities; the unknown partition functions
cancel. A refresh uses the precomputed integer tables. So a step has
polynomial arithmetic cost and rational probabilities of polynomial bit
length, and s(xi) is polynomial, proving the assertion for the ideal process.
The primitive finite-bit implementation is in Part IX. QED.


================================================================
PART VIII. FROM SAMPLES TO THE NUMBER OF PERFECT MATCHINGS
================================================================

Now we give the counting algorithm. It uses only the bag sampler of Part VII.
Neither balancing nor estimating a ratio needs any other oracle. The
framework is annealing: start from the complete graph, whose matchings are
easy to count, and slowly suppress the nonedges floor by floor, estimating
each successive partition ratio from fresh samples. A vertex scale, updated
from observed two-hole ratios, keeps the weight balanced so the hole bound of
Part III keeps the inflation under control.

35. The schedule and vertex scales.

If n = |V| = 0, return one. If a binary vertex count is used and n > 2|E|,
return zero: the listed edges cannot cover all vertices. On the remaining
nonempty input, run the deterministic perfect-matching existence test
(Edmonds) and return zero if it fails. The remaining case has even n >= 2 and
Z(G) >= 1. Set
    b = 1 + 1/n ,
    K = min{ k in N : k >= 1,  n^{n/2} b^{-k} <= eps/32 } .        (35.1)
Since log(1+1/n) >= 1/(2n),
    K <= 1 + 2n ( (n/2) log n + log(32/eps) ) .        (35.2)
At stage j in {0,...,K} the complete logical graph has raw activities
    w^{(j)}_{uv} = 1      if uv is in E,
                   b^{-j} if uv is not in E.
Write Z_j for its perfect-matching partition function. Then
    Z_0 = (n-1)!!,   Z_K = (n-1)!! prod_{j=0}^{K-1} Z_{j+1}/Z_j .    (35.3)

For sampling we also keep positive vertex scales s_i and use activities
lambda_ij = w^{(j)}_{ij} s_i s_j. Every perfect matching has the same vertex
factor prod_i s_i, so
    Z_lambda = Z_j prod_i s_i ,   g_lambda(ij) = g_{w^{(j)}}(ij)/(s_i s_j) .
                                                                 (35.4)
Initialize s_i = 1/floor(sqrt(n-1)). At stage zero
    g_lambda(ij) = floor(sqrt(n-1))^2 / (n-1)  in [1/4,1],
so the logical graph is balanced. The update below always multiplies each
scale by a positive number at most two, whether or not the estimates
succeed. Starting from scales at most one and making at most K updates, we
therefore have at every sampling call
    0 < lambda_ij <= 4^K .        (35.5)
We use the same structural parameters throughout a trial:
    D0 = 10^8 (n+1)^4 ,  p = 2K+2 ,  N = n + 4p C(n,2) ,  D = 100 N^2 D0 .
                                                                 (35.6)
For each current lambda construct the enlarged graph and marked bag edges of
Part IV.

36. Observables from one marked sample.

Let lambda+ denote the activities at the NEXT raw stage, keeping the current
vertex scales. If P is a logical perfect matching, or a logical matching
missing one specified pair of vertices, put
    u(P) = b^{-#{ e in P : e not in E }} .
Then wt_{lambda+}(P) = u(P) wt_lambda(P) and
    1/2 < b^{-n/2} <= u(P) <= 1 .        (36.1)
The strict left inequality follows from (1+1/n)^{n/2} < e^{1/2} < 2.

Sample a colored perfect matching from the bag graph and reveal the real,
virtual, and probe portions of its edges. Define the observables, all in
[0,1]:
    a     = 1_{ all edges real } ,
    d     = 1_{ all edges real } u(P) ,
    z_ij  = 1_{ probe ij used and all other edges real } u(P) .
Here P is decoded only on the indicated event; elsewhere the observable is
zero. On the all-real event P is a full logical perfect matching. On the
sole-probe event P is a logical perfect matching missing i,j. Write
I = Z_bag / Z' for the inflation. Under the exact marked bag law the means
satisfy
    E a      = 1/I ,
    E d      = Z_{lambda+} / ( I Z_lambda ) ,
    E z_ij   = Z_{lambda+}(-ij) / ( D I Z_lambda ) .        (36.2)
The all-real probability (Part IV, Prop 17.1) gives the first equality; the
other two follow by multiplying the weights on the all-real event, or in the
sole-probe identity, by u(P) and summing. In particular
    R := Z_{j+1}/Z_j = Z_{lambda+}/Z_lambda = (E d)/(E a) ,
    g_{lambda+}(ij) = D (E z_ij)/(E d) .        (36.3)
So one batch of marked samples estimates both the next raw partition ratio
and the two-hole ratios needed to choose the next vertex scales.

37. Estimating and restoring balance.

Use
    alpha = 10^{-5} ,
    sigma = min{ alpha/(100 D) , eps/(1000 K) } ,
    S = ceil( 10 sigma^{-2} (n+K+1) ) ,
    xi = 1/(1000 K S) .        (37.1)
At each stage make S independent fresh sampler runs, each with marked output
law at total variation distance at most 3 xi from the exact marked bag law
(Part IX implements them in bounded-bit time). Let bars denote empirical
means. Set
    Rhat = bar d / bar a   if bar a > 0,
           1               if bar a = 0 .        (37.2)
If bar d > 0 form the symmetric array
    G_ij = max{ alpha, min{ 3, D bar z_ij / bar d + alpha } }  (i != j) .
                                                                 (37.3)
If bar d = 0 set all G_ij = 1. The following elementary procedure balances.

LEMMA 37.1 (One-pass scaling).
Suppose G is symmetric and alpha <= G_ij <= 3 for i != j. Initialize v_i = 2
for every vertex and visit vertices in the fixed order, setting
    v_i <- max_{j != i} G_ij / v_j .        (37.4)
Every coordinate only decreases, all final coordinates lie in [alpha/2,2],
and the final vector satisfies v_i v_j >= G_ij with equality in every row.

Proof. Initially all products equal four and dominate G. At an update,
feasibility shows the new value is at most the old. The definition restores
all constraints involving i and leaves other constraints unchanged. Since
every other coordinate is at most two, the new value is at least alpha/2. At
least one constraint involving i is tight; if its neighbor is visited later,
that neighbor cannot decrease without violating this tight constraint, so
the equality persists to the end. QED.

After estimating the current ratio, run this procedure and replace s_i by
s_i v_i. These scales are used with the next raw stage.

Algorithm outline. After the zero test, one trial uses the parameters above
and the initial scales. For j = 0,...,K-1:
  1. Form lambda_uv = w^{(j)}_{uv} s_u s_v and its marked bag graph.
  2. Obtain S independent fresh marked samples (Part VII, bounded-bit version
     of Part IX). Compute empirical means, then Rhat_j and G by (37.2),(37.3),
     including the zero-denominator conventions.
  3. Run the one-pass update (37.4) on G, replace s_i by s_i v_i, and advance
     to the next raw stage.
The trial returns Y = (n-1)!! prod_{j=0}^{K-1} Rhat_j. The final algorithm
takes a median over independent trials as below. The current scales are held
fixed during each ratio estimate, so their common factors cancel. Every
empirical history follows these same steps with the caps (35.5). Balance is
used only to prove accuracy for histories whose estimates succeed.

LEMMA 37.2 (Successful stage).
Suppose the current logical activities are balanced and every empirical mean
is within sigma of its exact mean. Then the next scaled activities are
balanced and
    | Rhat/R - 1 | <= 10 sigma <= eps/(100K) .        (37.5)

Proof. By Part IV inflation and (36.1),
    E a >= 1/2 ,  E d >= 1/4 ,  1/2 <= R <= 1 .
Reweighting both a full matching and a matching with two holes by factors in
[1/2,1] gives
    (1/2) g_lambda(ij) <= g_{lambda+}(ij) <= 2 g_lambda(ij) .
In particular the pre-update row maxima lie in [1/8,2]. Write
g^+_ij = g_{lambda+}(ij). Since bar d >= E d - sigma > 0,
    | D bar z_ij/bar d - g^+_ij |
        <= ( D|bar z_ij - E z_ij| + g^+_ij |bar d - E d| )/(E d - sigma)
        <= (D+2) sigma / (1/4 - sigma) < alpha ,
using D >= 1 and sigma <= alpha/(100D). Since 0 <= g^+_ij <= 2, both
truncations in (37.3) preserve
    g^+_ij <= G_ij <= g^+_ij + 2 alpha .        (37.6)

During scaling every updated coordinate satisfies v_i >= max_j G_ij / 2 >=
1/16. At the end all entries of the newly scaled two-hole matrix are at most
one, by feasibility and (35.4). In each row choose a final tight neighbor. For
that pair,
    g^+_ij/(v_i v_j) >= 1 - 2 alpha/(v_i v_j) >= 1 - 512 alpha > 1/4 .
This proves balance.

For the ratio, using R >= 1/2 and bar a >= 1/2 - sigma,
    |Rhat/R - 1| <= ( |bar d - E d| + R|bar a - E a| ) / ( R(E a - sigma) )
        <= 2 sigma / ((1/2)(1/2 - sigma)) < 10 sigma .
The final bound in (37.5) is the other choice in (37.1). QED.

38. Success probability and output.

Hoeffding's inequality (for independent [0,1] variables with common mean mu):
    Pr[ |(1/S) sum X_i - mu| > t ] <= 2 exp(-2 S t^2) .        (38.1)
It applies separately to each observable; observables within one sample need
not be independent.

Condition on any history in which all preceding stages succeeded. By Lemma
37.2 the current activities are balanced. For independent ideal samples, a
union bound over the at most n^2+2 observables gives failure probability at
most
    2(n^2+2) exp(-2 S sigma^2) <= 2(n^2+2) exp(-20(n+K+1)) < 1/(100K) .
(One check: log(200K(n^2+2)) <= 6+K+2n < 20(n+K+1) for n>=2,K>=1.) The actual
fresh runs couple to these ideal samples with disagreement probability at
most 3 S xi = 3/(1000K). So the conditional probability of failure at the
first unsuccessful stage is at most 13/(1000K). Summing over K stages, a whole
trial succeeds with probability at least 1 - 13/1000 > 0.9.

Trial output. The trial returns the nonnegative rational
    Y = (n-1)!! prod_{j=0}^{K-1} Rhat_j .        (38.2)
On success, Lemma 37.2 and prod_i(1-t_i) >= 1 - sum_i t_i for t_i in [0,1]
give
    (1 - eps/100) Z_K <= Y <= exp(eps/100) Z_K .
Every matching using a nonedge has raw final weight at most b^{-K}. There are
at most (n-1)!! <= n^{n/2} perfect matchings on the complete graph, so
    Z(G) <= Z_K <= Z(G) + n^{n/2} b^{-K} <= (1 + eps/32) Z(G) .   (38.3)
For 0 < eps < 1 the resulting lower bound exceeds (1-eps)Z(G) and the upper
bound is at most (1+eps)Z(G). For example exp(eps/100) <= 1 + eps/50, which
gives
    exp(eps/100)(1 + eps/32)
        <= 1 + (1/50 + 1/32 + 1/1600) eps < 1 + eps .

Amplify. Let ell = ceil(log2(1/delta)), computed by rational comparisons with
powers of two. Run r = 10(ell+1) + 1 independent trials and return their
median (r is odd). Since each trial succeeds with probability at least 0.9,
Hoeffding's inequality on the success indicators bounds the probability of
fewer than half succeeding by
    exp(-2 (0.4)^2 r) <= exp(-3.2(ell+1)) < 2^{-ell} <= delta .
Whenever a majority succeed, the median lies in the desired interval. The
deterministic zero branch was taken before any trials. This proves the
correctness and probability assertions of Theorem 1.1, subject only to the
bit implementation of Part IX.


================================================================
PART IX. A BOUNDED-BIT IMPLEMENTATION
================================================================

So far "polynomial time" was proved for an ideal process. Here we specify a
classical finite-bit implementation and bound its worst-case bit cost, over
EVERY random history including ones where balance is lost. Balance controls
statistical accuracy of a successful trial; the unconditional caps control
running time.

39. Sizes of the parameters and state spaces.

Let b be the total binary input length. Use a standard explicit encoding of a
finite graph and the two rationals. If isolated vertices are given by a binary
vertex count, first return zero when n > 2|E|. This test is valid for every
nonempty graph admitting a perfect matching and ensures the nonzero branch
has n <= 2|E| = O(b). The deterministic existence test therefore has
polynomial bit cost on the original graph; it is the only matching-existence
oracle used.

The schedule length K is polynomial in b (35.2). It can be found without
logarithms by iterating rational powers of b and comparing the two sides of
(35.1). The parameters p,N,D0,D are integers of polynomial numerical size in
n,K. The hierarchy has at most
    H_tree = N(2K+1)        (39.1)
nodes, since its levels are 0,...,2K and every level has at most N
components. The number of colored edges is less than N^2.

Use the ladder parameters c,T,L,q,M,Lambda of Part VII and write
t_mix = s(xi). The structural parameters N,D,K are fixed throughout a trial,
so the same T makes the final tier unclamped on every empirical history, by
(35.5) and (32.1). It can be found by iterating rational powers of c until
the defining inequality in (33.1) holds; the bound (33.3) is polynomial.
Ceilings of binary logarithms are found by integer or rational comparisons
with powers of two. These bounds and the product-state size q(T+1) are
polynomial in b and eps^{-1}. Indeed
    sigma^{-1} <= C(D + K eps^{-1}) ,
    S = O( (D^2 + K^2 eps^{-2})(n+K+1) ) ,
    xi^{-1} = 1000 K S
for an absolute constant C, and the exponent 100 in L is fixed.

There is no initial-sampling problem at the upper tiers. Pair the logical
vertices in their fixed order and expand this matching by the path tilings.
All logical activities are positive, so this is an available real perfect
matching with assigned colors. Initialize every slot to that matching. The
mixing bound applies from this deterministic product state.

40. Rational bit lengths.

We record why the adaptive scales do not cause arithmetic blowup. Put
    B_G = 1 + n ceil(log2(n+1)) + ceil(log2(S+1)) + ceil(log2(D+1)) . (40.1)
Absolute multiplicative constants are independent of the input.

Every nonzero sample contribution is (n/(n+1))^a for some 0 <= a <= n/2.
These fractions share the denominator Q = (n+1)^{n/2}. Thus empirical means,
their ratios, and the capped entries G_ij have O(B_G) numerator and
denominator bits; this bound does not involve the current scales.

An assignment in (37.4) chooses one maximizer j and sets v_i = G_ij / v_j. At
that moment v_j is either the initial value two or an expression formed at an
earlier update. Following dependencies strictly decreases the update index, so
it produces a chain of at most n factors. Each v_i has O(n B_G) bits.
Accumulating at most K scale updates gives O(K n B_G) bits per scale. Raw
activities have O(K log(n+1)) bits. These estimates hold even when all
observations are unfavorable, because the clipping and branch conventions are
unconditional.

Bottleneck entries B_ij and maxima H_i select from finitely many input
activities and one; they do not involve sums over paths. Initialize
B_ij = max{1, lambda_ij} and, for each vertex k, update the entries with
distinct i,j != k by
    B_ij <- max{ B_ij, min{ B_ik, B_kj } } .
This is the max-min Floyd-Warshall recurrence: after processing k the entries
allow the processed vertices as internal path vertices. It uses O(n^3) rational
comparisons. Path profiles add only powers of two of bit length O(K). Bag
activities are sums of a real activity and a dyadic height divided by D.
Finally c^{+-t} has O(T log(N+1)) bits for t <= T.

It follows that every single-edge activity used by the sampler has numerator
and denominator lengths at most
    B_W = C1 ( K n B_G + K + ceil(log2(D+1)) + T ceil(log2(N+1)) + 1 ) ,
                                                                 (40.2)
for a sufficiently large absolute positive integer C1. A product of at most
N such activities has O(N B_W) bits. In a Metropolis acceptance ratio the
unknown normalizing constants cancel, so even a cycle exchange at unequal
tiers needs only rational products of this size. The real and probe
revelation probabilities are likewise rational (the real portion divided by
the collapsed activity, and 1/(D W) for the probe).

By Lemma 32.1 the bottom-tier dynamic program uses counts of
O(N log(N+1)) bits; its categorical normalizers count the same partial
completions. Label and loop indices use an extra
O(log(H_tree+1)+log(N+1)) bits, since b_tree <= H_tree in the constructed
hierarchy. Table computation and sampling use polynomially many integer
operations with polynomial bit lengths.

41. Random choices with a fixed number of bits.

LEMMA 41.1 (Finite random choice).
Let p_1,...,p_r be nonnegative rationals summing to one. Choose a uniform
integer J in {0,...,2^k-1} using exactly k fair bits, and return the least
index i such that J/2^k < p_1+...+p_i. The output law is at total variation
distance at most 2r 2^{-k} from (p_i).

Proof. For any interval [a,b) in [0,1) the number of grid points j/2^k in it
differs from 2^k(b-a) by at most two. Apply this to the successive
cumulative-probability intervals and sum absolute discrepancies; the 1/2 in
total variation only improves the bound. An outcome with p_i = 0 has an
empty interval and is never returned. QED.

The exact categorical choices in the unit-tier dynamic program define an
ideal transition law. The actual algorithm implements each such choice, along
with every proposal and acceptance choice, by the fixed grid of Lemma 41.1.
Hence exactness of the dynamic program is a counting identity; sampling from
its tables in bounded-bit time incurs the error budget below.

Every choice in the chain is either uniform on an explicitly indexed finite
set, Bernoulli, or proportional to a list of nonnegative integers supplied by
the dynamic program. Final revelation of each used edge is a rational
categorical choice with at most three outcomes. Subsets and pairings are
sampled sequentially, so no exponential list is needed. Uniform choices among
the M scan positions are implemented by integer arithmetic on the index rather
than by enumerating positions.

A bound on the number of random choices in one complete sampler run,
including marking its output, is
    R_draw = 100 (t_mix+1) (N+1)^2 (K+2) .        (41.1)
Laziness and the scan take two choices per step, a pair update takes at most
eight more, a refresh (by Lemma 32.1) takes at most 4(H_tree+N+1), and final
marking takes at most N. Allowing both bounds at every step and adding the
marking gives
    t_mix ( 10 + 4(H_tree+N+1) ) + N ,
and since H_tree = N(2K+1) this is bounded by (41.1). The bound covers every
categorical choice made by the implemented sampler. The number of options in
any one choice is at most
    R_opt = 10 (M + N^2 + 1) .        (41.2)
Choose the same bit budget k for all choices, with
    2^k >= 2 R_draw R_opt / xi .        (41.3)
This integer is found by doubling; k is polynomially bounded. By Lemma 41.1
the sum of all conditional simulation errors is at most xi. Successive
coupling, while the simulated and exact histories agree, bounds the total
variation error of the whole run and its marking by xi. The exact-kernel
mixing error is at most xi, so the implemented marked sample is within 2xi,
and in particular 3xi, of the required law. The number of fair bits used has
the deterministic bound k R_draw; there is no rejection loop or random
convergence test.

42. Total bit cost.

The preceding bounds already give a polynomial-time implementation. To make
the accounting explicit, take a common bit bound
    B_* = C ( N B_W + N log(N+1) + b + log(M+1) + log(t_mix+1)
              + log(xi^{-1}+1) + 1 )
with a sufficiently large absolute constant C and B_W from (40.2). In
particular N B_W dominates K B_G, the bit cost of multiplying the K empirical
ratios. B_* covers all integers used in weight products, dynamic-program
counts, cumulative choices, acceptance comparisons, and output products. All
categorical laws admit common-denominator integer weights, so their
cumulative sums respect the same bound. Schoolbook arithmetic and the
Euclidean algorithm perform the needed operations within O(B_*^3) bit time
each.

A deliberately loose bound of O((H_tree+1)(N+1)^4) arithmetic operations
covers graph construction, the bottom-tier tables, and any one transition or
refresh; storing the product state takes O(q(T+1) N B_*) bits. Thus a trial,
including all K stages and S fresh runs at each stage, has bit cost bounded by
a fixed polynomial in
    K, S, t_mix, H_tree, N, q(T+1), B_* .
For example the sum of
    C K S (t_mix+1)(H_tree+1)(N+1)^4 B_*^3
and
    C K S q(T+1)(N+1) B_*^3
is a valid coarse envelope for arithmetic, repeated initialization, and
storage. Each displayed parameter is polynomial in b and eps^{-1}. The
r = 10(ceil(log2(1/delta))+1)+1 repetitions and the rational comparisons used
to select their median add a factor polynomial in log(1/delta) and the input
length.

All data structures, loop bounds, and rational comparisons are now specified
by finite procedures. Every auxiliary parameter is bounded by a polynomial of
fixed degree, and the bound holds on every random history. Together with
Part VIII this completes the proof of Theorem 1.1.


================================================================
PART X. PRESCRIBED-DEGREE SUBGRAPHS AND FIXED-SIZE MATCHINGS

43. What else we get.

Two counting problems reduce to perfect-matching counting with a constant
number of preimages per target object, so Theorem 1.1 immediately gives them
an FPRAS. We also need a sampler, so we build an explicit deletion sampler
that uses only Theorem 1.1.

Input here: a finite simple undirected graph G = (V,E), vertices and edges
explicitly listed, isolated vertices included. Only edges of E may be chosen,
so absent edges may be forbidden freely. Counted objects are LABELED edge
subsets, not isomorphism classes. For binary-encoded integer demands
f = (f_v) and a binary-encoded integer k define
    F(G,f) = { F subset E : deg_F(v) = f_v for every v } ,
    M_k(G) = { M subset E : M is a matching and |M| = k } .

COROLLARY 43.1 (Prescribed degrees and fixed cardinality).
For A = F(G,f) or A = M_k(G) there are uniform classical randomized
algorithms with:
 (i)  Given 0<eps<1 and 0<delta<1/2, return a nonnegative rational Nhat with
      Pr[(1-eps)|A| <= Nhat <= (1+eps)|A|] >= 1-delta. If A is empty, return
      zero with certainty. Bit time on every execution is polynomial in the
      full input length, 1/eps, and log(1/delta).
 (ii) Given 0<tau<1, report infeasibility exactly when A is empty; otherwise
      return some X in A on every execution, with
      TV( law(X), uniform on A ) <= tau. Bit time on every execution is
      polynomial in the input length and 1/tau.

Only total variation and a polynomial-in-1/tau guarantee is claimed, not exact
sampling. The explicit simple unweighted host model does not encode edge
weights or compressed multiplicities.

44. Constant-fibre reductions.

Write n=|V|, m=|E|, d_v = deg_G(v).

Prescribed degrees. Use Tutte's reduction of prescribed degrees to perfect
matchings, keeping dummy vertices LABELED so the count is exact. If some f_v is
outside 0 <= f_v <= d_v, the family is empty; return zero or report
infeasibility. For valid demands put s_v = d_v - f_v. Build a simple
unweighted graph H_f with one tagged PORT (v,e) per incidence of e at v. For
each original edge e={u,v} join (u,e) to (v,e) by a cross edge. At each v add
s_v labeled private dummy vertices, each adjacent to all d_v ports at v. No
other edges; tags make H_f simple. Its sizes are
    |V(H_f)| = 2m + sum_v s_v = 4m - sum_v f_v <= 4m ,
    |E(H_f)| = m + sum_v d_v s_v <= m + 2m(n-1) .
The initial demand check prevents expansion of huge binary demands.

Project a perfect matching of H_f to the original edges whose cross edges it
contains. Each dummy at v uses a distinct v-port, so the remaining f_v ports
use cross edges and the projection is in F(G,f). Conversely an f-factor fixes
its cross edges; its remaining s_v ports at v biject to the labeled dummies in
s_v! ways, independently per vertex. So every factor has the same number of
preimages, and
    C_f = prod_v (d_v - f_v)! ,   Z(H_f) = C_f |F(G,f)| .        (44.1)
The fibre may be factorial, but log C_f = O(m log(n+1)), so C_f has polynomial
bit length. The empty host with all demands zero gives the unique empty factor
and the unique empty matching, with C_f = 1 by 0! = 1. Other infeasible valid
inputs give gadgets without perfect matchings by the same correspondence.

Fixed cardinality. If k<0 or 2k>n, the family is empty. Otherwise put
q = n-2k, add q labeled dummies to G, join each dummy to every original
vertex, add no edges between dummies, keep all original edges. This simple
graph H_k has at most 2n vertices and m+n^2 edges. Every perfect matching uses
the dummies on q distinct original vertices; the other 2k are paired by exactly
k host edges. Conversely a k-matching leaves q original vertices, which biject
to the dummies in q! ways. Therefore
    C_k = (n-2k)! ,   Z(H_k) = C_k |M_k(G)| .        (44.2)
Here log C_k = O(n log(n+1)); the same bijection covers k=0, 2k=n, n=k=0.

Counting proof of Corollary 43.1(i). Apply Theorem 1.1 to the gadget and
divide its nonnegative rational output by C_f or C_k. Compute the factorials
exactly and divide exactly as rationals, never rounding to an integer. The
displayed bit lengths and polynomial gadget sizes keep the relative error,
confidence, certain-zero behavior, and every-execution bit bound. Feasibility
is equivalent to perfect-matching feasibility in the gadget.

45. A deletion sampler for perfect matchings.

The conversion from counts to samples uses the self-reduction framework of
Jerrum, Valiant, Vazirani. We spell it out to keep feasibility on every
execution and to track total variation and bit time. It calls Theorem 1.1 only
through its counting guarantee.

LEMMA 45.1 (Deletion sampler).
Given a finite simple undirected unweighted graph H_0 with vertices and edges
explicitly listed, and a rational 0<tau<1, there is a uniform classical
randomized algorithm that reports infeasibility exactly when H_0 has no perfect
matching. Otherwise it returns a perfect matching on every execution, and its
output law is within total variation distance tau of the uniform
perfect-matching law. Bit time on every execution is polynomial in the input
length and 1/tau.

Proof. Decide feasibility with Edmonds. Report infeasibility if appropriate;
return the empty matching when V(H_0) is empty. Otherwise set m_0 = |E(H_0)|
and m_* = max{1,m_0}. Choose
    eta = gamma = tau/(8 m_*) ,   t = min{ j in N : 2^j >= 8 m_*/tau } ,
the integer t found by doubling and exact comparison.

Maintain a feasible residual graph H and the already selected edges. Choose the
first residual edge e=uv in a fixed order. Perfect matchings of H excluding e
are Z(H-e); those including e correspond, after deleting e, to perfect
matchings of H-u-v. Test both children for feasibility exactly; at least one is
feasible. If only one, take it. If both, make two fresh calls to Theorem 1.1,
relative error eta and failure probability gamma, getting nonnegative rational
estimates A for Z(H-u-v) and B for Z(H-e). If A+B=0 take the exclusion child.
Else phat = A/(A+B); draw exactly t fresh fair bits as J in {0,...,2^t-1}; take
the inclusion child precisely when J < floor(2^t phat). Replace H by the chosen
child; when the inclusion child is taken (including a forced choice), append e
to the selected edges. All ratio and floor operations are exact.

Every chosen child is feasible regardless of estimates. Each decision removes
at least one residual edge, so there are at most m_0 decisions. When no
residual edges remain, feasibility forces the residual vertex set empty, so the
selected edges are a perfect matching on every execution.

Error. Compare to the ideal deletion sampler that takes the inclusion child
with its exact count ratio; its ratios telescope, giving the uniform
perfect-matching law. At a history where both children are feasible, write
their positive counts z_+, z_-, p = z_+/(z_+ + z_-). If both estimates succeed,
A = z_+(1+a), B = z_-(1+b), |a|,|b| <= eta, then
    |phat - p| = z_+ z_- |a-b| / ((z_++z_-)(z_+(1+a)+z_-(1+b)))
               <= eta / (2(1-eta)) ,
using z_+ z_- <= (z_++z_-)^2/4. Fresh randomness bounds the conditional chance
that either estimate fails by 2 gamma at every adaptive history. On failure the
branch law changes by at most one in total variation, while the dyadic floor
changes the successful branch probability by at most 2^{-t}. The fallback
A+B=0 occurs only on a failed estimate when both true counts are positive.
Thus the conditional branch-law error is at most
    beta = eta/(2(1-eta)) + 2 gamma + 2^{-t} .
Forced branches have zero error. Couple the processes until their prefixes
differ; with at most m_0 decisions the output total variation is at most
m_0 beta. Since eta < 1/8 and 2^{-t} <= eta, beta <= 4 eta, so
m_0 beta <= 4 m_* eta = tau/2 < tau.

Cost. At most 2 m_0 counting calls and 2 m_0 + 1 feasibility tests, all on
graphs no larger than H_0. The inverse relative error is 8 m_*/tau, the
confidence cost is log(8 m_*/tau). The every-execution bit bound of Theorem 1.1
also bounds the bit lengths of its outputs on failed calls. So exact ratio and
floor computations, the t = O(log(m_*/tau)) fresh bits per branch, and all
graph updates have bit cost polynomial in the input length and 1/tau on every
execution. No rejection loop or random stopping is used. QED.

Sampling proof of Corollary 43.1(ii). Apply the deletion sampler to H_f or H_k,
then decode: keep the original edges represented by selected cross edges, or
discard dummy-incident edges. Exact feasibility testing of the gadget reports
infeasibility precisely for an empty target family. Uniform perfect matchings
project to the uniform target law because the fibres (44.1),(44.2) are constant
across target members. Deterministic decoding cannot increase total variation
and runs in polynomial time without enumerating any fibre. Every decoded output
is feasible. This proves the sampling assertion and completes the corollary.
