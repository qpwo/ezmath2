================================================================================
  A COUNTEREXAMPLE TO KAPLANSKY'S DIRECT-FINITENESS CONJECTURE
  IN CHARACTERISTIC TWO

PART I. WHAT THIS PAPER IS ABOUT
================================================================================

1. THE QUESTION IN ONE SENTENCE
--------------------------------
If two square matrices A, B over a field satisfy A B = I, must
B A = I? Yes, by elementary linear algebra. The same question for
infinite-dimensional linear maps has a subtle answer. Kaplansky's
direct-finiteness conjecture was that the same ought to be true for
a group ring: if a b = 1 in K[G], must b a = 1? The answer turns
out to be NO in characteristic two.

2. PREREQUISITES: GROUPS
------------------------
A group is a set G together with an operation G x G -> G, written
(g, h) -> g h, satisfying:

  - associativity:  (g h) k = g (h k);
  - identity:       there is 1 in G with 1 g = g 1 = g;
  - inverses:       for every g there is g^-1 in G with
                       g g^-1 = g^-1 g = 1.

Examples.
  - The integers under addition, (Z, +).
  - The nonzero reals under multiplication.
  - The symmetric group S_n: all permutations of {1, . . . , n}
    under composition. S_3 has six elements and is nonabelian.
  - Z/nZ, the integers modulo n, under +.
  - A group generated by symbols subject to a set of equations. For
    example

        G = <x, y | x^2 = y^3 = (x y)^5 = 1>

    means: the group generated by x and y, with exactly the relations
    shown. If G is defined by finitely many generators and finitely
    many relations, we call G FINITELY PRESENTED. Many natural
    groups are finitely presented; many others are not.

A subgroup is a subset closed under the operation and inverse. The
order of an element g is the smallest positive n with g^n = 1, if
one exists, and infinite otherwise. "Odd-order torsion" means: G
contains an element of finite order n but n is odd.

3. PREREQUISITES: FIELDS, CHARACTERISTIC p, FINITE FIELDS
---------------------------------------------------------
A field K is a set with + and x making it behave like Q, R, or C.
Characteristic p means: 1 + 1 + ... + 1 (p terms) = 0 in K, and p
is the smallest such positive integer. Examples:

  - Q, R, C: characteristic 0.
  - F_p = Z/pZ: characteristic p.
  - F_{p^r}: finite field with p^r elements, also characteristic p.
  - Specifically F_2 = {0, 1}: characteristic two, and 1 + 1 = 0.

In this paper the field K has characteristic two, so 2 = 0 as a
scalar. That small fact -- "the integer 2 is zero, but the integer
3 is not zero" -- is used throughout the construction. Whenever
the paper says "because the characteristic is two", this is why.

4. PREREQUISITES: GROUP RINGS (GROUP ALGEBRAS)
----------------------------------------------
Let K be a field and G a group. K[G] is the set of formal finite
sums

      a = sum_{g in G} c_g g,
          where c_g in K and c_g = 0 for all but finitely many g.

Two such sums add coefficient by coefficient. They multiply by
multiplying the group elements and collecting coefficients:

      (sum_{g in G} c_g g) (sum_{h in G} d_h h)
         = sum_{g, h in G} (c_g d_h) (g h).

The result is again a finite formal sum: both factors were finite,
so the product is a sum of finitely many nonzero terms. The identity
of K[G] is the group identity 1_G with coefficient 1.

K[G] is called the group ring, or group algebra, of G over K. It is
a ring. It is non-commutative whenever G is non-abelian.

Example. Take G = Z/3Z = {1, z, z^2} with z^3 = 1 and K = F_2.
Then K[G] has 8 elements: all sums c_0 1 + c_1 z + c_2 z^2 with
each c_i in {0, 1}. Product:

      (1 + z) (1 + z) = 1 + 2 z + z^2 = 1 + z^2  (since 2 = 0).

Fine print on "finite support". K[G] is NOT the set of all functions
G -> K. It is the set of all such functions that are nonzero at only
finitely many points. This is essential: finite support is what
makes the product well-defined without infinite sums.

5. WHAT "DIRECTLY FINITE" MEANS
-------------------------------
A unital ring R is DIRECTLY FINITE if the implication

      a b = 1 in R   implies   b a = 1 in R

holds for all a, b in R. (Some authors say "Dedekind finite".)

For finite-dimensional linear maps, direct finiteness is a fact of
linear algebra. Over a finite-dimensional vector space, if A B = I
then A is surjective (as B is a right inverse), so A is injective
(dimension argument), so A is an isomorphism, so B A = I. The whole
point of Kaplansky's conjecture is that a general group algebra
K[G] is NOT of finite dimension: each element has finite SUPPORT,
but the group itself is infinite.

A ring R is STABLY FINITE if every finite square matrix ring
Mat_n(R) is directly finite. In finite dimension these coincide. In
infinite dimensions they can fail in different ways, and that
difference will matter below.

6. KAPLANSKY'S DIRECT-FINITENESS CONJECTURE
-------------------------------------------
Conjecture (Kaplansky 1970; paraphrased). For every field K and
every group G, K[G] is directly finite.

Kaplansky posed this in a paper about infinite groups and group
algebras. "Directly finite" and "stably finite" are his terms. Even
a one-sided scalar inverse in a general ring is not automatic; the
conjecture was that group rings always behave like finite square
matrices.

7. WHAT THIS PAPER PROVES
-------------------------
The main theorem says Kaplansky's conjecture is FALSE. Specifically:

      There is a finite field K of characteristic two and a
      finitely presented group G containing an element of odd prime
      order, plus finite sums a_out, b_out in K[G], such that

            a_out b_out = 1      but      b_out a_out != 1.

Two remarks.
  (a) The group G is not a small familiar group (finite, free,
      abelian, etc.). It is engineered.
  (b) F_2 = {0, 1} by itself is not the counterexample field. The
      field is a finite extension of F_2 large enough to contain a
      primitive l-th root of unity for an odd prime l. The
      extension is finite, so K is still finite.

So the paper says: Kaplansky was right in characteristic zero,
probably right in many other characteristics, but wrong in
characteristic two.

8. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR SOFIC GROUPS
-----------------------------------
The paper also shows that the group G it constructs is NONSOFIC.
"Sofic" is a notion of "approximable by finite groups"; here is the
shape of it. For a nonempty finite set Omega, let Perm(Omega) be
the group of permutations of Omega, and for two permutations
f, h of Omega let

      d(f, h) = (number of omega in Omega with f(omega) != h(omega))
                / |Omega|.

A group G is SOFIC if, for every finite subset E of G and every
eps > 0, there are a finite nonempty set Omega and a map
sigma : G -> Perm(Omega) such that

  - d(sigma(1), id)                              <= eps;
  - d(sigma(g h), sigma(g) sigma(h))             <= eps  for all g, h in E;
  - d(sigma(g), id)                              >  1 - eps  for every g in E with g != 1.

The intuition: sigma is a "finite shadow" of multiplication in G.
Every finite group is sofic (take the regular representation). Free
groups are sofic. Solvable groups are sofic. All soficity comes from
finite approximants of multiplication, up to an error that shrinks
under refinement of Omega. Whether every group is sofic was open for
over two decades.

The paper's group G is FINITELY PRESENTED and NONSOFIC. Before the
paper existed, some nonsofic groups had been exhibited, but none
were known to be finitely presented. So this is a new qualitative
type of counterexample.

9. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR CELLULAR AUTOMATA (SURJUNCTIVITY)
--------------------------------------------------------
For a set G and a finite alphabet A, a "configuration" is any
function x : G -> A. (Think of x as writing a letter of A at each
element of G.) For a finite subset M of G and a function
phi : A^M -> A, define a map tau on configurations by

      tau(x)(g) = phi( (x(g u))_{u in M} ).

So tau(x)(g) depends only on a FINITE set of "nearby" values of x
around g. Such tau is called a CELLULAR AUTOMATON. If tau is
injective as a map on configurations, is tau also surjective? For
finite-dimensional vector spaces, injective implies surjective. For
infinite configuration spaces this fails dramatically; for example,
"shift one letter to the right" is injective but not surjective on
A^Z.

A group G is SURJUNCTIVE if for every finite nonempty alphabet A,
every injective cellular automaton on configurations over G is also
surjective. Gottschalk's conjecture (1973) is that every group is
surjunctive.

This paper proves the constructed G is NOT surjunctive: the pair
(a_out, b_out) in K[G] gives a cellular automaton with alphabet K
that is injective but not surjective. Because G is finitely
presented, G is an unusually concrete group; the negative answer to
Gottschalk's conjecture is correspondingly concrete.

10. WHY CHARACTERISTIC TWO AND WHY ODD TORSION
----------------------------------------------
Two numeric facts power the construction.

  (a) Odd torsion gives an idempotent average. If G contains an
      element z of odd prime order l, and K contains a primitive
      l-th root of unity zeta, then

            e = (1/l) (z^0 + zeta^-1 z^1 + zeta^-2 z^2 + ... + zeta^-(l-1) z^(l-1))

      is a well-defined element of K[G]. Direct computation gives
      e^2 = e and e z = zeta e. So e is an idempotent that "twists"
      by zeta when conjugated by z. The 1/l makes sense only when
      l is invertible in K, so l must be prime to char K. Since
      char K = 2 and l is odd, 1/l exists.

  (b) Orthogonal character idempotents. In F_l^2, define

            Q_alpha = (1/l^2) sum_{u in F_l^2} zeta^(alpha(u)) u

      for each linear form alpha : F_l^2 -> F_l. One checks:
            Q_alpha Q_beta = 0  if alpha != beta;
            Q_alpha^2 = Q_alpha.
      These give orthogonal "slots" in the group algebra, and the
      slots are the building blocks of matrix packing.

  (c) The integer 2 is zero, but the integer 3 is not. In the
      geometric part the paper builds a family of "lines" through
      a finite vector space, with each point on exactly D = l + 1
      lines. Here l is odd and D = l + 1 is even. Since char K = 2,
      the scalar D = 0 in K. That vanishing makes a pair of seeming
      contradictions coincide: the line-sum pairing vanishes
      on each line AND on all points at once (in characteristic
      two you cannot recover the total from D times it).

So the two hypotheses pull in different directions:
  - characteristic two is what makes the line-sums vanish;
  - odd prime order is what makes the averages of l-cycles and the
    primitive l-th roots exist and 1/l invertible in K.

That is the "why" for the two hypotheses.

11. WHY FINITE PRESENTATION MATTERS
-----------------------------------
Kaplansky's conjecture is about EVERY group. A single G suffices to
refute it, so at a glance any G would do. Finite presentation is
strengthening: it makes the group an explicit, computable object
rather than a theoretical existence. This is why the paper spends
Sections 02-criterion and 05-prescription on getting the group
finitely presented, and on turning "there is a counterexample" into
"here is a computation that finishes".

PART II. EXACT STATEMENTS OF THE MAIN RESULTS
================================================================================

The original proves exactly six headline results, plus many technical
lemmas. Below are the six, restated cleanly. Throughout: K is a
finite field of characteristic two, l is an odd prime, zeta is a
primitive l-th root of unity in K (or in a finite extension of K),
and kappa stands for l^2.

Theorem 1.1 (Main result). There exist a finite field K of
characteristic two, a finitely presented group G containing an
element of odd prime order, and finite sums a_out, b_out in K[G]
such that

      a_out b_out = 1            and            b_out a_out != 1.

The field, group, and finite sums are specified by a terminating
prescription involving finite sets and finite-field arithmetic.

Remark. The theorem uses "characteristic two", not "K = F_2". The
field K is a finite extension of F_2 that contains a primitive l-th
root of unity for an odd prime l. There is nothing special about the
specific field; it is chosen to make the construction work.

Theorem 2.1 (Incidence criterion). Let l be an odd prime, and let K
be a finite field of characteristic two containing a primitive l-th
root of unity zeta. Put kappa = l^2. Let V be a finite set of
cardinality t, and let F be a finite family of nonempty subsets of V.
Assume:

  (a) every v in V belongs to exactly l + 1 members of F, and
  (b) the bipartite incidence graph of (V, F) has girth at least 12.

Suppose an integer m and column vectors x_v in K^m, v in V, satisfy

  (c) 0 < m < t <= m * kappa,

  (d) sum_{v in V} x_v x_v^T = -I_m,     and
  (e) sum_{v in f} x_v x_v^T = 0    for every f in F.

Then there are a finitely presented group G containing an element of
order l and finite sums a_out, b_out in K[G] such that

      a_out b_out = 1            and            b_out a_out != 1.

The elements a_out, b_out are given by explicit formulas (PARTS III
and IX below; Section 02-criterion of the original).

What each hypothesis does:
  (a) exact incidence gives the "subgroup average" identity;
  (b) large girth stops nonobvious relations from collapsing the
      local subgroups K_v = F_l^2 in the glued group;
  (c) the upper bound t <= m kappa leaves room to pack t
      coordinates into m slots, the lower bound m < t is what
      detects the reverse-product defect;
  (d) + (e) are algebraic identities that build a rectangular
      matrix identity over the group ring of the glued group.

Proposition 3.3 (The pairing of holes). Let q >= 4 be a power of
two, let Q be a field of order q, fix any enumeration of Q, and let
n >= 1. Put d_0 = (q - 2) / 2. Let S be the "index simplex"

      S = S_{d_0}
        = { (a_{i_1}, ..., a_{i_n}) : i_1 + ... + i_n <= d_0 }  subset A = Q^n,

where a_0, a_1, ..., a_{q-1} is the chosen enumeration of Q. Let
N_pt = q^n. Let l be an odd prime, D = l + 1. Suppose O is a subset
of S and F is a family of full affine Q-lines contained in
V = A \ O, with

      (i)   2 N_pt / D^2 <= |O| <= 7 N_pt / D^2;
      (ii)  every point of V lies on D members of F;
      (iii) the incidence graph of V and F has girth at least 12.

If K is a finite extension of Q containing a primitive l-th root of
unity zeta, then, with m = |O| and t = |V|, there are columns
x_v in K^m for v in V such that

      0 < m < t <= m * l^2,
      sum_{v in V} x_v x_v^T = -I_m,
      sum_{v in f} x_v x_v^T = 0    for every f in F.

So: from a small set O of "holes" inside the finite affine space A,
and a family of full lines F that avoids O and hits every other
point exactly D times, one produces the vectors required by
Theorem 2.1. The vectors come from interpolation: the indicator
polynomials of the holes.

Theorem 4.1 (Regular affine-line hypergraph exists). For all
sufficiently large h >= 2, for every finite field Q of order q = 2^h
and every enumeration of Q, there exist:

  - a set O subset S (S as in Proposition 3.3) of "holes", and
  - a set F of full affine Q-lines, contained in V = A \ O,

such that

      2 N_pt / D^2 <= |O| <= 7 N_pt / D^2,
      every point of V lies on exactly D members of F,
      the bipartite incidence graph of V and F has girth at least 12,

where l is the least prime congruent to -1 modulo q^100, D = l + 1,
s = floor(log_q D), b = s + 20, n = 10*b, and N_pt = q^n.

Theorem 4.1 is the "existence of the geometric data" that
Proposition 3.3 assumes. The proof uses random subspaces and a
Lovasz Local Lemma argument to control short incidence cycles
(triangles, rectangles, pentagons): the "girth at least 12" is
achieved by construction, not hypothesis.

Corollary 6.3 (Not surjunctive). For the field K, group G, and
outputs a_out, b_out of Theorem 1.1, the cellular automaton

      T_{b_out} : K^G -> K^G

is injective and not surjective. Its image is exactly the fixed set
of T_{b_out a_out} on K^G. In particular, G is not surjunctive.

So the counterexample to Kaplansky's direct finiteness conjecture is
simultaneously a counterexample to Gottschalk's surjunctivity
conjecture for finitely presented G.

Corollary 6.4 (Type F_infinity version). For the same finite field
K, there are a group U of type F_infinity and scalar elements
A, B in K[U] such that

      A B = 1            and            B A != 1.

The group U is nonsofic and non-surjunctive.

"Type F_infinity" means: U admits a classifying CW complex with
finitely many cells in every dimension. It is a homotopical
finiteness condition, stronger than finite presentation. The
corollary comes from a separate embedding theorem in a companion
paper ("A universal group of type F_infinity"), applied to the
finitely presented group G from Theorem 1.1.


PART III. THE SHAPE OF THE PROOF (NO DETAILS)
================================================================================

Here is the whole proof in ten short steps. Each step is indexed
lemma-by-lemma in PART VIII of this companion, and is proved in full
in the original.

Step 1. Choose the geometric data. Pick h >= 2, q = 2^h, and l the
least prime congruent to -1 modulo q^100. Let Q be the field with q
elements, K a finite extension of Q containing a primitive l-th root
of unity zeta, and A = Q^n for n = 10b with b = s + 20 and
s = floor(log_q D) where D = l + 1. Theorem 4.1 supplies a set
O subset S (S = index simplex) of "holes" and a family F of
full affine Q-lines in V = A \ O such that every point of V lies
on exact D lines of F, and the incidence graph has girth >= 12.

Step 2. Produce the vectors x_v. Proposition 3.3 uses interpolation
to build indicator polynomials f_o for each hole o, and sets
x_v = (f_o(v))_{o in O} in K^m (with m = |O|). The two identities
sum_v x_v x_v^T = -I_m and sum_{v in f} x_v x_v^T = 0 follow from
a line-sum argument: a degree-(q-2) polynomial sums to zero over
a full affine Q-line, because in characteristic two the nonconstant
power sums vanish by a "cyclic shift" trick. The contributions
from the removed holes are the identity I_m, and their complement
in A is -I_m (the minus sign costs nothing in char 2, but the
identity (d) can be written either way).

Step 3. Glue the local groups. For each v in V, let K_v^0 = F_l^2
(an additive group). For each f in F, let L_f^0 = F_l. Since every v
lies on exactly D = l + 1 lines, put a bijection between the l + 1
one-dimensional subspaces of K_v^0 and the l + 1 lines meeting v,
and for each v in f use the induced injection iota_{v f} : L_f^0 -> K_v^0
on the matching one-dimensional subspace. Then present a group N
with one generator per nonzero element of L_f^0 (for every line f),
and one defining relation for each local zero sum of the form
s_{f_1 c_1}^{eps_1} ... s_{f_k c_k}^{eps_k} lying inside one K_v^0.
The word "girth >= 12" is what forbids conflicts between different
local copies.

Step 4. Show the local groups embed. Lemma 2.2 says: the girth >= 12
hypothesis forces every theta_v : K_v^0 -> N to be injective. If it
were not, one nonzero s_{f,c} would be trivial in N; represent that
triviality by a finite planar graph with black vertices labelled
by points of V and white vertices labelled by lines of F. Minimality
of the graph, plus the "no two consecutive neighbors with same
label" contraction argument, forces every black vertex degree >= 3
and every white vertex degree >= 2 (one possible degree 1). Euler's
formula for a connected plane graph then contradicts the incidence
girth bound. This is the crux: it is the ONLY place where girth
enters.

Step 5. Averaging produces a rectangular identity. In K[N] put
P_v = (1/kappa) sum_{u in K_v} u for each v. One checks P_v^2 = P_v
(the sum is over exactly kappa = l^2 group elements, so the
convolution square reshuffles them without loss). Define

      U in Mat_{m x t}(K[N]),       T in Mat_{t x m}(K[N])

by U_{-,v} = -kappa x_v P_v and T_{v,-} = P_v x_v^T. Then a short
computation (using the incidence data and the two matrix identities
from Step 2) gives UT = I_m. Also UP = U and PT = T where P is
diag_v(P_v). This is the "rectangular matrix identity" of
Section 02-criterion.

Step 6. HNN extend to get character idempotents. To pack t > m
coordinates into m slots, we need to "twist" copies of the same
subgroup into different characters. Adjoin a cyclic group <z> of
order l, take H = N x <z>, and then form a multiple HNN extension
L with stable letters tau_v, v in V, that conjugate K_v x <z> to
K_* x <z> times a twist by a linear form alpha_v : K_* -> F_l
(where K_* is a fixed copy K_{v_*}). The trick is that H embeds in
L: the HNN base embedding is proved explicitly by counting orbits
of the regular action of sub-subgroups of order l^3 on H.
With z central in L, the element

      e = (1/l) sum_{r=0}^{l-1} zeta^{-r} z^r

is a central idempotent satisfying e z = zeta e. Similarly for each
linear form alpha on K_*,

      Q_alpha = (1/kappa) sum_{u in K_*} zeta^{alpha(u)} u

is an idempotent, with Q_alpha Q_beta = 0 whenever alpha != beta.
Then tau_v e P_v tau_v^{-1} = e Q_{alpha_v}, so twisting by tau_v
changes the character of the average P_v.

Step 7. Pack into square matrices. Assign each v in V a slot
s(v) in {1, ..., m} and a character alpha_v : K_* -> F_l such that
the pairs (s(v), alpha_v) are all distinct. This is possible because
t <= m l^2 (there are exactly m l^2 possible pairs) and m < t
(some slots carry multiple coordinates). Define

      C_{s,v} = tau_v e P_v       if s = s(v),       else 0,
      (J_0)_{v,s} = e P_v tau_v^{-1}   if s = s(v),   else 0.

The orthogonality Q_alpha Q_beta = 0 (alpha != beta) makes the
cross-terms inside any one slot vanish, so J_0 C = e P. Put

      a_mat = U J_0 + (1 - e) I_m,
      b_mat = C T + (1 - e) I_m.

Then a_mat b_mat = I_m. Indeed UJ_0 C T = U (eP) T = e (U P T) =
e (U T) = e I_m, using UP=U, PT=T, and UT=I_m from Step 5; the
(1-e) terms give (1-e)^2 = 1-e and are killed by the e-factors, so
the total is e I_m + (1-e) I_m = I_m. So far, a failure of STABLE
finiteness in Mat_m(K[L]).

Step 8. Detect the reverse defect. Set Delta = b_mat a_mat - I_m.
Direct multiplication gives Delta = C T U J_0 - e I_m and hence

      J_0 Delta C = e (T U - P).

The right-hand side is a matrix over K[H] (H = N x <z>), where a
scalar specialization epsilon : K[H] -> K is available:
epsilon(n z^r) = zeta^r for n in N. Then epsilon(e) = 1,
epsilon(P_v) = 1, epsilon(U) = X and epsilon(T) = X^T where
X in Mat_{m x t}(K) is the matrix with columns x_v. So

      epsilon(e (T U - P)) = X^T X - I_t,

which is NONZERO because t > m: choose y in ker X nonzero, then
(X^T X - I_t) y = -y. Since J_0 Delta C is nonzero in Mat_t(K[H]),
Delta is nonzero in Mat_m(K[L]). So b_mat a_mat != I_m.

Step 9. Matrix pair to scalar pair. Let F = (Z/l)^m semidirect S_m,
a finite group. Let B = (Z/l)^m be the normal subgroup; define
p_i = |B|^{-1} sum_{w in B} zeta^{-w_i} w for i = 1, ..., m. These
are pairwise orthogonal idempotents in K[F] (again the character
orthogonality argument, now over B). Set sigma_1 = 1 and let
sigma_i in S_m be the transposition (1 i); then p_{ij} = sigma_i p_1
sigma_j^{-1} are the m^2 matrix units with p_{ij} p_{kr} = delta_{jk}
p_{ir}. Now let G = L x F (direct product) and view K[L] and K[F]
as commuting subalgebras of K[G]. Define

      f_blk = p_11 + p_22 + ... + p_mm,
      Phi(Z) = sum_{i,j} Z_{ij} p_{ij}  for Z in Mat_m(K[L]).

Phi is injective and multiplicative from Mat_m(K[L]) into K[G]. Set

      a_out = 1 - f_blk + Phi(a_mat),
      b_out = 1 - f_blk + Phi(b_mat).

Then (1 - f_blk) has zero product with anything in the image of Phi,
so a_out b_out = 1 - f_blk + Phi(I_m) = 1. And
b_out a_out - 1 = Phi(Delta) != 0 (by injectivity). This is
Theorem 1.1.

Step 10. Track finite presentation, then take consequences. The
group N is finitely presented (Section 02a, using finitely many
generators and relations of length <= 3). Then H = N x Z/l is
finitely presented; the HNN extension L adds |V| stable letters
and at most |V| l^3 relators; the finite group F has a finite
presentation; so G = L x F is finitely presented. Finite
presentation is the "computable" quality theorem 1.1 claims.

From the scalar pair (a_out, b_out) we get:
  - a nonsofic group G (contradiction with Elek-Szabo stable
    finiteness of sofic group algebras plus direct finiteness in
    matrix size one);
  - an injective-not-surjective cellular automaton T_{b_out} on
    configurations K^G (Corollary 6.3);
  - after embedding G into a group U of type F_infinity (companion
    paper), a type F_infinity counterexample too (Corollary 6.4).


PART IV. GLOSSARY
================================================================================

Every specialized word in the paper, defined briefly. Words are
cross-referenced only where a cross-reference is short.

Group. A set G with an associative product, an identity 1, and
inverses.

Subgroup. A subset closed under product and inverse.

Order of an element. The smallest positive n with g^n = 1, if any;
else infinite.

Torsion (of a group). The set of elements of finite order. "Group
with torsion" means: has some non-identity element of finite order.

Odd-order torsion. Torsion where the finite order is odd.

Finitely presented (group). A group defined by finitely many
generators and finitely many relations.

Field. A commutative ring where every nonzero element has a
multiplicative inverse.

Characteristic p. The smallest positive integer p with
1 + 1 + ... + 1 (p times) = 0; 0 if no such p exists.

Finite field. A field with finitely many elements. Every finite
field has p^r elements for some prime p and some r >= 1; write F_{p^r}.

F_2. The finite field {0, 1} with 1 + 1 = 0. Characteristic two.

Primitive l-th root of unity. An element zeta with zeta^l = 1 and
zeta^k != 1 for 1 <= k < l. It exists in any field K of characteristic
not dividing l, provided l | |K^×|. Here l is prime and K is a finite
extension of F_2 chosen so l | |K^×| = 2^{hr} - 1.

Group ring (group algebra) K[G]. The set of formal finite sums
sum c_g g with c_g in K, under convolution product.

Support. For a in K[G], the set of g with c_g != 0.

Directly finite (ring). a b = 1 implies b a = 1 for all a, b.

Stably finite (ring). Mat_n(R) is directly finite for every n.

Idempotent. An element e with e^2 = e.

Central idempotent. An idempotent commuting with every element of
the ring.

Orthogonal idempotents. Idempotents e, f with e f = f e = 0.

Matrix units. A family p_{ij}, 1 <= i, j <= m, with
p_{ij} p_{kr} = delta_{jk} p_{ir}.

Incidence structure. A pair (V, F) where V is a set and F is a
family of subsets of V (the "members" or "lines").

Bipartite incidence graph. Vertices V union F; edges (v, f) whenever
v in f.

Girth. The length of the shortest cycle in a graph; infinite for a
forest.

Affine line (over F_q). A full translate of a one-dimensional
F_q-subspace of F_q^n; has exactly q points.

Direction. A one-dimensional subspace of F_q^n (not a single point
or vector; the set of all nonzero multiples of any nonzero vector
spans one direction).

Index simplex S_d. The set of tuples (a_{k_1}, ..., a_{k_n}) in F_q^n
with sum of indices k_1 + ... + k_n <= d, where a_0, ..., a_{q-1} is a
fixed enumeration of F_q. Used as the "full set" in the interpolation
argument.

Interpolation on S_d. Every function on S_d is representable by a
unique polynomial of total degree <= d; dimension count basis:
numerator matches the number of monomials of total degree <= d.

Full affine line. An affine line that has all q of its F_q-points
present in the ground set (no holes).

Girth bound requirement. Incidence graph has no 3-, 4-, ..., 11-cycles.
Written "girth >= 12".

HNN extension. A group construction: starting from a group H, two
subgroups A, B, and an isomorphism phi : A -> B, produce a group that
contains H and an extra generator t with t a t^{-1} = phi(a) for a in A.
"Multiple HNN" allows several such (A_v, B_v, phi_v, t_v) tuples.

HNN base embedding. The theorem (Higman-Neumann-Neumann) that H
embeds in the HNN extension it produces, provided A and B are
associated subgroups. Proof is a permutation-action certificate.

Stable letter. In an HNN extension, the "extra generators" t_v that
do not lie in the base group.

Sofic group. Defined in PART I (section 8). Formally: a group G
admits, for every finite E subset G and every eps > 0, a finite Omega
and a map sigma : G -> Perm(Omega) that is an "eps-homomorphism" and
"eps-faithful" on E.

Surjunctive group. Every injective cellular automaton over every
finite alphabet is surjective.

Cellular automaton. A map on configurations x : G -> A defined by a
finite-memory local rule phi : A^M -> A. Formally:
tau(x)(g) = phi( (x(g u))_{u in M} ).

Type F_infinity. A group U that acts freely, properly, and cellularly
on a CW complex with finitely many cells in every dimension.

kappa. The symbol kappa in the paper always means l^2 (with l the odd
prime from Theorem 1.1). It is the number of elements in K_v = F_l^2.

Transpose. For a matrix X, X^T denotes the transpose; there is no
"conjugate-transpose" (no complex conjugation, and no involution on K).

Rank. The rank of X in Mat_{m x t}(K) is the dimension of its column
space. "rank <= m" whenever X has only m rows.

Five named theorems of the wider literature.

  Linnik's theorem. For fixed coprime a, m the least prime congruent
  to a mod m is <= C m^L for absolute constants C, L > 0.
  Xylouris (2011) gives explicit L = 5.2, or L = 5.18 on GRH.

  Lovasz local lemma (LLL). Given a finite set of "bad events" A_i
  determined by a finite set of independent random variables, with
  dependency graph where events are independent of all their
  non-neighbors; if the sum of event probabilities at every variable
  is < 1/16 (in the asymmetric form used here), there is positive
  probability that NO bad event occurs.

  Markov's inequality. If X is a nonnegative random variable,
  Pr[X >= t] <= E[X] / t.

  Chebyshev's inequality. Pr[|X - E[X]| >= t] <= Var(X) / t^2.

  Vandermonde. The matrix M_{ij} = xi_j^{i-1} (1 <= i, j <= k) for
  distinct xi_1, ..., xi_k is invertible; determinant
  prod_{i < j} (xi_j - xi_i). Used to select "10-wise independent"
  subspaces.


PART V. THE ABSTRACT AND THE CONVENTIONS OF THE PAPER
================================================================================

The abstract of the original paper, quoted verbatim:

  "We disprove Kaplansky's direct-finiteness conjecture by
   constructing a finite field K of characteristic two, a finitely
   presented group G, and finite sums a, b in K[G] with ab = 1 but
   ba != 1. The group G is nonsofic. The same elements define a
   cellular automaton on K^G that is injective but not surjective,
   disproving Gottschalk's surjunctivity conjecture."

The conventions of the paper, quoted verbatim:

  "All incidence structures in the proof are finite. Their incidence
   graphs have one vertex for each point and one for each member of
   the family, with an edge for each membership. The girth is the
   length of a shortest cycle, and is infinite for a forest. An
   affine Q-line is a translate of a one-dimensional Q-subspace. A
   direction is such a linear subspace, not a chosen nonzero vector.
   Superscript T denotes transpose, without any involution on the
   coefficient field. All group presentations define abstract
   discrete groups."

Two things worth emphasizing from those conventions:

  1. All incidence structures (V, F) are FINITE. This is why every
     "for every v" or "every f" is a finite for-all, and why the
     polynomial interpolation arguments are exact rather than
     limiting.

  2. There is NO complex conjugation anywhere in the paper. X^T is
     the plain transpose. Whenever you see X^T X it means real
     transpose-times-X, not conjugate-transpose; the field K has no
     notion of conjugate-transpose in general.

PART VI. WHAT A CURIOUS READER CAN ACTUALLY COMPUTE
================================================================================

The mathematics of the paper is genuinely beyond high school. But
several SECTIONS OF IT are not: they are finite calculations you can
do by hand with a pencil, or on a computer in a short script. Below
are six small exercises, each roughly 30-60 minutes of careful work
for a determined reader. Together they touch the "nerve" of the
argument.

Exercise 1 (Direct finiteness of finite matrices). Let A, B be
N-by-N matrices over any field. Prove A B = I implies B A = I.
Hint: think of A as a linear map K^N -> K^N. AB = I means A is
surjective (B is a section). A surjective endomorphism of a
finite-dimensional vector space is invertible (rank-nullity). So A
has a two-sided inverse; multiply A B = I on the left by A^-1.

Contrast: over the ring of infinite-by-infinite column-finite
matrices (each column has finitely many nonzero entries), the shift
matrix S on the standard basis e_0, e_1, e_2, ... with S e_i =
e_{i+1} satisfies L S = I but S L != I, where L is the left-shift.
This is the exact phenomenon the paper constructs at the level of a
group algebra in characteristic two.

Exercise 2 (Multiply in F_2[S_3]). Take G = S_3 (the six
permutations of {1, 2, 3}; label them e, (12), (13), (23), (123),
(132)) and K = F_2. Consider

      a = e + (12),       b = e + (123).

Compute ab. Compute ba. Verify ab != ba, i.e. F_2[S_3] itself is
non-commutative. Then verify ab != 1 and ba != 1 (easy since the
unit 1 has coefficient 1 on e and 0 elsewhere; check that both a b
and b a have more than one nonzero coefficient).

Exercise 3 (The idempotent e in F_4[Z/3]). Take l = 3, K = F_4
(the four-element field, computed as F_2[omega]/(omega^2 + omega +
1)), and G = Z/3 = {1, z, z^2} with z^3 = 1. Let zeta = omega (a
primitive cube root of unity inside F_4) and define

      e = (1/3) (1 + omega^{-1} z + omega^{-2} z^2) in F_4[G].

Since 3 != 0 in F_4 (characteristic two), 1/3 makes sense.
Compute e^2 and verify directly that e^2 = e. Compute e z and
verify e z = omega e. Compute z e and verify z e = omega e as well
(so e is central in F_4[Z/3]).

The idempotent e is a "balanced average over the 3-cycle" and the
whole construction uses it as a projection onto one isotypic
component.

Exercise 4 (The line-sum lemma by hand). Let F_q be a finite field
with q = 2^h and q > 2. Verify these two identities:

      (a) sum_{x in F_q} x                     = 0;
      (b) sum_{x in F_q} x^k                   = 0 for 1 <= k < q-1.

Hint for (b): F_q^x (nonzero elements) is a cyclic group of order
q - 1. Let g generate it. Then sum_{x in F_q} x^k = sum_{j=0}^{q-2} g^{jk}.
If k is not divisible by q - 1, there is a g^j with (g^j)^k != 1;
multiplying the whole sum by that g^{jk} permutes the terms, so the
sum must satisfy s = g^{jk} s, i.e., s (g^{jk} - 1) = 0, i.e., s = 0.

Two values sit right at the boundary:

      (c) sum_{x in F_q} 1          = q = 0  (trivially zero since
                                              q = 0 in F_q);
      (d) sum_{x in F_q} x^{q-1}    = 1      (nonzero: for x != 0,
                                              x^{q-1} = 1, and x = 0
                                              contributes 0; total
                                              q - 1 = 1 in char 2).

So the power sums vanish exactly for degrees 1 through q-2. At
degree q-1 the sum is 1; at degree 0 it is 0. This is why the
interpolation degree must stay at most d_0 = (q-2)/2: products of
two interpolants have degree <= q-2 and therefore vanish under line
sum.

Exercise 5 (Why the exponent l needs to be ODD). Try the "degenerate
case" l = 2. The group of order 2 is Z/2 = {1, z} with z^2 = 1.
The "balanced average over the 2-cycle" would be

      e_2 = (1/2) (1 + zeta z),

where zeta is a primitive 2nd root of unity. In characteristic two,
1/2 = 0 in F_q, so e_2 = 0. So no average exists at all.

Even if you work in any field where 1/2 is a proper fraction, the
"character slots" of Z/2 fail: Z/2 has exactly TWO characters (trivial
and the sign character), so there is no family of orthogonal
idempotents indexed by linear forms. The construction of Theorem 2.1
needs

      Q_alpha Q_beta = 0  when alpha != beta,

which is only true when there are at least l > 2 characters, i.e.
l > 2 odd. With l = 2 you get just one nontrivial character, hence
no orthogonality slots.

So the exponent l being odd is essential: it is what makes the
idempotent average well-defined (1/l) AND gives enough characters
to pack coordinates orthogonally. Exercise 3 shows l = 3 in F_4;
try the analogous computation with l = 5 in F_16 to see the pattern.

Exercise 6 (Why a toy CA on Z cannot be injective-not-surjective).
Take G = Z and alphabet A = F_2. Consider a linear CA given by a
finite sum

      tau(x)(n) = sum_{u} c_u x(n + u),

where c_u in F_2, only finitely many nonzero. That is: tau = T_c in
the notation of PART VI of the original, i.e., T_c(x)(g) =
sum_u c_u x(g u). Note right-multiplication: g goes to g u.

(a) Suppose c has at least two nonzero coefficients. Show that tau
has a nonzero kernel, so tau is NOT injective.

Concrete example: take c = (1 at u=0) + (1 at u=1), i.e.,
tau(x)(n) = x(n) + x(n+1). Then the "all-ones" configuration
x(n) = 1 for every n satisfies tau(x)(n) = 1 + 1 = 0. So x is a
nonzero element of ker(tau): tau is not injective.

Another: tau(x)(n) = x(n) + x(n+2). The "alternating" configuration
x(n) = n mod 2 satisfies x(n) + x(n+2) = (n mod 2) + (n mod 2) = 0
in F_2, so again tau has a nonzero kernel.

(b) Suppose c has exactly ONE nonzero coefficient, say c at u = u_0.
Then tau(x)(n) = x(n + u_0), a pure shift. This is bijective; it is
both injective and surjective.

So NO linear CA on Z is injective-but-not-surjective. Over Z, the
phenomenon is impossible. This is a feature of Z, not a defect of
the definitions.

The deeper reason: for a in F_2[Z] = F_2[x, x^{-1}], the operator
T_a on F_2^Z is injective iff a is a monomial. Multi-term a always
has nonzero kernel on all configurations. So the paper HAS to find
a more exotic group.

Corollary 6.3 does exactly that: it constructs a specific finitely
presented group G and a specific a in K[G] giving T_a injective but
not surjective. The exercise shows that no amount of cleverness with
Z alone can produce Kaplansky's counterexample at the CA level. That
is why the paper's group is intricate. The point of the group is not
"it is big" or "it is fancy"; it is "it has the right subgroup
structure" (specifically, subgroups K_v = F_l^2 and L_f = F_l
glued according to high-girth incidence) to make the algebraic
construction of Theorem 2.1 fire.


PART VII. WHY THIS MATTERS
================================================================================

1. It is one of Kaplansky's 1970 conjectures. The direct-finiteness
conjecture is one of several "Kaplansky conjectures" stated that
year. The others include:

  - Zero-divisor conjecture. In K[G] with K a field of characteristic
    zero and G torsion-free, if ab = 0 then a = 0 or b = 0. Still
    open.
  - Idempotent conjecture. In K[G] with K a field of characteristic
    zero, if e^2 = e then e = 0 or e = 1 (that is, K[G] has no
    "exotic" idempotents). Still open.
  - Direct finiteness conjecture. The subject of this paper. FALSE
    in characteristic two (this paper); open in characteristic zero.

Direct finiteness was the mildest of the three: it only required
studying one-sided inverses of elements, not of arbitrary products
(which enter zero-divisors) or idempotents. It still took until
2026 to find a counterexample.

2. What was already known before 2026. In characteristic zero,
Kaplansky himself observed that C[G] is directly finite, because
the operator-norm completion of C[G] is finite (it is C[G] sitting in
the reduced C*-algebra) and the trace of the coefficient of the
identity element gives a functional that blocks one-sided inverses.
Montgomery (1969) extended this to every field of characteristic
zero, using the identity-coefficient trace on the operator-norm
closure of the regular representation; her argument extends to
every finite matrix size, so it gives stable finiteness as well.

In positive characteristic this method fails (there is no trace in
characteristic p). The known positive results fall into two
families:

  - Ara, OMeara, Perera (2002): K[G] is stably finite for
    free-by-amenable G, over any division ring K.
  - Elek-Szabo (2004): K[G] is stably finite for sofic G, over any
    division ring K.

Both results say: if G belongs to a certain class, then K[G] is
directly finite regardless of characteristic. The paper breaks this
by producing a finitely presented group G outside both classes for
which K[G] FAILS direct finiteness in characteristic two.

3. Consequences for soficity. The nonsoficity of the constructed
group G follows logically: if G were sofic, Elek-Szabo would make
K[G] stably finite, hence directly finite, contradicting the
counterexample. So G is nonsofic.

What makes this a step forward: until 2026, no finitely presented
nonsofic group was known. Nonsofic groups existed (e.g., as
"expanders" in a different context), but their finite presentation
was not established. The paper produces a finitely presented
nonsofic group explicitly, as a byproduct of a different algebraic
construction.

4. Consequences for surjunctivity. Every element A in K[G] defines a
cellular automaton T_A on K^G. If A B = 1 in K[G], then T_A is a
left inverse of T_B, so T_B is injective. If A B = 1 but B A != 1,
then T_B has a left inverse but not a right inverse, so T_B is not
surjective (the image misses the configuration with delta_0-like
structure at a nonzero coefficient of BA - 1).

So the same a_out, b_out that defeats direct finiteness gives an
injective-not-surjective cellular automaton. Gottschalk's conjecture
says every group is surjunctive, and G is a counterexample.

5. Why characteristic two specifically. The paper's characteristic-2
requirement is not a matter of taste. Two scalar facts drive it:

  - Odd prime order l in G + primitive l-th root of unity in K +
    1/l invertible in K requires char K to be coprime to l. With
    char K = 2 and l odd, this is satisfied.
  - The line-sum identity sum_v x_v x_v^T = 0 for each line v uses
    "the sum of a degree-(q-2) polynomial over an affine F_q-line
    is zero", which is true in characteristic two because the
    nonconstant power sums vanish. In other characteristics you
    would get different identities (e.g., for char p, "the sum
    of x^k over F_q is zero for 1 <= k <= q-2 with k not divisible
    by q-1"; in char 2 you get the whole range you want because
    q-1 is odd).

6. Some concrete open questions this result raises.

  - Does the direct finiteness conjecture fail in other positive
    characteristics?
  - Does it fail with F_2 itself (not a finite extension) in place
    of K?
  - Can the odd-torsion requirement be removed? The construction
    crucially uses odd torsion; a char-2 counterexample using only
    2-torsion is not currently known.
  - Can the "nonsofic group" be simplified to a nicer presentation
    with fewer generators and relations?

7. What a high school reader should take away.

  - Infinite-dimensional linear algebra really is different from
    finite-dimensional linear algebra. A one-sided inverse of a
    linear endomorphism in infinite dimensions need not be two-
    sided. This phenomenon is why group algebras matter.
  - Some simple-seeming questions ("if ab = 1 in K[G], is ba = 1?")
    can be open for decades, then resolved by combining several
    areas: combinatorics (hypergraphs), group theory (HNN
    extensions), commutative algebra (finite fields, interpolation),
    and probability (Lovasz Local Lemma).
  - The resolution is a CONSTRUCTION, not an argument by
    contradiction or by category-theoretic abstraction. You can, in
    principle, follow it step by step and verify each formula
    yourself.

Section 01-introduction
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Theorem (main). There exist a finite field K of characteristic two, a
finitely presented group G containing an element of odd prime order,
and elements a_out, b_out in K[G] such that

      a_out b_out = 1,          b_out a_out != 1.

The field, group, and finite sums are specified by a terminating
prescription involving finite sets and finite-field arithmetic.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Section 02-criterion
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Theorem (incidence criterion). Let l be an odd prime, let K be a
finite field of characteristic two containing a primitive l-th root
of unity zeta, and put kappa = l^2. Let V be a finite set of
cardinality t, and let F be a finite family of nonempty subsets of V.
Assume that every v in V belongs to exactly l + 1 members of F and
that the bipartite incidence graph of V and F has girth at least 12.

Suppose that an integer m and column vectors x_v in K^m, v in V,
satisfy

      0 < m < t <= m kappa,                              (1) size

and

      sum_{v in V} x_v x_v^T = -I_m,                     (2) total
      sum_{v in f} x_v x_v^T = 0,   for every f in F.    (3) per line

Then there are a finitely presented group G containing an element of
order l and finite sums a_out, b_out in K[G] such that

      a_out b_out = 1,          b_out a_out != 1.

They are given by the constructions and formulas of section 02-criterion.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Section 02a-embedding (part of 02-criterion)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lemma (embedding). Let (V, F) be a finite incidence structure with
girth at least 12, with associated groups K_v^0 = F_l^2 and
L_f^0 = F_l, and with glued group N presented by the local zero-sum
relations of section 02a. Then every canonical homomorphism
theta_v : K_v^0 -> N is injective.

Equivalently: for every nonzero c in some L_f^0, the generator
s_{f, c} is not the identity element of N.

Section 03-pairing
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lemma (interpolation on an index simplex). Let Q be a field of
order q, enumerate its elements as a_0, a_1, ..., a_{q-1}, and let
0 <= d < q. For n >= 1, set

      S_d = { (a_{k_1}, ..., a_{k_n}) : k_i >= 0, k_1 + ... + k_n <= d }  subset  Q^n.

Evaluation on S_d is an isomorphism from the Q-vector space of
polynomials of total degree at most d to Q^{S_d}.

Lemma (line sums). Let n >= 1. If P is a polynomial on Q^n of
total degree at most q - 2, then its sum on every full affine Q-line
is zero. Its sum on Q^n is also zero.

Proposition (the pairing supplied by holes). Let q >= 4 be a power of
two, Q a field of order q, fix an enumeration of Q, let n >= 1, and
put d_0 = (q - 2) / 2, S = S_{d_0} subset A = Q^n, N_pt = q^n. Let l
be an odd prime and D = l + 1. Suppose that O subset S and that F is
a family of full affine Q-lines contained in V = A \ O, with

      2 N_pt / D^2 <= |O| <= 7 N_pt / D^2,                       (hole size)

Suppose also that every point of V lies on D members of F and that
the incidence graph has girth at least 12. If K is a finite extension
of Q containing a primitive l-th root of unity, then, with m = |O|
and t = |V|, there are columns x_v in K^m, v in V, such that

      0 < m < t <= m l^2,
      sum_{v in V} x_v x_v^T = -I_m,
      sum_{v in f} x_v x_v^T = 0   for every f in F.

Remark (compatibility of line sums with the total). The simultaneous
identities "sum_v x_v x_v^T = -I_m" and "sum_{v in f} x_v x_v^T = 0
for all f in F" are compatible with regularity because D = l + 1 is
zero as a scalar in characteristic two. Summing the per-line identities
would ordinarily multiply the total point sum by D and therefore
recover the total; here it multiplies by zero and recovers nothing.
The two identities are genuinely independent.

Section 04-hypergraph
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The setup in section 04: q = 2^h, l is the least prime congruent to -1
modulo q^100, D = l + 1, s = floor(log_q D), b = s + 20, n = 10b,
N_pt = q^n, A = Q^n where Q is a field of order q, S is the index
simplex S_{d_0} with d_0 = (q - 2) / 2 (all tuples in Q^n of total
index <= d_0 in the fixed enumeration of Q). Lines always mean full
affine Q-lines with exactly q points.

Theorem (hypergraph). For all sufficiently large h, for every Q and
enumeration as above, there are a set O subset S and a set F of
affine lines contained in V = A \ O such that

      2 N_pt / D^2 <= |O| <= 7 N_pt / D^2,
      every point of V lies on exactly D members of F,
      the bipartite incidence graph of V and F has girth at least 12.

Lemma (direction spaces). There are two-dimensional subspaces
W_r subset A, one per bundle, and (b - 1)-dimensional subspaces
E_j subset A, one per index, such that any at most ten distinct
members of this list have direct sum. Choose a direction e_r in each
W_r, and assign its other q directions bijectively to the indices in
that bundle, calling them d_j. Then U_j = d_j (+) E_j has dimension
b, and

      U_j ∩ U_{j'} = 0            (j != j'),
      U_j ∩ e_r = 0,
      U_j ∩ W_r  = d_j            if r = rho(j),
                  0              if r != rho(j).

Here rho(j) denotes the bundle containing index j.

Lemma (compatible holes). For all sufficiently large h, there is
O subset S satisfying the hole-size bound and the following properties:

  (i)   Every translate of every W_r contains at most one point of O.
  (ii)  For every j, every coset C of U_j, and every r,
        |O ∩ (C + W_r)| <= 100.
  (iii) No exceptional rectangle has four distinct witnesses in O,
        one for each of its four side lines.

An "exceptional rectangle" has corners x, x+u, x+u+v, x+v with
0 != u in e_r, 0 != v in e_{r'}, and r != r'; a "witness" for a side
is a point off that side but in the relevant translate of W_r or W_{r'}.

Lemma (replacement lines). Let T_r = union_{o in O} (o + W_r). The
"replacement lines of label R_r" are all the lines of direction e_r
in each o + W_r, except o + e_r itself. Then:

      - the replacement lines avoid O;
      - they are pairwise disjoint within each label R_r;
      - no exceptional rectangle has all four of its side lines
        among these replacement lines.

Lemma (polygons). Every candidate sequence with a generic step has
one of the following forms:

  (i)   A rectangle alternating G_j, R_r, with opposite vectors
        paired.
  (ii)  A rectangle alternating distinct G_j, G_{j'}, with opposite
        vectors paired.
  (iii) A pentagon containing two G_j steps, two G_{j'} steps, and
        one R_r step, with rho(j) = rho(j') = r.

If g is the number of distinct generic labels, the corresponding bad
event uses 2g distinct random variables and has probability p_*^{2g}
where p_* = (q^2 - 1)/(q^{b-1} - 1). In case (iii), the exceptional
vector and one vector of each generic label determine the other two
vectors once their order is fixed.

Lemma (event bounds). Let mu be the largest, over all variables
P_{j,C}, of the sum of probabilities of bad events using that
variable. Then mu = O(q^{-7}), uniformly in the variable; and every
bad event uses at most four distinct variables.

Lemma (local lemma). Let a finite collection of events be determined
by independent random variables, each event assigned a nonempty set
of at most four variables that determine it. If the sum of event
probabilities at every variable is at most 1/16, there is positive
probability that no event occurs.

Lemma (slab partition). Let a three-dimensional affine space have
direction P (+) d, where dim_Q P = 2 and dim_Q d = 1. If it has no
designated point, it has a line partition whose directions project
nontrivially to P. If it has a designated point o, it has a line
partition containing o + d, with every other line's direction
projecting nontrivially to P.

Lemma (trade). For every hole o and bundle r, remove the q lines
o + d_j with rho(j) = r, and insert the q - 1 replacement lines of
label R_r in o + W_r. After all these operations, the resulting set
F consists of lines in V, and every point of V has degree D. The
surviving lines of each preliminary partition are pairwise disjoint,
as are the replacement lines within each label. There are no
duplicate lines across different labels.

Section 05-prescription
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section of the original has no named theorem, lemma, proposition,
corollary, or remark. It is the finite search that fixes every choice
made in the construction (the field K, the enumeration of Q, the holes
O, the line family F, the slot assignments s(v), the linear forms
alpha_v), proves that the search terminates by appealing to the
sufficiently-large-h existence theorem from section 04, and assembles
the assembly into Theorem 1.1.

The two elements a_out and b_out are displayed explicitly there as
finite sums:

      a_out = 1 - sum_{i=1}^m p_{ii}
               + sum_{i,j=1}^m (a_mat)_{ij} * p_{ij},
      b_out = 1 - sum_{i=1}^m p_{ii}
               + sum_{i,j=1}^m (b_mat)_{ij} * p_{ij},

where a_mat, b_mat are the m-by-m matrices over K[L] defined in
section 02-criterion (packing) and p_{ij} are the matrix units of
section 02-criterion (finite group F = (Z/l)^m semidirect S_m). The
displayed expressions are finite sums of finite sums of group words
with coefficients in K.

Section 06-cellular-automata
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The setup in section 06: K any field (later finite), G any group,
K[G] the group ring. For c = sum_{u in G} c_u u in K[G], the support
is supp(c) = {u in G : c_u != 0}. Define for x in K^G the map

      (T_c x)(g) = sum_{u in G} c_u x(g u).

The sum is finite even when x has infinite support, because c has
finite support.

Lemma (CA representation). For every field K and group G, the
assignment c |-> T_c is a faithful unital K-algebra homomorphism from
K[G] to the algebra of K-linear endomorphisms of K^G, with composition
as multiplication. In particular,

      T_c ∘ T_d = T_{cd},          T_1 = id_{K^G}.

Proposition (CA transfer). Let K be a finite field, G a group, and
a, b in K[G] satisfy a b = 1. Put M = supp(b) and define

      phi : K^M -> K,          phi(p) = sum_{u in M} b_u p(u).

The cellular automaton tau = T_b is injective, and

      im(tau) = { y in K^G : T_{ba} y = y }.

If b a != 1, every h in G with (ba - 1)_h != 0 gives a configuration
delta_h not in im(tau); hence tau is not surjective.

Corollary (not surjunctive). For the field K, group G, and outputs
of the main theorem, the cellular automaton

      T_{b_out} : K^G -> K^G

is injective and not surjective. Its image is the fixed set of
T_{b_out a_out}. In particular, G is not surjunctive.

Corollary (type F_infinity). For the finite characteristic-two field
K of the main theorem, there are a group U of type F_infinity and
scalar elements A, B in K[U] such that

      A B = 1,          B A != 1.

The group U is nonsofic and nonsurjunctive.
