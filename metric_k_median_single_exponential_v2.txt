  ============================================================
               METRIC K-MEDIAN EXPLAINED
       
     "Single-exponential recovery and bounded-price
      strictness for metric k-median"
  ============================================================

  

  PART 1. THE K-MEDIAN PROBLEM, FROM SCRATCH
  ============================================================

  Imagine you run a pizza chain. You have a map, and on
  that map some specific points are houses that want
  pizza delivered. Call these points the "clients". You
  can build kitchens on any point on the map. Call those
  candidate points the "facilities". The distances
  between points come from the map, so they obey the
  usual triangle inequality:

    for any three points a, b, c,
    dist(a, c) <= dist(a, b) + dist(b, c).

  A collection of points with these properties is
  called a "metric space". If the distances come from
  coordinates in the usual Euclidean sense, it is a
  "Euclidean metric". The general problem assumes only
  the triangle inequality.

  The k-median problem, in plain words:

    Pick at most k facilities so as to minimize the sum
    over all clients of the distance from each client to
    its nearest chosen facility.

  In math symbols: if F is the set of facilities and D
  is the set of clients, for a chosen subset S of F with
  |S| <= k, define

      cost(S) = sum_{p in D} d(p, S),

  where d(p, S) means the smallest distance from p to any
  point in S. We want S minimizing cost(S). The minimum
  over all valid S is written opt_k.

  Why this specific cost function?

    - Sum of distances describes average delivery time.
    - It also describes "place k cell towers to minimize
      total signal lag".
    - It is the simplest clustering model where you pay
      for distance from every point, not for the worst.

  Clustering with "worst case" distance instead of "sum"
  is a different problem, k-center, a cousin. Clustering
  with "squared" distances is k-means, another cousin.
  k-median is the plain sum-of-distances one.

  Why is it hard?

  Finding opt_k exactly is NP-hard. That means: unless
  P = NP (which basically no one believes), no
  polynomial-time algorithm can find the exact minimum
  on every input. So the question becomes: how close to
  opt_k can a polynomial-time algorithm get?

  That is what an "approximation ratio" measures.

  ============================================================
  PART 2. APPROXIMATION RATIOS
  ============================================================

  An algorithm A for k-median is a rho-approximation
  (read "rho-approximation") if, for every input, the
  solution S_A it returns satisfies

      cost(S_A) <= rho * opt_k.

  Smaller rho is better. rho = 1 would be exact, which is
  impossible unless P = NP. So rho > 1 is the game, and
  squeezing rho down toward 1 is the point.

  Deterministic vs randomized.

  A deterministic algorithm always returns the same answer
  on the same input. A randomized algorithm flips coins.
  A randomized rho-approximation means: either the
  expected cost is at most rho * opt_k, or the cost is at
  most rho * opt_k with probability at least 1 - p for
  some small failure probability p. The paper's main
  theorem is probabilistic: with probability at least
  1 - N^(-a) for any fixed a (N = input size), the cost
  is at most (2 - sigma) * opt_k for a tiny positive
  constant sigma. Also the expected cost is at most
  (2 - sigma) * opt_k. Both versions are proven.

  Big-Oh usage.

  You will see "O(log N)", "poly(N)", "exp(O(m))". They
  mean "up to a constant factor that does not depend on
  the input":

      poly(N)     something at most N^c for a fixed c
      O(log N)    something at most c * log(N)
      exp(O(m))   something at most exp(c * m)
      O(m)        something at most c * m

  Whenever you see "small constant" or "tiny constant",
  the paper really does mean positive-but-microscopic.
  That is a running theme: the improvement below 2 is
  provably positive, but the constant is astronomically
  tiny (see Part 6). This is normal in theory research:
  the point is that the factor is strictly below 2, not
  that the improvement is large in practice.

  Probability conventions.

  "With high probability" means with probability at least
  1 - (input size)^(-a) for any fixed a > 0. That is a
  very strong guarantee: failure probability drops faster
  than any inverse power of the input size.

  ============================================================
  PART 3. A SHORT MAP OF THE WHOLE ALGORITHM
  ============================================================

  The algorithm has two halves that are chosen between at
  run time. Because it does not know which half will
  succeed, it runs BOTH, keeps the cheaper answer, and
  the analysis shows the good half works. This is a
  common pattern in approximation algorithms and is
  called a "case distinction" in the analysis, not in
  the code.

  The two halves are chosen based on a quantity called
  "sensitivity":

    Let L be the "logarithmic surplus", a number of
    order log N. Compare opt_{k-2L} to opt_k:

    - If opt_{k-2L} <= (1 + small constant) * opt_k,
      then dropping ~2L centers barely changes the cost.
      Call this LOW SENSITIVITY.

    - Otherwise there is a cardinality h' very close to
      k where opt_{h'-1} >= (1 + small constant) *
      opt_{h'}: dropping the single best center costs
      a lot. Call this HIGH SENSITIVITY.

  In the low-sensitivity case, the algorithm uses a
  "surplus construction" from a separate paper
  (Cohen-Addad-Grandoni-Lee-Schwiegelshohn-Svensson 2026,
  abbreviated CGLSS) to get close to 2, and then the
  strict-improvement tool (bounded-price strictness) to
  go strictly below 2.

  In the high-sensitivity case, the algorithm runs an
  "anchoring plus recovery" procedure. The high
  sensitivity guarantees that a specific local-search
  solution has only a logarithmic number of "bad"
  clusters relative to the true optimum. Recovery then
  finds a good solution from that local-search anchor in
  time exponential only in the number of bad clusters,
  which is logarithmic, so the running time is
  polynomial.

  Both halves compute feasible solutions of size at most
  k, and the algorithm returns the cheaper of the two.
  Both halves are safe: even in a wildly unlucky outcome
  the worst-case guarantee is a factor-5 approximation,
  which keeps the expected cost bounded by 2. So the
  probabilistic guarantees are on top of a hard cap,
  not on top of a possible explosion.

  ============================================================
  PART 4. TOOL 1: RECOVERY FROM AN ANCHOR
  ============================================================

  4.1 THE SETTING

  Recovery starts from a candidate solution S, of size
  h, together with a promise that S roughly matches the
  true optimum O, also of size h. Under the promise, it
  produces a solution of cost close to cost(O), in time
  exponential only in the number of "bad matches".

  Notation used throughout the paper:

    D       = set of clients
    F       = set of facilities
    d(x, y) = distance from x to y
    n       = |D|
    N       = n + |F| = input size
    h       = size of the comparison solution O
              (think of h as the desired budget k)

  Given O, assign every client to its nearest center of
  O, breaking ties by a fixed rule. Write:

    C_i = the clients assigned to i in O
    n_i = |C_i|                          (may be 0)
    P_i = sum_{p in C_i} d(p, i)
    P   = sum_{i in O} P_i = cost(O)

  A "good" center of O is one whose cluster can be
  matched to a distinct facility phi_i in S with phi_i
  close to i. The facilities phi_i are called proxies.
  The proxy error is the cost of serving each good
  client through its proxy instead of through its true
  comparison center:

    proxy error = sum over good i
                  sum over p in C_i
                  d(p, phi_i).

  The recovery promise says: the proxy error is at most

    P_g + mu * P

  where P_g = sum of P_i over good centers, mu is a
  small fixed positive constant (for example 1/100), and
  m, the number of bad centers, is at most a given
  bound M_0.

  In words: the good centers already have near-perfect
  proxies inside S. Only m bad centers are "off". The
  promise gives us a handle on how bad the anchor is.

  Very important: the algorithm is NOT given O, P, the
  good or bad labels, the proxies, or S_0 = S minus the
  proxies. Those exist by assumption and are used only
  in the analysis. The algorithm sees only S, mu, M_0,
  and a failure parameter zeta in the open interval
  (0, 1).

  4.2 WHAT RECOVERY PROVES

  Two closely related statements.

  (A) The concrete version (Theorem comp:recovery of
      the paper):

    Assume m <= M_0 and the proxy error is at most
    P_g + P/100. Then in time

      exp(O(M_0)) * poly(N) * log(2/zeta)

    an algorithm returns S_hat with

      |S_hat| <= h       and
      cost(S_hat) <= 1.97 * P

    with probability at least 1 - zeta.

  (B) The clean version (Theorem ref:recovery):

    Let eps > 0 be fixed, choose mu <= min(1/4, eps/12),
    and assume m <= L_0 * log N for some fixed L_0. Then
    in polynomial time an algorithm returns S_hat with

      |S_hat| <= h       and
      cost(S_hat) <= (1 + 2/e + eps) * P

    with probability at least 1 - zeta.

  The exp(O(M_0)) shape is exactly what the phrase
  "single-exponential" in the paper title refers to.
  When M_0 = O(log N), as in version (B), the running
  time becomes N^O(1), i.e. polynomial.

  Both versions hold on normalized bounded-integer
  instances: D and F are disjoint indexed sets, d is a
  metric, and distances between distinct points are
  positive integers bounded by a polynomial in N.
  Section 2 of the paper shows how to reduce a general
  rational metric to this case at a multiplicative cost
  of (1 + eps_0).

  4.3 WHERE 1 + 2/e COMES FROM

  Here e is Euler's number (about 2.718), so 1 + 2/e is
  about 1.736. That is what makes recovery useful: it is
  strictly less than 2.

  The number is not a coincidence. It is dictated by
  the subproblem that the completion step has to solve:
  a monotone submodular maximization with a partition
  constraint. The best approximation known for this
  subproblem is a (1 - 1/e) fraction of the maximum, via
  the continuous-greedy method of Calinescu, Chekuri,
  Pal, and Vondrak (2011).

  Completion also has a knob theta in the interval
  (1/e, 1). Each covered client pays theta times the
  radius of its guessed ball on top of its comparison
  cost. Since the client pays the ball cost twice in
  the accounting (once through the "2I" term, once
  through the "sum c_p" term), the total ball cost
  becomes 2 * theta. Optimizing theta downward gives
  2 * (1/e) = 2/e. Add the base coefficient of 1 for
  the comparison cost, and the total is 1 + 2/e.

  Choose theta slightly above 1/e to buy slack. The
  (1 - 1/e) submodular guarantee pays for it. Nothing
  deeper.

  4.4 THE SAMPLING EXPERIMENT

  The algorithm does not know which centers of O are
  bad, so it looks for them by sampling.

  A "state" H is a reference set of points. Initially H
  is S together with one "permanent dummy" delta_inf at
  distance Dmax from every original point, where Dmax
  is the input diameter. Any path through it has length
  at least 2 Dmax, so it never shortens a real
  distance. Its only job is to keep H nonempty, so
  that d(p, H) is always finite and W(H) is defined.

  The algorithm performs up to 2m steps. At each step:

    Compute W(H) = sum over clients p of d(p, H).

    Sample a client p from D with probability
    d(p, H) / W(H). Clients far from H are chosen
    more often.

    Flip a fair coin: BALL mode or REMOVAL mode.

  BALL mode:

    Guess a radius R from the menu (Section 4.5).
    Build the ball B(p, R) = {x in F : d(p, x) <= R}.
    If B(p, R) is empty, stop the run.
    Otherwise append a "leaf": a virtual point at
    distance R from p. The leaf stands in for the
    comparison center near p that sits inside the ball.

  REMOVAL mode:

    Look up the reference point that currently
    serves p. If it is a surviving S-facility,
    remove it from H. Otherwise (already removed,
    or the server is a dummy), stop the run.

  Every intermediate state H is recorded as a
  candidate "prefix".

  The analytical guide (used for analysis only, not by
  the algorithm). At each state, an oracle that knows
  O can prescribe which mode to use and a class of
  clients to condition on, such that the actual random
  draw lands on that class with probability at least a
  constant rho > 0. If the actual run follows the guide
  for 2m steps, the resulting state is favorable. The
  probability that a run follows the guide is at least

    rho^(2m) * (menu probability loss) = exp(-O(m)).

  So with exp(-O(m)) probability a random run captures
  the bad clusters. That is why the running time is
  exp(O(m)): repeat exp(O(m)) times to make the
  failure probability small.

  Intuition for what the guide does. Whenever a bad
  cluster is still uncovered, it chooses BALL mode and
  a client near it, so the ball captures the cluster.
  Whenever too many clients are still served by
  about-to-be-removed facilities, it chooses REMOVAL
  mode and a client on such a facility, so the room in
  S is freed up. When both progress signals are small,
  the guide stops.

  4.5 THE RADIUS MENU

  Hard part of BALL mode: what radius R to guess.
  Too small, and the ball misses the bad comparison
  center. Too large, and the ball is full of useless
  facilities, costing budget later.

  Solution: pick R at random from a three-part menu,
  each part with probability 1/3.

    (1) FINE. Pick j uniformly in 1..M_kappa, where
        M_kappa = ceil(10 / kappa) + 1, and set
        R = j * kappa * r0(p).

    (2) RELATIVE TO LEAVES. If there is at least one
        leaf, let D_Lambda be the distance from p to
        the nearest leaf. Pick e uniformly in
        0..E_kappa, where E_kappa is the smallest e
        with (1 + kappa)^e >= 10, and set
        R = D_Lambda / (1 + kappa)^e.

    (3) GEOMETRIC TAIL. Let J = 1 + ceil(2m / kappa).
        Pick j in 1..J with probability proportional
        to 2^(-j), and set R = j * kappa * r0(p).

  Here r0(p) is a "computable reference scale"
  depending only on the guessed P, the guessed m, and
  the input metric:

    r0(p) = min R such that
            R * |{q : d(p, q) <= R}| >= P / m.

  It is the radius at which the ball around p covers
  clients totaling a share P/m of the total cost. When
  P and m are guessed correctly, r0(p) is small
  exactly when the cluster is dense.

  Why three menus.

    - If the correct radius r_i for a bad center is
      small relative to r0(p), menu (1) hits it up to
      constant slack.
    - If r_i is huge compared to r0(p), a previous
      leaf is likely already close, and menu (2) sizes
      the new ball relative to that leaf.
    - If neither, the ball is genuinely large, and
      menu (3) is used. Key lemma (sam:radius-budget):
      balls from menu (3) are pairwise disjoint on
      their client sets, so at most O(m) of them can
      arise. This amortizes the exponential tail.

  The upshot: the correct radius is reachable through
  the menu, at total probability exp(-O(m)) along the
  whole trajectory, without any waste.

  4.6 COMPLETION: FROM A PREFIX TO A SOLUTION

  A state H from the sampling process is not yet a
  real solution. It contains some real facilities
  (survivors of S) plus some dummy leaves (guessed
  balls). Completion turns H into a real solution U
  with |U| <= h.

  The accounting. Let Q be the set of S-facilities
  the sampling already removed. Let r = m - |Q| be
  the count of removals still needed. We choose r of
  the remaining S-facilities to remove during
  completion, and we add one facility for each ball
  and one for each uncovered bad center. Then |U| is:

    |S| - |Q|              (current size)
    - r                    (additional removals)
    + (number of balls)    (one per ball)
    + (number of uncovered bad centers)

  With |S| = h, (balls) + (uncovered) = m, and
  r = m - |Q|, this is exactly h. So |U| <= h.

  The optimization. Three choices.

    (a) Which r S-facilities to remove? Color the
        S-facilities green, red, or blue at random
        (probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/4). Green stays.
        Red and blue are removal candidates. Blue
        removals use color-coding (Alon-Yuster-Zwick)
        so that blue centers are all distinct with
        good probability. Red removals are chosen
        greedily to minimize a score.

    (b) Which facility to pick inside each ball?
        Build a submodular "savings" function over
        copies of the ball contents and maximize it
        subject to "at most one facility per ball"
        using continuous greedy.

    (c) Which facility to pick for each uncovered
        bad center? Solve a small exact subset
        dynamic program.

  Continuous greedy gives a (1 - 1/e) approximation
  to (b). The rest is exact. So completion outputs a
  solution U of size at most h whose EXPECTED cost
  satisfies the completion bound in Section 4.7.

  ============================================================

  4.7 THE COMPLETION BOUND

  The completion theorem (Theorem comp:completion in the
  paper) is the quantitative statement that makes
  recovery work. For every theta in the open interval
  (1/e, 1), the completion step outputs a solution U
  with size at most h and expected cost at most

    2 I + sum_{p in J} c_p + sum_{p not in J} b_p,

  where:

    I = sum_{p in J} d(p, H)
      (the "still-unpaid" cost of clients assigned to
       facilities that will be removed)

    J = union over surviving S-facilities s in R*
      = S_0 minus Q
      (the clients assigned in H to those facilities)

    c_p = d(p, phi_i)              if i is good
          d_p* + theta*(r_i + R_i) if i is in A
          d_p*                     if i is in B

    b_p = theta * d(p, H)
          + (1 - theta) * min(d(p, H), d(p, X*))

  Here X* = { (i, i) : i in A } is the "use the true
  comparison center" choice for the covered clusters.

  Reading the bound:

    - The 2I term is the cost of clients that must be
      reassigned after their S-facility is removed.
      The factor 2 accounts for two such reroutings
      in the accounting.

    - The sum over J of c_p charges clients that were
      already assigned to R* for their best plausible
      destination: the proxy distance for good
      clusters, the comparison cost plus theta times
      the ball radius for covered bad clusters, and
      the comparison cost alone for uncovered bad
      clusters.

    - The sum over not-J of b_p charges the other
      clients a convex combination of their current
      reference distance and the best available
      comparison center.

  This bound is what drives the 1.97 P (or
  1+2/e+eps P) guarantee. Combined with Part 5
  (strictness), it is the source of the below-2
  factor.

  4.8 WHY "SINGLE-EXPONENTIAL" MATTERS

  The running time is exp(O(M_0)) * poly(N), with the
  exponent linear in M_0. That "linear in" is what the
  phrase "single-exponential" in the title points at.

  Why it matters. exp(O(M_0)) is polynomial exactly
  when M_0 = O(log N). Higher powers, like exp(M_0^2)
  or 2^(2^M_0), would still be polynomial at
  M_0 = O(log N), but the wasted degree is much larger.
  Single-exponential is the correct scaling that lets
  the hypothesis "logarithmically many bad clusters"
  turn into polynomial running time.

  Where does M_0 = O(log N) come from? From the
  anchoring theorem (Section 4.10). High cardinality
  sensitivity forces many comparison centers to be
  well-separated, which forces at most logarithmically
  many of them to be "bad" relative to the local
  search anchor.

  4.9 THE LOCAL SEARCH ANCHOR

  Where does S come from? From width-5 local search.

  Start from any size-h facility set. Repeatedly try
  every exchange that removes at most 5 facilities and
  adds at most 5 while keeping the size at h. If any
  exchange strictly decreases cost, do it. Stop when
  no such exchange exists.

  Two guarantees matter (both parts of Lemma loc:swap).

  The weak form, always true:

    cost(S) <= 5 * P.

  This is the fallback. Local search never blows up,
  even on adversarial input. It is what makes the
  probability guarantee safe against total failure of
  the sampling step.

  The strong form, the "weighted swap bound":

    S_b + (6/5) S_g <= (17/5) P_b + (6/5) D_g.

  Here S_g is the local search cost of clients
  assigned to good centers, S_b is the same for bad,
  P_b is the comparison cost of bad clusters alone,
  and D_g is the proxy error of good clusters. This
  is the analytic engine that produces the proxy
  promise for good centers from local optimality.

  Width 5 is a trade-off. Larger widths give better
  bounds but slower exchange enumeration. Width 5 is
  what the paper needs.

  4.10 ANCHORING: FROM HIGH SENSITIVITY TO FEW BAD CENTERS

  The anchoring theorem (Theorem loc:anchoring) turns
  a solution S and a comparison set O into a good and
  bad partition with distinct proxies. Its hypothesis
  is a scale condition:

    if
      b * (|S| - h + |{ i in O : n_i * sep_i < b }|)
      <= 10 * P,
    then
      proxy error <= P_g + mu * P,
      bad count   m <= C_mu * P / b.

  Here sep_i = min over j in O, j != i, of d(i, j) is
  the separation of a comparison center from its
  nearest sibling. A center is "short" if
  n_i * sep_i < b: even n_i clients at distance sep_i
  cost more than b.

  The constant C_mu is

    C_mu = 10 + (600 / mu) * 2^(ceil(40 / mu) - 1),

  which is astronomical. At mu = 1/100, the paper's
  C_0 is about

    10 + 60000 * 2^3999.

  That is unimaginably huge: larger than the number
  of atoms in the universe, larger than any real
  quantity. But it is a fixed input-independent
  constant. So polynomial time holds. The degree of
  the polynomial is absurd, but for the theoretical
  argument it does not matter.

  Reading the scale condition. It says the quantity
  (extra facilities in S beyond the proxies) plus
  (short comparison centers), times the scale b, is
  at most 10 P. If b is of order P, this gives very
  few bad centers. If b can only be of order P/log N,
  it gives O(log N) bad centers.

  Where does the log N come from in the paper? From
  the surplus construction (Part 5). If lowering k to
  k - O(log N) barely changes the optimum, then the
  extra L = O(log N) slots in |S| - h give exactly
  the slack to set b = P / log N and still have the
  scale condition hold.

  4.11 THE BOTTOM LINE

  Recovery takes a candidate S (from width-5 local
  search), finds the few bad comparison clusters by
  random sampling with a radius menu, and completes
  the sample to a real solution via submodular
  optimization. In time exponential only in the
  number of bad clusters, it produces a solution of
  cost at most 1 + 2/e + eps times the true optimum,
  or (with the concrete constants of the paper) at
  most 1.97 times.

  ============================================================
  PART 5. TOOL 2: BOUNDED-PRICE STRICTNESS
  ============================================================

  5.1 THE FACILITY LOCATION RELAXATION

  The second tool starts from an LP view of k-median.

  Instead of enumerating which k facilities to open, the
  LP allows opening a fractional amount of them. Every
  open facility i charges a "price" lambda per opening;
  larger lambda means fewer facilities open. This is a
  standard Lagrangian relaxation of the cardinality
  constraint.

  The LP also produces a vector of client budgets
  alpha_p (one per client p in D), which is the
  Lagrangian dual. Every real solution U satisfies the
  "dual comparison" bound

    sum over p of alpha_p  <=  |U| * lambda + 2 * cost(U).

  In words: the sum of client budgets is at most the
  total opening price (|U| facilities times lambda) plus
  twice the connection cost.

  The factor 2 in front of cost(U) is the classical
  bottleneck that makes 2 the natural approximation
  barrier. If we could shave this coefficient below 2
  -- in even a small amount -- we would prove a strict
  improvement below 2. Bounded-price strictness is
  exactly that shave.

  5.2 WHAT STRICTNESS SAYS

  Lemma str:strictness of the paper. For every positive
  real T there is a small positive constant c(T) such
  that, for any subset C of clients with positive cost

    P_C = sum_{p in C} d(i, p)

  from a fixed facility i, if

    lambda <= T * P_C,                       (*)

  then

    sum_{p in C} alpha_p <= lambda + (2 - c(T)/2) * P_C.

  In words: whenever the price paid per opening is
  bounded by T times the connection cost of the cluster,
  the classical "2 P_C" coefficient can be replaced by
  "(2 - c(T)/2) P_C".

  The constant c(T) is explicit in the paper:

    B = T + 2
    rho = 1 / (200 * B)
    c(T) = rho^2 / 100.

  It is tiny: rho is doubly small in T. But it is
  positive, which is all the main theorem needs.

  Strictness does not require the algorithm to know the
  true optimum, and it does not require the price to
  satisfy (*) for every cluster. Both of these are what
  make it usable in the surplus construction (Part 6).

  5.3 THE REMOVAL ORDER

  The proof needs one extra notion: the removal order
  of clients.

  In the surplus construction (from the CGLSS paper),
  the algorithm runs a sequence of valid facility
  openings and phase updates. Every client eventually
  becomes inactive at some step. Order the clients by
  their inactivation step, breaking ties by decreasing
  final budget alpha_p. That order is the "removal
  order".

  The proof measures client costs in this order. This
  is purely for the analysis: the algorithm itself
  does not try to discover the removal order. It
  reorganizes the SAME set of client budgets into a
  convenient order to prove the strictness inequality.

  5.4 WHY IT IS TRUE: TWO COMPLEMENTARY SLACK BOUNDS

  Put q = |C| and d_p = d(i, p). Normalise by P_C so
  that sum_{p in C} d_p = 1. For client p, let z_p be
  the phase value just before p becomes inactive, and
  put a_p = z_p / K. Define the deficit

    Delta = lambda + 2 - sum_{p in C} a_p.

  We want Delta >= c(T). Assume Delta < c(T).

  For p in 1..q, define the "slack"

    E_p = lambda + 1 + D_{p-1} + (p-1) d_p - q a_p,

  where D_{p-1} = sum_{j < p} d_j. With [x]_+ = max(x,0),
  unwinding the suffix inequality gives two lower
  bounds on E_p:

    (Envelope)   E_p >= (p-1) (A_p - a_p + d_p),
    (Truncated)  E_p >= sum_j [d_j - a_p]_+,

  where A_p = max_{s >= p} (a_s - d_s). Averaging is
  exact:

    (1/q) sum_{p=1}^q E_p = Delta - 1/q < c(T).

  Each E_p is nonnegative (the envelope has p-1 >= 0
  and A_p >= a_p - d_p), so the average Delta - 1/q is
  also nonnegative. Combined with Delta < c(T), this
  forces q > 1/c.

  A tail witness. Set rho = 1/(200 B) with B = T + 2,
  and r = floor(rho q). Convexity of F(x) = sum_j
  [d_j - x]_+ plus the small average of E_p shows that
  fewer than rho q indices p satisfy either d_p > 2B/q
  or E_p > 1/10. So there is s in the last rho q
  positions of the removal order with both
  d_s <= 2B/q and E_s <= 1/10.

  Forcing a large suffix. The suffix from s carries at
  most rho q of the total client cost, so
  D_{s-1} > 49/50. Rearranging the definition of E_s:

    q(a_s - d_s) > lambda + 1.8,

  hence the suffix maximum A_s > (lambda + 1.8)/q.

  An early interval blows up. Let I = [ceil(rho q),
  floor(2 rho q)]. Every p in I has A_p >= A_s and
  D_{p-1} <= 2 rho q; the same rearrangement gives
  q(a_p - d_p) < lambda + 1.02, so
  A_p - a_p + d_p > 0.78/q. The envelope then forces

    E_p >= (p-1) * 0.78/q >= 0.39 rho.

  Contradiction. Averaging over all q indices,

    (1/q) sum_{p=1}^q E_p
      >= (|I|/q) * 0.39 rho  >  0.19 rho^2  >  c(T).

  So Delta >= c(T). Undoing the normalisation gives
  sum_C alpha_p <= lambda + (2 - c) P_C up to the K^2
  slack factor; the lemma follows.

  5.5 WHAT MAKES IT USEFUL

  Strictness plugs into the surplus construction like
  this. We have a candidate real solution U (from the
  external CGLSS paper's surplus algorithm) with its
  associated price lambda and client budgets alpha_p.

  Partition the clients by their nearest center in a
  comparison solution O. For each cluster i:

    - If lambda <= T * P_i, strictness applies and
      that cluster contributes lambda + (2 - c/2) P_i
      to the budget sum.

    - If lambda > T * P_i, keep the classical
      "lambda + 2 P_i" bound.

  Summed over all clusters, we pay the full |O| *
  lambda for openings, plus 2 * P_i per cluster minus
  c/2 * P_i on the ones where strictness applies. As
  long as the total cost of clusters with lambda <= T
  P_i is at least a constant fraction of P, the
  improvement is at least that same constant fraction
  times c * P.

  If instead most of the mass is on clusters with
  lambda > T P_i, those clusters are individually
  expensive relative to the price. That is exactly the
  "high sensitivity" signal: it forces few bad clusters
  and is handled by the anchoring + recovery logic in
  Part 6.

  The two tools are complementary. One of them always
  fires.

  PART 6. PUTTING THE TWO TOOLS TOGETHER
  ============================================================

  6.1 THE STABILITY DICHOTOMY

  The final algorithm splits into cases based on how
  the optimum changes when the budget drops from k to
  k - 2L, where L = O(log N) is the surplus from the
  CGLSS construction. Write opt_r for the minimum cost
      of a facility set of size at most r.

  Fix a small rational xi > 0 (the exact value will be
  chosen once c, gamma, and sigma are extracted below).

  LOW SENSITIVITY:
    opt_{k-2L} <= (1 + xi) * opt_k.
    Dropping 2L centers barely changes the cost.
    Handled by the surplus construction + strictness.

  HIGH SENSITIVITY:
    opt_{k-2L} > (1 + xi) * opt_k.
    Dropping 2L centers costs a lot. There is a
    cardinality h' very close to k where dropping just
    one center costs a lot. Handled by anchoring +
    recovery.

  The algorithm cannot distinguish these two cases
  directly (it does not know the optima). So it
  computes candidates for BOTH and keeps the cheaper.

  6.2 THE CASES

  There are actually three disjoint cases.

  Case S (small cardinality): k <= 2L.
    Apply recovery with M_0 = 2L, all comparison
    clusters declared bad. This handles k = O(log N).

  Case L (low sensitivity): k > 2L and
      opt_{k-2L} <= (1 + xi) * opt_k.
    Use surplus construction (CGLSS) at target
    h = k - L. Improve with local search. Use
    strictness on the resulting price and budgets to
    shave below 2.

  Case H (high sensitivity): k > 2L and
      opt_{k-2L} > (1 + xi) * opt_k.
    Locate a sensitive index h' near k. Apply
    anchoring at scale b = xi * opt_{h'} / (4 L) and
    then recovery.

  All three candidates are size at most k. The
  algorithm returns the cheapest. The analysis shows
  that whichever case the instance actually falls
  into, the corresponding candidate is good with high
  probability. And every candidate is size-k, so the
  algorithm always returns at most k facilities.

  6.3 CASE S: SMALL CARDINALITY (k <= 2L)

  Compute a width-5 local optimum S_k of size k. It is
  a factor-5 approximation, and we keep it as a
  candidate and fallback.

  Run recovery (Theorem comp:recovery) on S_k with
  M_0 = 2L, declaring every comparison cluster bad.
  Since every cluster is bad, the proxy promise is
  vacuous: proxy error is zero.

  Recovery guarantees cost <= 1.97 * opt_k with
  probability >= 1 - zeta. Since k <= 2L = O(log N),
  M_0 = O(log N), so recovery runs in polynomial time.

  1.97 <= 2 - gamma for gamma < 0.03, so this case is
  below 2.

  6.4 CASE L: LOW SENSITIVITY

  Assume opt_{k-2L} <= (1 + xi) opt_k.

  Let h = k - L. Apply the CGLSS surplus construction
  at target h. By its Theorem imp:cglss, it returns
  either (i) a solution of valid sequences with exactly
  h regular facilities and at most L free copies, or
  (ii) a 0-valid sequence with at most h regular
  facilities and price N^(-2).

  Project each free copy to its underlying facility.
  The result is a real solution U of size at most
  h + L = k. Pad U to exactly size k (possible because
  k <= |F|) and run width-5 local search starting from
  the padded set. Call the result S. It has size k and
  cost(S) <= cost(U).

  The CGLSS construction also returns a price lambda
  and client budgets alpha_p. Two inequalities hold.
  First, the payment inequality of Lemma
  imp:surplus-history:

    (1 - delta) cost(U) + h (lambda - N eta) <= sum_p alpha_p.

  Here delta = 3v is a small parameter and eta = 2^(-N)
  is the LP precision.

  Second, from the same lemma, the dual comparison:

    sum_p alpha_p <= |H| lambda + 2 cost(H)

  for any nonempty real H. Applied to an optimum of
  size k - 2L:

    L lambda <= 2 (1 + xi) opt_k + e,

  where e = h N eta is the negligible payment error.

  Now partition clients by their comparison center in
  an optimum of size h. For each cluster i with cost
  P_i:

    - If lambda <= T * P_i (T = 200 C_0 is huge),
      strictness applies: cost alpha_p in this cluster
      <= lambda + (2 - c/2) P_i.

    - Otherwise keep the classical "lambda + 2 P_i".

  Sub-case A: bad cost is small. If the total cost of
  "bad" clusters (those not proxied) is <= P_h / 10,
  then local search swap analysis (Lemma loc:swap)
  alone proves

    cost(S) <= 1.432 * P_h  <  (2 - c/100) P_h.

  Sub-case B: bad cost is large. Otherwise, P_b > P_h/10,
  so more than 1/10 of P_h is on bad clusters. Within
  that, define the "high-price" portion Q = sum of P_i
  where lambda <= T P_i. The bad-price bound from
  anchoring gives m * lambda <= 8 C_0 P_h, so the
  low-price portion is at most m lambda / T <= P_h/25.
  Therefore Q >= P_h/10 - P_h/25 = 3 P_h/50.

  Apply strictness on the high-price portion. Summing
  the per-cluster gains:

    sum_p alpha_p <= h lambda + (2 - c/40) P_h.

  Combining with the payment and dual bounds, plus the
  small price error e <= v P_h, gives

    (1 - delta) cost(S) <= (2 - c/40) P_h + e.

  Dividing by (1 - delta) with delta = 3v:

    cost(S) <= (2 - c/100) P_h.

  Finally P_h = opt_{k-L} <= opt_{k-2L} <= (1 + xi) opt_k.
  So

    cost(S) <= (2 - c/100)(1 + xi) opt_k.

  With xi = gamma/16 <= c/1600 and gamma <= c/100, this
  is at most

    (2 - 7 gamma / 8) opt_k  <  (2 - gamma/2) opt_k.

  Below 2, good.

  6.5 CASE H: HIGH SENSITIVITY

  Assume opt_{k-2L} > (1 + xi) opt_k.

  Lemma asm:sensitive of the paper says there is an
  integer h' in (k-2L, k] such that, with beta =
  xi / (4L),

    opt_{h'-1} >= (1 + beta) opt_{h'},    and
    opt_{h'}   <= (1 + xi) opt_k.

  So there is a nearby cardinality where dropping just
  one center costs a lot, and the cost at h' is barely
  worse than opt_k.

  The single-center sensitivity forces the comparison
  clusters of any optimum O of size h' to be well
  separated. Specifically, for each i in O,

    n_i * sep_i  >=  beta * P_O,

  where P_O = opt_{h'} is the cost of O and sep_i =
  min over j in O, j != i, of d(i, j) is the separation
  of i from the nearest other center.

  The algorithm does:

    1. Compute a width-5 local optimum S_{h'} of size
       h'. It is a factor-5 approximation.

    2. The scale condition of anchoring holds trivially
       with b = beta * P_O / 4:
         |S_{h'}| - h' = 0,
         no centers are "short" (each has
         n_i * sep_i >= beta P_O >= b),
         so 0 + 0 <= 10 P_O.
       Anchoring therefore produces a partition into
       good and bad with:
         proxy error <= P_g + 0.01 P_O,
         m <= C_0 P_O / b
           = C_0 * 4 L / xi.

    3. Set M_0 = ceil(4 C_0 L / xi). Call recovery
       (Theorem comp:recovery) on S_{h'} with this
       bound. Recovery returns a solution of size
       at most h' with cost <= 1.97 P_O and
       probability >= 1 - zeta, in time

         exp(O(M_0)) * poly(N) * log(2/zeta).

       Since M_0 = O(C_0 log N), this is polynomial
       in N for fixed constants.

  The recovery output has cost <= 1.97 P_O.
  Since gamma <= 3/100, we have 1.97 = 2 - 0.03
  <= 2 - gamma, so cost <= (2 - gamma) P_O.
  Combined with P_O <= (1 + xi) opt_k = (1 + gamma/16)
  opt_k and the rounding transfer (1 + eps_0) =
  (1 + gamma/16), we get

    cost <= (2 - gamma)(1 + gamma/16)^2 opt_k
         <= (2 - 3 gamma/4) opt_k
         <  (2 - gamma/2) opt_k.

  Below 2, good.

  6.6 THE FINAL THEOREM

  Choose the fixed constants in this order:

    mu  = 1/100          (anchoring accuracy, Section 4.10)
    C_0 = 10 + 60000 * 2^3999   (from mu = 1/100)
    T   = 200 C_0        (strictness price bound)
    c   = c(T)           (strictness gap, Section 5.2)
    gamma = min(3/100, c/100)
    xi  = eps_0 = gamma/16
    sigma = gamma/4

  Every one of these is a fixed positive constant
  depending only on the paper's parameters. They do
  not depend on the input. Each is astronomically
  tiny in practice, but each is well-defined and
  positive.

  Main theorem (main:theorem) of the paper. There is an
  absolute constant sigma > 0 with the following
  property. For every fixed a > 0, a randomized
  polynomial-time algorithm for metric k-median always
  returns a set S with |S| <= k and satisfies

    Pr[ cost(S) <= (2 - sigma) opt_k ]
      >= 1 - (|D| + |F| + 2)^(-a).

  The same algorithm can be chosen to satisfy

    E[ cost(S) ] <= (2 - sigma) opt_k.

  The running time is polynomial in the bit length of
  the input metric. Its degree may depend on the fixed
  constants (like C_0), but not on the input size.

  6.7 HOW THE PROBABILITY AND EXPECTATION WORK

  The algorithm runs all three subcalls (Cases S, L, H)
  and keeps the cheapest feasible output. The
  corresponding analysis says:

    Case S: if k <= 2L, recovery at M_0 = 2L
            succeeds on 1.97 opt_k with probability
            >= 1 - zeta.

    Case L: if the instance is low-sensitivity,
            the surplus + local search candidate
            beats (2 - gamma/2) opt_k
            deterministically.

    Case H: if the instance is high-sensitivity,
            recovery at the sensitive h' succeeds on
            1.97 (1+xi) opt_k with probability
            >= 1 - zeta.

  Whichever case the instance falls into, the
  corresponding subcall succeeds with probability
  >= 1 - zeta. So the algorithm returns cost
  <= (2 - sigma) opt_k with probability >= 1 - zeta.

  For the probability bound: take zeta = (N+2)^(-a).
  Its bit length is O(log N). Repeating each subcall
  poly(N) times (or exp(O(M_0)) times) drives the
  failure probability below zeta. This is exactly what
  the running time budget allows.

  For the expectation bound: split the analysis into
  the success event (probability >= 1 - zeta, cost
  <= (2 - sigma) opt_k) and the failure event
  (probability <= zeta, cost <= 5 opt_k, since the
  factor-5 local-search fallback is always available).
  Then

    E[cost] <= (2 - sigma) opt_k + 5 * zeta * opt_k
             <= (2 - sigma/2) opt_k

  when zeta <= sigma / 10.

  That is the final guarantee: factor strictly less
  than 2, both with high probability and in expectation,
  while always respecting the budget |S| <= k.

  6.8 WHAT NOBODY CLAIMS IN THIS PAPER

  Some honest caveats.

  First, the factor 2 - sigma is not usable in
  practice. The constant sigma is astronomically small
  (something like 2^(-8000)), so a factor-2 algorithm
  would be indistinguishable from this one on any real
  input. The point is that the theoretical factor is
  strictly below 2, not that the improvement is large.

  Second, the running time is polynomial for fixed
  constants, but the constants are absurd. C_0 is
  bigger than 2^3999, so N^C_0 is not a running time
  anyone will ever run. Theoretical polynomial does
  not imply practical.

  Third, the algorithm heavily depends on
  external papers: the CGLSS 2026 surplus construction,
  the Vondrak/Calinescu-Chekuri-Pal-Vondrak continuous
  greedy, and the Alon-Yuster-Zwick color coding. The
  paper's contribution is the recovery and strictness
  tools plus the assembly, not the subroutines.

  Fourth, the paper requires positive integer
  distances bounded by a polynomial in N. Real
  metrics with rational distances are handled by
  reducing to that case at cost (1 + eps_0) each,
  which multiplies into the final factor. This is
  what makes the general rational-metric theorem
  possible.
