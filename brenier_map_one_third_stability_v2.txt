Fix an integer $d geq 2$. Let $K subset ℝ^d$ be a compact convex set with nonempty interior, and let $rho$ denote the uniform probability measure
$$
rho = (1_K(x) , dx)/(|K|).
$$
Fix a nonempty compact set $Y subset ℝ^d$; write $Prob(Y)$ for the Borel probability measures on $Y$.

For $mu in Prob(Y)$, the quadratic-cost optimal transport map from $rho$ to $mu$ is the minimizer $T_mu$ of
$$
int_K |x - T(x)|^2 , drho(x)
$$
over all Borel $T : K to Y$ with $T_{#} rho = mu$. The minimizer exists, is unique up to $rho$-null sets, and equals the gradient of a convex function on $K$. Both the existence and the gradient structure go back to Brenier.

For compactly supported $mu$ and $nu$, the quadratic Wasserstein distance is
$$
W_2(mu,nu) = ( inf_{pi in Pi(mu,nu)} int_{ℝ^d times ℝ^d} |y-z|^2 , dpi(y,z) )^{1/2},
$$
where $Pi(mu,nu)$ is the set of couplings of $mu$ and $nu$.

The stability question: how strongly does $T_mu$ depend on $mu$? The answer below is one-third Holder in $W_2$, with a constant that depends only on $K$ and $Y$.

There is a finite constant $C(K,Y)$ such that for all $mu,nu in Prob(Y)$,
$$
| T_mu - T_nu |_{L^2(rho)} leq C(K,Y) , W_2(mu,nu)^{1/3}.
$$
The targets may be atomic, singular, or absolutely continuous. The exponent $1/3$ cannot be increased: if $K = Y = [-1,1]^d$ and $alpha > 1/3$, the ratio $| T_mu - T_nu |_{L^2(rho)} / W_2(mu,nu)^alpha$ is unbounded over pairs of measures supported on three atoms.

pdfrefs_brenier/arxiv_2404.02855v1-divol-nilesweed-pooladian-2025.pdf

The bound is uniform in atom count, in positive atom masses, in site separation, and in density bounds. Neither strict convexity nor smoothness of $∂ K$ enters, and $Y$ need not be convex.

An explicit constant appears in the interpolation step later. With $K subset B(0,R)$, $Y subset B(0,L)$ and $P_K$ the sum of the $(d-1)$-volumes of the coordinate projections of $K$ onto the hyperplanes orthogonal to the coordinate axes, one admissible value is
$$
C_* = ( 12 , d , R^2 ( 1 + √(C_0) )^2 + (28 L^2 P_K)/(|K|) )^{1/2},
$$
with $C_0 = 162$.

A three-atom construction on a fixed cube gives the sharpness assertion, disproving every exponent above $1/3$. Then the upper bound is built from four pieces: finite-optimal-map stability, cell calculus for prescribed masses, a uniform moment estimate that removes any dependence on the inverse graph-Laplacian, and a coordinate interpolation inequality that converts potential stability into map stability. Taking limits over finite nets extends everything to all Borel targets.

The exponent $1/3$ is sharp. The construction uses three affine functions on a fixed cube; a small tilt of one slope moves a thin central cell while the target displacement stays second order.

Begin with an optimality criterion for gradients of maxima of affine functions. Let $u(x) = max_{1 leq i leq N} { x cdot y_i + c_i }$ on a convex $K$, with distinct slopes $y_i in ℝ^d$ and constants $c_i in ℝ$. Let $T(x) = y_i$ at the smallest maximizing $i$, and let $lambda = T_# rho$.

On the complement of the finite union of hyperplanes where two distinct slopes tie for the maximum, $u$ equals a single affine function in a neighborhood, so $nabla u(x) = y_i$; the tie set is null for $rho$. Extend $c(y) = c_i$ to the slopes $y_i$, and $c(y) = -∞$ otherwise. Then $x cdot y + c(y) leq u(x)$ for all $(x,y)$, with equality if and only if $y = T(x)$ for $rho$-almost every $x$ (equal slopes are merged to the largest intercept). For any coupling $pi$ of $rho$ and $lambda$,
$$
int x cdot y , dpi(x,y) leq int_K u , drho - int_Y c(y) , dlambda(y),
$$
with equality for the graph plan of $T$. The cross term is the only part of the quadratic cost not fixed by the marginals, since
$$
int |x - y|^2 , dpi(x,y) = int_K |x|^2 , drho(x) + int_Y |y|^2 , dlambda(y) - 2 int x cdot y , dpi(x,y).
$$
Hence the graph of $T$ is the unique optimal plan, and $T = nabla u$ almost everywhere.

Specialize to $K = Y = [-1,1]^d$, so that $rho$ is uniform on $K$. Fix $0 < a < 1/2$ and put $b = a/2$. Define
$$
u_a(x) = max{ -x_1, x_1, a }, qquad tildetilde u_a(x)u_a(x) = max{ -x_1, x_1, a + b x_2 }.
$$
The slopes lie in $Y$: $-e_1, e_1, 0$ for $u_a$; $-e_1, e_1, b e_2$ for $tildetilde u_a$.u_a$. Set $mu_a = (nabla u_a)_# rho$ and $nu_a = (nabla tildetilde u_a)_u_a)_# rho$.

The central cells are $A_a = { x in K : |x_1| < a }$ and $B_a = { x in K : |x_1| < a + b x_2 }$. Since $|b x_2| leq b = a/2 < a$ throughout $K$, the quantity $a + b x_2$ stays strictly between $0$ and $3 a / 2 < 1$. Both cells therefore live strictly inside $K$ in the first coordinate, away from the null boundaries where two affine pieces tie, and both have mass $a$:
$$
rho(A_a) = rho(B_a) = a.
$$
Away from $A_a$ the first map equals $sgn(x_1) e_1$, and away from $B_a$ the second map equals $sgn(x_1) e_1$. The two targets are three-atom measures:
$$
mu_a = (1-a)/(2) (delta_{-e_1} + delta_{e_1}) + a , delta_0, qquad
nu_a = (1-a)/(2) (delta_{-e_1} + delta_{e_1}) + a , delta_{b e_2}.
$$

The two targets share the outer atoms. Matching the outer atoms and sending $0$ to $b e_2$ costs $a b^2$. Every point of $supp(mu_a)$ has second coordinate zero, while the atom $b e_2 in supp(nu_a)$ has second coordinate $b$ and mass $a$, so every coupling satisfies
$$
int |y - z|^2 , dpi(y,z) geq int |y_2 - z_2|^2 , dpi(y,z) geq a b^2.
$$
Consequently
$$
W_2(mu_a, nu_a)^2 = a b^2.
$$

For the map distance, break the pointwise identity
$$
|T_{mu_a}(x) - T_{nu_a}(x)|^2
$$
into four regions. On $A_a cap B_a$ the maps are $0$ and $b e_2$, and the squared difference is $b^2$. On $A_a ∖ B_a$ the maps are $0$ and $sgn(x_1) e_1$, and the squared difference is one. On $B_a ∖ A_a$ the maps are $sgn(x_1) e_1$ and $b e_2$, and the squared difference is $1 + b^2$. Outside $A_a cup B_a$ the two maps coincide.

Fix $x_2 = t$. On that slice, $A_a$ occupies an interval of length $2a$, while $B_a$ occupies an interval of length $2(a + b t)$. Elementary integration gives
$$
rho(A_a ∖ B_a) = rho(B_a ∖ A_a) = (b)/(4), qquad
rho(A_a cap B_a) = a - (b)/(4).
$$
Combining,
$$
int_K |T_{mu_a} - T_{nu_a}|^2 , drho
= b^2 ( a - (b)/(4) ) + (b)/(4) + (b)/(4) (1 + b^2)
= (b)/(2) + a b^2.
$$

Substituting $b = a/2$,
$$
W_2(mu_a, nu_a) = frac{a^{3/2}}{2}, qquad
| T_{mu_a} - T_{nu_a} |_{L^2(rho)} = frac{√(a)}{2} √(1 + a^2).
$$
For $alpha > 1/3$,
$$
frac{| T_{mu_a} - T_{nu_a} |_{L^2(rho)}}{W_2(mu_a, nu_a)^alpha}
= 2^{alpha - 1} √(1 + a^2) , a^{(1 - 3alpha)/2}.
$$
The exponent is negative, so the ratio diverges as $a ↓ 0$. The source $K$, the target container $Y$, and the number of atoms are fixed; only the geometry of three affine pieces varies. This shows the exponent $1/3$ cannot be increased.

u(x) = max_{1 leq i leq N} { x cdot y_i + c_i },

where the intercepts $c_i$ are the unique choice that puts exactly the mass $m_i$ on cell $i$. Cell calculus and a unique normalized vector of intercepts are established first, then a moment estimate that controls variations of the first moments $B_i$ by the mass variations $M_i$.

Cell calculus. Fix distinct sites $y_1, ..., y_N in ℝ^d$ and intercepts $c = (c_1, ..., c_N)$. The closed Laguerre cells
$$
P_i(c) = K cap ∩_{j neq i} { x in ℝ^d : x cdot (y_i - y_j) + c_i - c_j geq 0 }
$$
partition $K$ up to the null tie set. Write $M_i(c) = rho(P_i(c))$ and $B_i(c) = int_{P_i(c)} x , drho(x)$.

For each $i neq j$ the equality plane is $H_{ij} = { x in ℝ^d : x cdot (y_i - y_j) + c_i - c_j = 0 }$, and the common face is $F_{ij} = P_i cap P_j$. Introduce the surface coefficients
$$
g_{ij} = (1)/(|K| |y_i - y_j|) int_{F_{ij}} 1 , dS, qquad
b_{ij} = (1)/(|K| |y_i - y_j|) int_{F_{ij}} x , dS,
$$
where $dS$ denotes the $(d-1)$-dimensional surface measure on $H_{ij}$. Faces of lower dimension, or faces that meet the boundary of $K$ only on a null set, contribute zero.

On the open set of configurations with distinct sites and $M_i > 0$ for every $i$, the maps $M_i$ and $B_i$ are jointly $C^1$ in intercepts and sites. For an intercept direction $r = (r_i)$ and a site direction $v = (v_i)$,
$$
D M_i[r,v] = sum_{j neq i} ( g_{ij} (r_i - r_j) + b_{ij} cdot (v_i - v_j) ),
$$
$$
D B_i[r,v] = sum_{j neq i} b_{ij} (r_i - r_j) + (1)/(|K|) sum_{j neq i} (1)/(|y_i - y_j|) int_{F_{ij}} x ( x cdot (v_i - v_j) ) , dS(x).
$$
The intercept-only operator
$$
(G q)_i = D M_i[q,0] = sum_{j neq i} g_{ij} (q_i - q_j)
$$
is a symmetric weighted graph-Laplacian: its kernel is the constant vectors, and its range is ${ z in ℝ^N : sum_i z_i = 0 }$.

The derivative formula is obtained by telescoping the difference of indicator products one factor at a time. For a fixed pair $i, j$ write $ell_{ij}(x) = x cdot (y_i - y_j) + c_i - c_j$ and perturb the configuration by $h(r, v)$ with $h ↓ 0$. On $K$, the region where the indicator $1_{ell_{ij} geq 0}$ changes has $|ell_{ij}(x)| leq C h$ for a constant $C$ that depends on the configuration and direction. Coordinatizing by $(z, t) mapsto z + h t , n_{ij}$ with $n_{ij} = (y_i - y_j) / |y_i - y_j|$ and $z in H_{ij}$, the volume factor $h$ cancels the difference quotient. Outside the null set $(∂ K cap H_{ij}) cup ∪_{k neq i, j} (H_{ik} cap H_{ij})$, all remaining indicators converge to the values at $z$, and the changed factor converges to $1_{{ |y_i - y_j| t + r_i - r_j + z cdot (v_i - v_j) geq 0 }} - 1_{{ t geq 0 }}$. Its integral in $t$ is $(r_i - r_j + z cdot (v_i - v_j)) / |y_i - y_j|$. Dominated convergence yields the formula. Applying the same argument in the reverse direction gives two-sided derivatives, and continuity of the coefficients in $(c, y)$ follows from a similar uniform convergence of the face integrals and the Jacobian factors.

Connectivity of the graph on ${ 1, ..., N }$ with edges ${ g_{ij} > 0 }$ follows from a segment argument. Choose strictly active points $p, q$ in two cells with $p$ outside all equality planes and $q$ outside all equality planes and the affine spans of $p$ with every triple-equality set. The segment $[p, q]$ stays in the interior of $K$ and does not meet any triple equality set. Along the segment the maximizing index changes only by crossing equality planes one at a time. At each crossing exactly two affine functions are maximal; a small relatively open disk in the common equality plane lies inside the common face, so the corresponding $g_{ij} > 0$. The successive maximizing indices give a graph path. Symmetry of the face coefficients gives $q cdot G q = sum_{i < j} g_{ij} (q_i - q_j)^2$, which identifies the kernel of $G$ with the constant vectors and its range with the orthogonal complement of the constant vectors.

Prescribed cell masses. Fix positive weights $m_1, ..., m_N$ with $sum_i m_i = 1$. For every distinct site configuration there is a unique intercept vector $c$ with
$$
M_i(c) = m_i quad (1 leq i leq N), qquad sum_{i=1}^N m_i c_i = 0,
$$
and this normalized vector depends $C^1$ on the sites.

The proof minimizes the convex function
$$
F_y(c) = int_K max_i { x cdot y_i + c_i } , drho(x) - sum_{i=1}^N m_i c_i
$$
on the hyperplane $H_m = { c in ℝ^N : sum_i m_i c_i = 0 }$. On $H_m$, an upper bound on $max_i c_i$ together with the weighted zero-mean condition bounds every coordinate from below, so $F_y(c) to ∞$ as $|c| to ∞$; coercivity provides a minimizer. Distinct sites make the maximizing index unique almost everywhere, and differentiating the maximum under the integral (justified by bounded difference quotients) gives $nabla F_y(c) = (M_i(c) - m_i)_{i=1}^N$. At a minimum on $H_m$ this gradient lies in the span of $m$. Its coordinate sum is zero, since the masses and weights each sum to one. As $sum_i m_i = 1$, the gradient must vanish, so $M_i(c) = m_i$. Conversely, any such $c$ minimizes $F_y$ by convexity.

Local uniqueness as the sites vary follows from the implicit function theorem applied to the mass residual on $H_m$. At any solution all cells have positive mass, so the calculus above applies and the intercept derivative of the mass residual is the restriction
$$
G : H_m → { z in ℝ^N : sum_i z_i = 0 }.
$$
Both spaces have dimension $N-1$ and the only constant vector in $H_m$ is zero, so the restriction is an isomorphism. The implicit function theorem gives a locally unique $C^1$ branch. Global uniqueness follows: two normalized solutions would produce a segment of minimizers of $F_y$ by convexity, contradicting local isolation. The $C^1$ branches glue by uniqueness. Only invertibility of the restricted $G$ is used; no quantitative bound on its inverse is needed.

A geometric coupling lemma. Let $U, V subset ℝ^d$ be compact convex sets of positive volume. There is a coupling $(X, Z)$ of the uniform probability measures on $U$ and $V$ with
$$
Law!( (X + Z)/(2) ) leq frac{dx}{√(|U| |V|)}.
$$
The inequality is an inequality of measures on $ℝ^d$.

The construction is Knothe's triangular rearrangement, adapted to averaging the map with its counterpart. Alternative derivations come from displacement concavity and from the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality on the diagonal entries of the triangular map.
pdfrefs_brenier/carlier-galichon-santambrogio-2010-siam-j-math-anal.pdf
pdfrefs_brenier/mccann-1997-advances.pdf

For $0 leq k < d$ set
$$
p_k(xi) = int_{ℝ^{d-k}} 1_{int(U)}(xi, eta) , deta, qquad p_d = 1_{int(U)}.
$$
Thus $p_0 = |U|$. Let $D_k$ be the projection of $int(U)$ onto its first $k$ coordinates. Each $p_k$ is positive and continuous on $D_k$. Continuity follows by dominated convergence: at a prefix in $D_k$, the sublevel sets of the fibers converge almost everywhere as the prefix varies, because a hyperplane meeting the interior of a convex body meets it in a face whose outer boundary is null; the boundedness of $U$ provides a common integrable bound. Fiber integrals are Borel functions on the full domains.

Given $s = (s_1, ..., s_d) in (0,1)^d$, construct $X(s)$ recursively. Given a prefix $xi = (X_1, ..., X_{k-1}) in D_{k-1}$, let $X_k$ be the quantile at level $s_k$ of the density
$$
t mapsto frac{p_k(xi, t)}{p_{k-1}(xi)}.
$$
Fubini makes this a probability density on a bounded open interval, and its cumulative distribution function is strictly increasing there. The quantile is therefore unique, keeps the new prefix in $D_k$, and satisfies
$$
∂_{s_k} X_k = frac{p_{k-1}(X_1, ..., X_{k-1})}{p_k(X_1, ..., X_k)} > 0.
$$
The construction is measurable: the conditional cumulative distribution functions are Borel in the arguments, and quantiles of Borel functions are Borel. With $s$ uniform on $(0, 1)^d$, successive conditioning gives the uniform law on $U$. The diagonal partial derivatives telescope:
$$
prod_{k=1}^d ∂_{s_k} X_k = (p_0)/(p_d) = |U|,
$$
which is constant on the interior of $U$.

The same construction for $V$ with the same parameter $s$ yields $Z(s)$ and $prod_{k=1}^d ∂_{s_k} Z_k = |V|$. For $H = (X+Z)/2$, the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality applied to the diagonal derivatives yields
$$
prod_{k=1}^d ∂_{s_k} H_k
geq sqrt{( prod_{k=1}^d ∂_{s_k} X_k ) ( prod_{k=1}^d ∂_{s_k} Z_k )}
= √(|U| |V|).
$$

Now apply a change of variables to $H$. For a nonnegative Borel $g$ on $ℝ^d$ and fixed $a = (s_1, ..., s_{d-1})$, one-dimensional substitution in the strictly increasing $C^1$ map $s_d mapsto H_d(a, s_d)$, followed by enlargement of its image to all of $ℝ$, gives
$$
int_0^1 g(H_1(a), ..., H_{d-1}(a), H_d(a,t)) ∂_{s_d} H_d(a,t) , dt leq int_{ℝ} g(H_1(a), ..., H_{d-1}(a), z) , dz.
$$
The last integral is itself a nonnegative Borel function of $a$. Multiplying by the earlier derivative factors (which depend only on $a$) and applying Tonelli's theorem, then iterating this argument backwards over $k = d-1, ..., 1$, gives
$$
int_{(0,1)^d} g(H(s)) prod_{k=1}^d ∂_{s_k} H_k(s) , ds leq int_{ℝ^d} g(x) , dx.
$$
Combining with the pointwise lower bound on the product gives
$$
𝔼 g!( (X + Z)/(2) ) leq frac{1}{√(|U| |V|)} int_{ℝ^d} g(x) , dx,
$$
which is the asserted measure domination.

The moment estimate. Fix distinct sites and intercepts with $M_i > 0$ for every $i$, and let $K subset B(0, R)$ for some $R > 0$. Then every intercept direction $q in ℝ^N$ satisfies
$$
sum_{i=1}^N (|D B_i[q, 0]|^2)/(M_i) leq C_0 , R^2 sum_{i=1}^N (|D M_i[q, 0]|^2)/(M_i),
qquad C_0 = 162.
$$
The estimate is uniform over the number of cells, the cell masses, and the site separations.

Fix the direction $q$ and hold the sites fixed. For small $s > 0$ let $P_i^-$ and $P_i^+$ be the cells at $c - s q$ and $c + s q$. Set
$$
alpha_i = rho(P_i^-), qquad beta_i = rho(P_i^+), qquad gamma_i = √(alpha_i beta_i).
$$
Continuity in the cell-calculus lemma makes every $alpha_i, beta_i$ positive for small $s$. Couple the uniform measures on $P_i^{pm}$ by the geometric coupling lemma, writing $X_i in P_i^-$, $Z_i in P_i^+$ and $D_i = 𝔼 |X_i - Z_i|^2$.

For any competing index $j$, the definition of $P_i^{mp}$ gives
$$
X_i cdot (y_i - y_j) + c_i - c_j - s (q_i - q_j) geq 0, qquad
Z_i cdot (y_i - y_j) + c_i - c_j + s (q_i - q_j) geq 0.
$$
Averaging,
$$
(X_i + Z_i)/(2) cdot (y_i - y_j) + c_i - c_j geq 0.
$$
Convexity of $K$ keeps the midpoint in $K$, so the midpoint lies in $P_i$. Since $|P_i^{pm}| = |K| alpha_i^{pm}$, the coupling lemma applied to $U = P_i^-$ and $V = P_i^+$ gives the measure domination
$$
gamma_i , Law!( (X_i + Z_i)/(2) ) leq rhobig|_{P_i}.
$$

Apply this domination with the nonnegative test function $Phi(x) = R^2 - |x|^2$. Set $A_i^{pm} = 𝔼 Phi(X_i^{pm})$, where $X_i^-$ and $X_i^+$ denote the two uniform samples. The algebraic identity
$$
Phi!( (X_i + Z_i)/(2) )
= (Phi(X_i) + Phi(Z_i))/(2) + (|X_i - Z_i|^2)/(4)
$$
together with the measure domination gives
$$
sum_i gamma_i ( (A_i^- + A_i^+)/(2) + (D_i)/(4) )
leq int_K Phi , drho
= (1)/(2) sum_i ( alpha_i A_i^- + beta_i A_i^+ ).
$$
The rightmost equality uses that the two partitions at $c pm s q$ have null overlaps.

Set $delta_i = alpha_i - beta_i$ and
$$
A = sum_i gamma_i D_i, qquad
B = sum_i (delta_i^2)/(gamma_i).
$$
Subtracting the averaged endpoint terms in the display above yields
$$
frac{A}{4}
leq sum_i [ ( (alpha_i + beta_i)/(2) - gamma_i ) (A_i^- + A_i^+)/(2) + (delta_i)/(4) (A_i^- - A_i^+) ].
$$
Note that $0 leq A_i^{pm} leq R^2$, and
$$
(alpha_i + beta_i)/(2) - gamma_i
= frac{(alpha_i - beta_i)^2}{2 (√(alpha_i) + √(beta_i))^2}
leq (delta_i^2)/(8 gamma_i).
$$
Since $|X_i^{pm}| leq R$,
$$
|A_i^- - A_i^+| leq 2 R , 𝔼 |X_i^- - X_i^+| leq 2 R √(D_i).
$$
Cauchy-Schwarz therefore gives
$$
frac{A}{4} leq frac{R^2 B}{8} + (R)/(2) sqrt{A B}.
$$
Using $(R)/(2) sqrt{A B} leq frac{A}{8} + frac{R^2 B}{2}$ and rearranging,
$$
A leq 5 R^2 B.
$$

The first-moment difference over the symmetric change is
$$
Delta B_i = B_i(c - s q) - B_i(c + s q) = alpha_i 𝔼 X_i - beta_i 𝔼 Z_i.
$$
Hence
$$
|Delta B_i| leq R |delta_i| + alpha_i √(D_i).
$$
For the fixed configuration and the fixed direction $q$, continuity allows $s$ small enough that $alpha_i, beta_i, gamma_i$ all lie in $[M_i/2, 2 M_i]$. Set $E = sum_i delta_i^2 / M_i$. These comparisons give $alpha_i^2 / M_i leq 8 gamma_i$ and $B leq 2 E$. Consequently
$$
sum_i (|Delta B_i|^2)/(M_i)
leq 2 R^2 E + 2 sum_i (alpha_i^2)/(M_i) D_i
leq 2 R^2 E + 16 A
leq 162 R^2 E.
$$
Divide by $(2 s)^2$ and let $s ↓ 0$. The cell-calculus lemma gives
$$
(Delta B_i)/(2 s) to -D B_i[q, 0], qquad
(delta_i)/(2 s) = (M_i(c - s q) - M_i(c + s q))/(2 s) to -D M_i[q, 0],
$$
so the claimed estimate follows.

The threshold for $s$ may depend on the direction $q$ and on the configuration, including its smallest mass. Only the constant $162$ survives the derivative limit.

From the moment estimate to potential stability along an optimal coupling. Let $mu, nu in Prob(Y)$ be finitely supported. Choose an optimal coupling
$$
pi = sum_{i=1}^N m_i delta_{(y_i^0, y_i^1)}, qquad m_i > 0, quad sum_i m_i = 1,
$$
after merging identical pairs and discarding zero-mass ones. Then
$$
W_2(mu, nu)^2 = sum_{i=1}^N m_i |y_i^0 - y_i^1|^2.
$$
Write $v_i = y_i^1 - y_i^0$; the quantity $sum_i m_i |v_i|^2 = W_2(mu, nu)^2$.

Optimality implies the monotonicity inequality
$$
(y_i^0 - y_j^0) cdot (y_i^1 - y_j^1) geq 0 qquad (1 leq i, j leq N).
$$
If the scalar product is negative, swapping equal positive portions of the mass between pairs $i$ and $j$ reduces the total quadratic cost by $2 ε (y_i^0 - y_j^0) cdot (y_i^1 - y_j^1)$ for $0 < ε leq min(m_i, m_j)$.

Set $y_i(t) = (1-t) y_i^0 + t y_i^1$. For $0 < t < 1$, the sites $y_1(t), ..., y_N(t)$ are distinct. Indeed, with $a = y_i^0 - y_j^0$ and $b = y_i^1 - y_j^1$,
$$
|y_i(t) - y_j(t)|^2 = (1-t)^2 |a|^2 + t^2 |b|^2 + 2 t (1-t) a cdot b,
$$
and the right-hand side is strictly positive unless $a = b = 0$, in which case the pairs $(y_i^0, y_i^1)$ and $(y_j^0, y_j^1)$ coincide and were already merged.

By the prescribed-mass lemma, for $0 < t < 1$ there are unique intercepts $c_i(t)$ with $M_i(t) = m_i$ and $sum_i m_i c_i(t) = 0$, and these are $C^1$ in $t$. Let $r_i = c_i'(t)$; then $sum_i m_i r_i = 0$.

Differentiating the mass constraints gives $D M_i[c'(t), y'(t)] = 0$ for every $i$. Combining the two derivative formulas from cell calculus, for every intercept direction $q$,
$$
sum_i q_i D M_i[r, v] = sum_i r_i D M_i[q, 0] + sum_i v_i cdot D B_i[q, 0].
$$
Indeed, expanding
$$
sum_i q_i D M_i[r, v] = sum_{i < j} g_{ij} (q_i - q_j)(r_i - r_j) + sum_{i < j} b_{ij} cdot (q_i - q_j)(v_i - v_j),
$$
$$
sum_i v_i cdot D B_i[q, 0] = sum_{i < j} b_{ij} cdot (q_i - q_j)(v_i - v_j),
$$
and the difference of the first expression and the second is $sum_{i < j} g_{ij} (q_i - q_j)(r_i - r_j) = sum_i r_i D M_i[q, 0]$ by symmetry of $g_{ij}$. Setting $D M_i[r, v] = 0$ gives the duality relation
$$
sum_i r_i D M_i[q, 0] = - sum_i v_i cdot D B_i[q, 0].
$$

The range of $G$ is ${ z in ℝ^N : sum_i z_i = 0 }$. Together with $sum_i m_i r_i = 0$, this permits a choice of intercept direction $q$ with
$$
D M_i[q, 0] = m_i r_i qquad (1 leq i leq N).
$$
No quantitative bound on $q$ is needed.

Set $E_r = sum_i m_i |r_i|^2$. Using the duality relation, weighted Cauchy-Schwarz, the equality $sum_i m_i |v_i|^2 = W_2(mu, nu)^2$, and the moment estimate,
$$
E_r = sum_i r_i (m_i r_i) = sum_i r_i D M_i[q, 0] = - sum_i v_i cdot D B_i[q, 0]
$$
$$
leq ( sum_i m_i |v_i|^2 )^{1/2} ( sum_i (|D B_i[q, 0]|^2)/(m_i) )^{1/2}
leq W_2(mu, nu) ( C_0 R^2 sum_i (|D M_i[q, 0]|^2)/(m_i) )^{1/2}
$$
$$
= W_2(mu, nu) ( C_0 R^2 sum_i m_i |r_i|^2 )^{1/2}
= √(C_0) R , W_2(mu, nu) , √(E_r).
$$
This includes the case $E_r = 0$. Hence
$$
( sum_i m_i |r_i|^2 )^{1/2} leq √(C_0) R , W_2(mu, nu).
$$

Define the interpolating potential
$$
u_t(x) = max_i { x cdot y_i(t) + c_i(t) }.
$$
On $K$, the slopes $y_i(t)$ stay in $B(0, L)$ (a convex combination of two points in the ball), so $u_t$ is convex and $L$-Lipschitz. At a strict maximum index $i$, the time derivative is $∂_t u_t(x) = x cdot v_i + r_i$. Fubini identifies this derivative almost everywhere in $(t, x) in (0,1) times K$, since the tie set has zero $rho$-measure for each fixed $t$. Consequently, for interior times,
$$
|∂_t u_t|_{L^2(rho)} leq R ( sum_i m_i |v_i|^2 )^{1/2} + ( sum_i m_i |r_i|^2 )^{1/2} leq (1 + √(C_0)) R , W_2(mu, nu).
$$
The fundamental theorem of calculus together with Minkowski's inequality gives, for $0 < t < t' < 1$,
$$
|u_t - u_{t'}|_{L^2(rho)} leq (1 + √(C_0)) R , W_2(mu, nu) |t - t'|.
$$

Extend to endpoints. For each $i$ the estimate $|c_i'(t)| leq √(C_0) R W_2(mu, nu) / √(m_i)$ shows that $c_i(t)$ has finite limits as $t ↓ 0$ and $t ↑ 1$. Denote them again by $c_i(0)$ and $c_i(1)$, and set
$$
u_0(x) = max_i { x cdot y_i^0 + c_i(0) }, qquad u_1(x) = max_i { x cdot y_i^1 + c_i(1) }.
$$
Because $y_i(t) to y_i^0$ and $c_i(t) to c_i(0)$ as $t ↓ 0$, the potentials $u_t$ converge uniformly to $u_0$ on $K$; similarly $u_t to u_1$ as $t ↑ 1$. The stability inequality therefore holds at the endpoints:
$$
|u_0 - u_1|_{L^2(rho)} leq (1 + √(C_0)) R , W_2(mu, nu).
$$

Finally, $u_0$ and $u_1$ are the finite-maximum potentials of $mu$ and $nu$. At each interior $t$, the slope map of $u_t$ produces exactly the law $sum_i m_i delta_{y_i(t)}$, because $M_i(t) = m_i$. Passing to the limit, the slope law of $u_0$ is $mu$ and that of $u_1$ is $nu$: for any continuous test $varphi$,
$$
int_K varphi(nabla u_t) , drho = sum_i m_i varphi(y_i(t)) xrightarrow[t ↓ 0]{} sum_i m_i varphi(y_i^0) = int_Y varphi , dmu.
$$
By the optimality criterion, the slope selection of each finite maximum with a given slope law is that law's unique optimal map from $rho$. Hence $T_mu = nabla u_0$ and $T_nu = nabla u_1$ almost everywhere.

Gradient interpolation. Let $u_0, u_1$ be convex and $L$-Lipschitz on $K$, and set $f = u_0 - u_1$. For each coordinate axis $e_j$, let $A_j$ be the $(d-1)$-dimensional volume of the projection of $K$ onto the hyperplane orthogonal to $e_j$, and set $P_K = sum_{j=1}^d A_j$. Then for every $h > 0$,
$$
|nabla u_0 - nabla u_1|_{L^2(rho)}^2 leq (12 d)/(h^2) |f|_{L^2(rho)}^2 + (28 L^2 P_K)/(|K|) h.
$$
Only the almost-everywhere coordinate derivatives of $u_0$ and $u_1$ are needed.

This is the coordinate-slice version of the convex-function interpolation inequality of Delalande and Merigot, with explicit constants.

Fix $j$ and put
$$
K_{j,h} = { x in K : x + h e_j in K }, qquad D_{j,h} u(x) = (u(x + h e_j) - u(x))/(h) quad (x in K_{j,h}).
$$
Almost every nonempty coordinate slice of $K$ (a line parallel to $e_j$) is an interval $[alpha, beta]$ on which the restriction of each $u_i$ is convex and $L$-Lipschitz. Its derivative exists almost everywhere, is monotone, and has absolute value at most $L$. Fubini then gives the existence of all coordinate derivatives almost everywhere in $K$.

At a point where both $D_{j,h} u$ and $∂_j u$ exist, convexity gives
$$
0 leq delta_u(x) := D_{j,h} u(x) - ∂_j u(x) leq ∂_j u(x + h e_j) - ∂_j u(x) leq 2 L.
$$
The bounds come from the ordering of secant slopes between the one-sided derivatives at $x$ and at $x + h e_j$, and from the uniform $L$-Lipschitz bound on the derivative.

For a slice $[alpha, beta]$ of length at least $h$, cancellation of translated integrals gives
$$
int_alpha^{beta - h} ( ∂_j u(t + h) - ∂_j u(t) ) , dt
= int_{beta - h}^{beta} ∂_j u(t) , dt - int_alpha^{alpha + h} ∂_j u(t) , dt leq 2 L h.
$$
Each boundary term is bounded by $L h$. Coordinates transverse to the slice are suppressed. For a slice of length less than $h$, its allowed portion is empty. Using $delta_u^2 leq 2 L , delta_u$ and integrating over slices, then over the orthogonal projection,
$$
int_{K_{j,h}} delta_u^2 , drho leq (4 L^2 h A_j)/(|K|).
$$
The excluded portion of any slice has length at most $h$, so
$$
rho(K ∖ K_{j,h}) leq (h A_j)/(|K|).
$$

On the allowed set where both $x$ and $x + h e_j$ lie in $K$, translation invariance of Lebesgue measure and $(a - b)^2 leq 2 a^2 + 2 b^2$ give
$$
int_{K_{j,h}} |D_{j,h} f|^2 , drho leq (4)/(h^2) |f|_{L^2(rho)}^2.
$$
On the boundary strip $K ∖ K_{j,h}$, the Lipschitz bound gives $|∂_j f| leq 2 L$, so
$$
int_{K ∖ K_{j,h}} |∂_j f|^2 , drho leq 4 L^2 rho(K ∖ K_{j,h}) leq (4 L^2 h A_j)/(|K|).
$$
On $K_{j,h}$ the pointwise identity $∂_j f = D_{j,h} f - delta_{u_0} + delta_{u_1}$ together with $(a + b + c)^2 leq 3 (a^2 + b^2 + c^2)$ yields
$$
int_{K_{j,h}} |∂_j f|^2 , drho leq (12)/(h^2) |f|_{L^2(rho)}^2 + (24 L^2 h A_j)/(|K|).
$$
Summing with the boundary strip contribution gives
$$
int_K |∂_j f|^2 , drho leq (12)/(h^2) |f|_{L^2(rho)}^2 + (28 L^2 h A_j)/(|K|).
$$
Summing over $j$ and using $sum_j A_j = P_K$ proves the claimed inequality. All projection volumes $A_j$ are finite because $K$ is compact, and the argument applies to any $h > 0$, including values exceeding some or all slice lengths.

The coordinate derivatives form the full gradient almost everywhere. At an interior point $x$ where every coordinate partial of a convex function $u$ exists, set $p = (∂_j u(x))_j$ and $q = |v|_1$ for a small nonzero vector $v$. The point $x + v$ is a convex combination of $x + sgn(v_j) q e_j$ with weights $|v_j| / q$ (omitting zero weights). The one-sided derivative limits and convexity give $u(x + v) - u(x) leq p cdot v + o(q)$. Applying the same bound to $-v$ and using midpoint convexity gives the reverse, so $u$ is differentiable at $x$ with gradient $p$. Since $∂ K$ has zero volume, this applies almost everywhere to both $u_0$ and $u_1$.

The finite-case bound at exponent one-third. Set $C_0 = 162$ and
$$
C_* = ( 12 d R^2 ( 1 + √(C_0) )^2 + (28 L^2 P_K)/(|K|) )^{1/2}.
$$
Then, for any two finitely supported probabilities $mu, nu in Prob(Y)$,
$$
|T_mu - T_nu|_{L^2(rho)} leq C_* , W_2(mu, nu)^{1/3}.
$$

The proof is a direct substitution. Write $w = W_2(mu, nu)$. The finite-potential estimate provides finite-maximum potentials $u_0, u_1$ with $nabla u_0 = T_mu$ and $nabla u_1 = T_nu$ almost everywhere and
$$
|u_0 - u_1|_{L^2(rho)} leq (1 + √(C_0)) R w.
$$
If $w > 0$, apply gradient interpolation with $h = w^{2/3}$:
$$
|nabla u_0 - nabla u_1|_{L^2(rho)}^2
leq frac{12 d}{w^{4/3}} (1 + √(C_0))^2 R^2 w^2 + (28 L^2 P_K)/(|K|) w^{2/3}
= C_*^2 w^{2/3}.
$$
Taking the square root completes the finite case. If $w = 0$, then $mu = nu$ and $u_0 = u_1$ almost everywhere, so letting $h ↓ 0$ in the interpolation inequality gives $nabla u_0 = nabla u_1$ almost everywhere and the bound $C_* w^{1/3} = 0$ holds.

Extension to all Borel targets. For every $lambda in Prob(Y)$ there is a unique quadratic optimal map $T_lambda$ from $rho$ to $lambda$, up to a $rho$-null set. If $F_n subset Y$ are finite $ε_n$-nets with $ε_n to 0$ and $Q_n : Y to F_n$ are Borel nearest-point selections, then
$$
T_{(Q_n)_#lambda} → T_lambda quad in  L^2(rho).
$$

Compactness of $Y$ provides the finite nets. Enumerate each $F_n$ and choose the first closest point, obtaining a Borel selection $Q_n$ with $|Q_n(y) - y| leq ε_n$. Put $lambda_n = (Q_n)_#lambda$, discard any zero-mass sites, and let $T_n = T_{lambda_n}$. These are optimal finite-target maps by the cell calculus. The coupling induced by $y mapsto (Q_n(y), Q_m(y))$ gives
$$
W_2(lambda_n, lambda_m) leq ε_n + ε_m.
$$
By finite-target stability, $(T_n)$ is Cauchy in $L^2(rho)$. Let $S$ be the $L^2(rho)$ limit and choose a subsequence converging almost everywhere. Since $Y$ is closed, the limit lies in $Y$ almost everywhere; modifying $S$ on a null set makes it Borel with values in $Y$. For any continuous $varphi : Y to ℝ$, bounded convergence and uniform continuity give
$$
int_K varphi(S(x)) , drho(x) = lim_n int_Y varphi(Q_n(y)) , dlambda(y) = int_Y varphi(y) , dlambda(y),
$$
so $S_#rho = lambda$.

Direct optimality. Choose finite maximum potentials $u_n$ for $T_n$ and subtract a constant so $u_n(x_*) = 0$ at a fixed $x_* in K$. Slopes lie in $Y$, so each $u_n$ is $L$-Lipschitz and $|u_n| leq L , diam(K)$ on $K$. Arzela-Ascoli gives a further subsequence converging uniformly on $K$ to a convex, $L$-Lipschitz function $u$. Intersect the full-measure set on which each of the countably many supporting inequalities holds with the set where the chosen subsequence of $T_n$ converges; this intersection has full $rho$-measure. On the intersection, since each $u_n$ is a finite maximum with slopes in $Y$,
$$
u_n(z) geq u_n(x) + T_n(x) cdot (z - x) qquad (z in K).
$$
Passing to the limit gives, simultaneously for every $z in K$,
$$
u(z) geq u(x) + S(x) cdot (z - x) quad for  rho-almost every  x.
$$
Define the restricted convex conjugate $u^*(y) = max_{z in K} (z cdot y - u(z))$. It is finite and continuous, and bounded on $Y$. The same two displays give
$$
x cdot y leq u(x) + u^*(y) quad (x in K, ; y in Y), qquad
x cdot S(x) = u(x) + u^*(S(x)) quad rho-almost everywhere.
$$

For any competing map $V$ with $V_#rho = lambda$, the integral of $u(x) + u^*(V(x))$ d$rho$ is fixed by the marginals. The first inequality of the display shows that $S$ maximizes $int_K x cdot V(x) , drho(x)$. Combined with the cost identity
$$
int_K |x - V(x)|^2 , drho(x) = int_K |x|^2 , drho(x) + int_Y |y|^2 , dlambda(y) - 2 int_K x cdot V(x) , drho(x),
$$
where the first and second terms are fixed by the marginals, this identifies $S$ as an optimal map.

Uniqueness. If $V$ is another optimal map for $lambda$, the nonnegative Fenchel gap $u(x) + u^*(V(x)) - x cdot V(x)$ has zero integral, hence vanishes almost everywhere. Consequently $u(z) geq u(x) + V(x) cdot (z - x)$ for every $z in K$ at almost every $x$. On almost every interior point of $K$ the coordinate partials of $u$ exist (Fubini and convexity of $u$ along each coordinate axis); at such $x$ a supporting slope $p$ must satisfy
$$
p_j leq lim_{t ↓ 0} (u(x + t e_j) - u(x))/(t), qquad p_j geq lim_{t ↑ 0} (u(x + t e_j) - u(x))/(t).
$$
Both limits equal $∂_j u(x)$, so $V(x) = nabla u(x) = S(x)$. The boundary $∂ K$ has zero volume, so $V = S$ almost everywhere.

The same integrated inequality also handles arbitrary transport plans: equality in the Fenchel inequality forces the target coordinate to be the unique supporting slope over almost every source point, so the optimal plan is the one induced by $S$. Hence, for every $lambda in Prob(Y)$, there is a unique quadratic optimal map $T_lambda$, and $T_{(Q_n)_#lambda} to T_lambda$ in $L^2(rho)$.

Assembly. Let $mu, nu in Prob(Y)$ be arbitrary, and use the same finite nets and selections as above. Put $mu_n = (Q_n)_#mu$ and $nu_n = (Q_n)_#nu$. For any coupling $pi in Pi(mu, nu)$, its image under $(Q_n, Q_n)$ is a coupling of $mu_n$ and $nu_n$, and the triangle inequality in $L^2(pi)$ gives
$$
int |y - z|^2 , d((Q_n, Q_n)_#pi)(y, z) leq ( ( int |y - z|^2 , dpi(y,z) )^{1/2} + 2 ε_n )^2.
$$
Taking the infimum over $pi$ yields $W_2(mu_n, nu_n) leq W_2(mu, nu) + 2 ε_n$. Applying finite-target stability to $mu_n$ and $nu_n$,
$$
|T_{mu_n} - T_{nu_n}|_{L^2(rho)} leq C_* ( W_2(mu, nu) + 2 ε_n )^{1/3}.
$$
Since $T_{mu_n} to T_mu$ and $T_{nu_n} to T_nu$ in $L^2(rho)$, passing to the limit gives
$$
|T_mu - T_nu|_{L^2(rho)} leq C_* , W_2(mu, nu)^{1/3}.
$$

The constant $C_*$ depends only on $K$ and the enclosing radius $L$ for $Y$. It is independent of atom counts, masses, site separations, and density bounds. No convexity of $Y$ is required: the site interpolation stays in $B(0, L)$ and the discretization takes values in $Y$. The construction covers the extreme case of a singleton $Y$, for which every target map coincides with the constant map.

The finite-target bound, the limiting extension, and the sharpness example together establish the two claims stated at the outset: the exponent $1/3$ is admissible with a uniform constant, and no larger exponent is. This closes the gap between the $W_1^{1/4}$ estimate that Merigot records for the nonuniform $L^1$ setting and the $W_2^{1/3}$ optimal rate in the quadratic setting.
