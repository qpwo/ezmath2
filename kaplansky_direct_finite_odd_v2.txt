\begin{abstract}
Kaplansky conjectured in 1972 that the group algebra of any group
over any field would be ``directly finite'': if $ab=1$ then $ba=1$.
The conjecture is true when the field has characteristic zero, but it
fails in positive characteristic once the group is allowed to be
infinite. This paper constructs a counterexample in odd characteristic.
We exhibit one explicit odd prime~$p$, a field~$K$ of order~$p^4$,
a finitely generated group~$G$ containing torsion, and two finite
sums $a,b\in K[G]$ with $ab=1$ and $ba\ne 1$. The same two sums then
produce an injective cellular automaton on~$K^G$ that is not surjective,
so the group~$G$ is not surjunctive.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The puzzle: one-sided versus two-sided inversion}

Let $K$ be a field and $G$ a group. The \emph{group algebra} $K[G]$
consists of all formal finite sums
\[
 c_1 g_1 + c_2 g_2 + \cdots + c_r g_r,
 \qquad c_i\in K,\ g_i\in G,
\]
added coordinate by coordinate and multiplied by writing out both sides,
using the group law $g_ig_j$ and extending linearly. Two sums are equal
only when their coefficients agree on every~$g$. So $K[G]$ is like an
infinite-dimensional vector space over $K$, with $G$ itself as a basis,
plus a multiplication rule coming from the group.

Concrete example. When $G=\mathbb Z$ and $K$ is any field, the
group algebra $K[\mathbb Z]$ is (isomorphic to) the ring of Laurent
polynomials $K[x,x^{-1}]$ in one variable: send the integer~$n$ to~$x^n$.
That ring is commutative, so left and right behave the same there.
Not every group algebra is commutative; for example, if $G$ is
nonabelian, then $K[G]$ is noncommutative.

A ring $R$ is \emph{directly finite} when the equation $ab=1$, for
$a,b\in R$, always implies~$ba=1$. Fields are directly finite (the
only nonzero element with a multiplicative inverse is the obvious one).
The $n\times n$ matrix ring $\Mat_n(K)$ over a field is directly finite
because linear maps on an $n$-dimensional vector space have finite
dimension as input and output. In general, any finite-dimensional
algebra over a field is directly finite: any one-sided inverse between
linear maps on an $n$-dimensional space has rank~$n$, so it is
automatically a two-sided inverse. A ring in which every matrix ring
$\Mat_n(R)$, $n\ge1$, is directly finite is called \emph{stably finite}.

Since a finite group $G$ produces a finite-dimensional algebra $K[G]$,
it is directly finite (and stably finite; the matrix rings $\Mat_n(K[G])$
are again finite-dimensional). So the interesting question is about
infinite groups.

\subsection{Kaplansky's question}

In his 1972 book on fields and rings, Kaplansky proved that when the
field has characteristic~$0$, the group algebra $K[G]$ is directly finite
for every group~$G$; his proof used finite von Neumann algebras. He then
asked whether the same conclusion holds in characteristic~$p>0$, where
his von Neumann-algebra method does not apply directly.

A large body of later work found positive partial answers. Ara, O'Meara,
and Perera (2002) proved stable finiteness of $K[G]$ for $G$ free-by-amenable
over any division ring. Elek and Szab\'o (2004) proved stable finiteness
for every sofic group: informally, a group that can be approximated by
finite permutation models. Since many familiar infinite groups are
sofic -- all residually finite groups, all amenable groups, all free
groups, and many more -- this covered a wide range of cases.
These theorems constrain any counterexample: whatever group we build
must be far from sofic.

The purpose of this paper is to give an actual counterexample, in a
single specific odd characteristic.

\subsection{The main theorem}

Put $m=\binom{1200}{600}$ and let $p$ be the least integer $>1$ that
divides $(m!)^2+1$. This $p$ is an odd prime; see Lemma~\ref{lem:field}.
We construct:

\begin{itemize}
\item a field $K$ of order $p^4$ (an explicit quadratic tower over the
      prime field $\F_p$);
\item a finitely generated group $G$ containing nontrivial elements of
      finite order;
\item two finite sums $a,b\in K[G]$ with $ab=1$ and $ba\ne 1$.
\end{itemize}

The construction is entirely finite: every field element, every group
generator, every coefficient is produced by a finite list of operations
from fixed finite sets. No infinite search and no word-problem oracle
is needed to define $a,b$; the point of the construction is to build them
from finite data rather than by deciding equalities in~$G$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $m=\binom{1200}{600}$ and let $p$ be the least integer $h>1$
dividing $(m!)^2+1$. Then $p$ is an odd prime. There is a specified
field $K$ of order $p^4$, a specified finitely generated group $G$
containing torsion, and specified sums $a,b\in K[G]$ satisfying
$ab=1$ but $ba\ne 1$. In particular Kaplansky's direct-finiteness
conjecture fails in odd characteristic.
\end{theorem}

The word ``specified'' means: written as an explicit composition of
finite operations on finite sets, in an order that is fixed in advance.
Different coefficients may happen to coincide at the end; that is
harmless. What matters is that $a$ and $b$ are objective consequences
of a finite recipe, not of an arbitrary choice among infinitely many.

\subsection{Why the failure of direct finiteness matters}

Two remarks connect this algebra to other topics. First, by
Elek--Szab\'o, the constructed group $G$ cannot be sofic; otherwise
$K[G]$ would be stably finite, contradicting the theorem. So the
construction is also a source of non-sofic groups of a very specific
kind.

Second, direct finiteness is equivalent to a statement about ``linear
cellular automata.'' A cellular automaton on $K^G$ is any transformation
that reads a finite window of the configuration around each point and
writes a new value there. If we take, for a fixed finite sum
$c=\sum c_u u\in K[G]$, the rule
\[
 (T_c x)(g) = \sum_u c_u\, x(gu),
\]
then $T_c$ is always a cellular automaton (the memory set is $\supp c$).
Composition behaves well: $T_c T_d = T_{cd}$. So if $ab=1$, then
$T_b$ has left inverse $T_a$, hence is injective. If $ba\ne 1$, we can
show $T_b$ is not surjective on $K^G$. Section~\ref{sec:cellular} gives
the details. The upshot is a group that fails to be \emph{surjunctive},
refuting Gottschalk's conjecture for this particular~$G$.

\subsection{The plan of the proof}

The construction has five independent pieces. Each piece is fairly
self-contained; the reader may take them in whatever order feels best,
as long as the final step receives all the pieces.

\emph{Piece 1 (Section~\ref{sec:finite}): finite data.}
We fix the prime $p$, the field $K=\F_{p^4}$ (built as a quadratic
tower so we can descend to $\F_p$ for checks), and a simple triangle-free
graph~$\Gamma$ on $m$ vertices with a factorization of its adjacency
matrix into a very short sum of rank-one pieces. We also define a finite
group $H=A_0\rtimes P$ of order $w$ that will be the local block.

\emph{Piece 2 (Section~\ref{sec:modular}): a large idempotent.}
Inside the group algebra $K[H]$, we build an explicit idempotent~$e$
whose regular rank (the dimension of $eK[H]$ as a $K$-vector space)
satisfies $\dim_K eK[H]/|H| > 1/96$. It also satisfies two opposite
annihilations: writing $u(s),v(s)$ for certain elements of~$H$ and
$\ell=(p-1)/2$,
\[
 (u(s)-1)^\ell e = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad e\,(v(s)-1)^\ell = 0
 \quad\text{for every } s.
\]
The asymmetry is the point: the annihilation on the left and the
annihilation on the right use the same exponent~$\ell$, but $u$ and $v$
sit in different subgroups of~$H$, so no symmetry forces them to be
compatible.

\emph{Piece 3 (Section~\ref{sec:gluing}): gluing without collapsing.}
We take $m$ copies $H_1,\ldots,H_m$ of $H$ and identify one specific
cyclic subgroup across each edge of~$\Gamma$. The resulting quotient
$G_0$ must remain injective on each $H_i$; if it did not, a nonzero
element of $K[H_i]$ could vanish in $K[G_0]$. A short-and-sweet
spherical-map argument proves this injectivity.

\emph{Piece 4 (Section~\ref{sec:sector}): turning ranks into matrices.}
Enlarge $G_0$ to a bigger group $G_1$ whose group algebra contains a
nonzero central idempotent $f$ with special properties. In the ring
$fK[G_1]$, the images of the idempotents $e_i$ become rectangular
matrix blocks with \emph{exact} integer-scaled right and left inverses.
This is the technical heart of the paper: a nilpotent-ideal
change-of-basis argument turns dimension equalities over~$K$ into
equalities over the full ring.

\emph{Piece 5 (Sections~\ref{sec:compression} and~\ref{sec:scalar}):
matrices, then scalars, then a cellular automaton.}
Using the triangle-free condition and the deliberate asymmetry from
Piece~2, we assemble all the rectangular blocks into two matrices with
one-sided inverse. Then the finite Heisenberg group from Section~\ref{sec:scalar}
embeds a matrix algebra into a group algebra, letting us turn that
matrix identity into an identity between two scalar elements. Section~\ref{sec:cellular}
translates it into a statement about cellular automata.

\subsection{Notation summary}

All matrices below act on column vectors in free \emph{right} modules
$S^n$. So multiplying a matrix by a column vector means multiplying
each entry on the left by the corresponding group algebra element.
This matters because group algebras are not always commutative.

\begin{itemize}
\item $K$: a field. $\F_p$: the prime field of order $p$.
\item $G$: a group; $K[G]$: the set of finite $K$-linear sums of
      elements of $G$, with multiplication from the group law.
\item $1_K$, or just $1$: the multiplicative identity of $K$. In $K[G]$
      the identity is the group identity element, written $1_G$ or~$1$.
\item $e\in K[H]$: an idempotent, meaning $e^2=e$.
\item $d=\dim_K e K[H]$: the \emph{regular rank} of $e$, i.e., the
      dimension of the image of left multiplication by~$e$ on $K[H]$.
\item $w=|H|$: the order of the finite group~$H$.
\item $[n]$: $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ for a positive integer $n$.
\item Hermitian transpose: never used here. Ordinary transpose is
      written $X^{\mathsf T}$, with no scalar conjugation.
\item $\Mat_{a\times b}(S)$: $a\times b$ matrices with entries in~$S$.
\end{itemize}

\section{The field, the graph, and the local group}
\label{sec:finite}

The finite-data stage has three independent ingredients: a prime~$p$ and
a specific field $K$ of order $p^4$; a simple triangle-free graph~$\Gamma$
on $m$ vertices together with an extremely compact factorization of its
adjacency matrix; and a finite group $H$ with a natural action that will
later host the crucial idempotent. Each ingredient is built once and for
all here, in the order needed later.

\subsection{The prime and the field}
\label{subsec:field}

Recall $m=\binom{1200}{600}$, a specific (very large) positive integer,
and let $p$ be the least integer $h>1$ that divides $(m!)^2+1$.
The definition of~$p$ is purely arithmetic. We need four properties
of~$p$ and one field-extension construction.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:field}
With $m=\binom{1200}{600}$ and $p$ the least $h>1$ dividing
$(m!)^2+1$, we have:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $p$ is an odd prime, and $p>m$.
\item $p$ divides $(m!)^2+1$, so $-1$ has a square root in $\F_p$.
\item If $\eta\in\F_p^*$ is any nonsquare, then the polynomial
      $X^2-\eta$ is irreducible over $\F_p$ and its root field
      $K_0=\F_p[\xi]/(\xi^2-\eta)$ is a quadratic extension of $\F_p$.
\item The root $\xi$ of $X^2-\eta$ is \emph{not} a square in $K_0$,
      so $X^2-\xi$ is irreducible over $K_0$ and
      $K=K_0[\nu]/(\nu^2-\xi)$ is a further quadratic extension.
      Consequently $|K|=p^4$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) The least divisor $>1$ of any integer $n>1$ is prime: if it were
composite, one of its proper divisors would also be $>1$ and would be
a smaller divisor of~$n$. So $p$ is prime. Now, if $p\le m$, then
$p$ would divide $m!$ (since $m\ge p$), so also $p$ would divide
$(m!)^2$; combined with $p\mid (m!)^2+1$ this gives $p\mid 1$,
impossible. Hence $p>m>600$. Since $m\ge600\ge1$ and $p>m\ge600$,
$m!$ is even (as $m\ge2$), so $(m!)^2+1$ is odd, and $p\ne2$.
Therefore $p$ is odd.

(2) The congruence $(m!)^2\equiv -1\pmod p$ says that $-1$ is the
square of $m!$ modulo~$p$, that is, $-1$ is a quadratic residue in
$\F_p$.

(3) A polynomial $X^2-\eta$ over a field is irreducible exactly when
$\eta$ is not a square in that field. Nonsquares exist in $\F_p^*$
for every odd prime~$p$: the map $x\mapsto x^2$ on $\F_p^*$ is
two-to-one on its image, so only half of the $p-1$ elements are
squares, and $p-1\ge2$ gives at least one nonsquare. For such~$\eta$
the polynomial $X^2-\eta$ has no roots in $\F_p$, hence is irreducible
and $\F_p[X]/(X^2-\eta)$ is a field of $p^2$ elements.

(4) In $K_0=\F_p[X]/(X^2-\eta)$, the two roots of $X^2-\eta$ are
$\xi$ and $-\xi$. Therefore $\xi\cdot(-\xi)=-\xi^2=-\eta$ is the
field norm of $\xi$ down to $\F_p$. By (2), $-1$ is a square in $\F_p$.
Since $\eta$ is chosen to be a nonsquare, the product $-\eta$ is a
product of a square and a nonsquare, hence a nonsquare in $\F_p$.

Now suppose, toward a contradiction, that $\xi=y^2$ for some $y\in K_0$.
Apply the field norm $N_{K_0/\F_p}(u)=u\cdot u'$ (where $u'$ is the
conjugate, negating the $\xi$-coordinate). Because $N$ is multiplicative,
$N(\xi)=N(y^2)=N(y)^2$, which would exhibit the nonsquare~$-\eta$
as a square in $\F_p$. That is impossible. So $\xi$ is not a square
in~$K_0$, and hence $X^2-\xi$ is irreducible over~$K_0$. The quotient
$K=K_0[\nu]/(\nu^2-\xi)$ is therefore a field of $|K_0|^2=p^4$ elements.
\end{proof}

From now on, $K$ is the specific field $K=\F_{p^4}$ supplied by
Lemma~\ref{lem:field}. We descend to $\F_p$ only when we need to check
things like ``$-1$ is a square.'' All our formulas over $K$ are
already fixed.

Several additional choices are needed below. Because we want the
construction to be ``specified'' in the sense of the introduction,
we spell out an explicit finite recipe for each choice.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Representatives and order on $\F_p$.}
      Identify $\F_p$ with $\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}$ using the remainder
      in $0,\ldots,p-1$, so that $\F_p$ has a natural total order.
      Choose $\eta$ as the least nonsquare in $\F_p^*$ under this order.
\item \emph{Representatives and order on $K$.}
      Write every element of $K$ uniquely as
      $c_0+c_1\xi+c_2\nu+c_3\xi\nu$ with $c_j\in\F_p$. Order $K$ by
      the lexicographic order on $(c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3)$. This gives a
      total order on $K$.
\item \emph{A generator $\zeta$ of $K^*$.} Put $M=p^4-1$.
      Choose the least element $\zeta$ of $K^*$ whose multiplicative
      order equals $M$. Such an element exists; the reasoning is
      standard but we include it for completeness below.
\end{itemize}

Why does an element of order $M$ exist? Let $b$ be the least common
multiple of all element orders in $K^*$. Conventionally $b\le M$ because
every element of $K^*$ has order at most $M$. On the other hand, every
element of $K^*$ is a root of $X^b-1$ (because each element's order
divides~$b$), so $X^b-1$ has at least $M$ distinct roots. A nonzero
polynomial over a field has at most as many roots as its degree, so
$M\le b$. Hence $b=M$. Now, by the definition of least common multiple,
we can choose elements of $K^*$ whose orders are the prime powers
occurring in the factorization of $M$; raising appropriates elements to
the right powers gives an element of order exactly each prime power
factor. Their product, being the product of commuting elements of
pairwise coprime orders, has order exactly $M$. So an element $\zeta$
of order $M$ exists.

In particular $M=p^4-1$ and all of its powers are invertible in $K$
(equivalently: the multiplicative group of $K$ is cyclic of order
$M=p^4-1$). We will divide by various integers below; every such
integer is a factor of~$M$ and hence nonzero in~$K$.

\subsection{The graph and the low-rank fit}
\label{subsec:graph}

The second finite ingredient is a simple triangle-free graph~$\Gamma$
on many vertices whose adjacency matrix admits a factorization into
a number of rank-one pieces that is very small compared to the number
of vertices. That is what allows Section~\ref{op:compression-section}
to build a ``rank paradox'' with which the odd-characteristic
counterexample becomes possible.

Set $[1200]=\{1,2,\ldots,1200\}$. Enumerate all six-hundred-element
subsets $I\subseteq[1200]$ in increasing lexicographic order (comparing
subsets as increasing lists of integers) and denote them
$I_1,\ldots,I_m$ with $m=\binom{1200}{600}=\binom{1200}{600}$. Remember
this same~$m$ is the one appearing in the definition of~$p$.

Define a simple graph $\Gamma$ on vertex set $\{1,\ldots,m\}$ by
declaring the distinct vertices $i\ne j$ to be adjacent exactly when
the intersection $I_i\cap I_j$ has fewer than $200$ elements:
\[
 i\sim j \quad\text{iff}\quad |I_i\cap I_j| < 200.
\]
By convention a vertex is never adjacent to itself, so the graph is
simple.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:graph}
The graph $\Gamma$ is simple and triangle-free. Let
\[
 \mathcal T=\{T\subseteq[1200]:|T|\le 400\},\qquad
 t=|\mathcal T|=\sum_{j=0}^{400}\binom{1200}{j}.
\]
There exist coefficients $\alpha_i(T)$ and $\beta_j(T)$ in $\F_p$,
indexed by $T\in\mathcal T$, such that the $m\times m$ matrix
$F$ given by
\[
 F_{ij}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal T}\alpha_i(T)\,\beta_j(T)
\]
satisfies $F_{ii}=1$ for every $i$ and $F_{ij}=0$ for every pair of
distinct nonadjacent vertices~$i,j$. Moreover $t/m<1/100$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Triangle-free.} Suppose $i,j,k$ are pairwise adjacent. Then each
of the three pairwise intersections has at most $199$ elements (since
``fewer than $200$'' and integer valued). So
\[
 |I_i\cup I_j\cup I_k| \ge |I_i|+|I_j|+|I_k| - |I_i\cap I_j| - |I_j\cap I_k| - |I_k\cap I_i|
 \ge 3\cdot 600 - 3\cdot 199 = 1203,
\]
by inclusion-exclusion. But the union is contained in $[1200]$, which
has only $1200$ elements. Contradiction. So $\Gamma$ has no triangles.

\emph{The fit.} Pick, for each $i$, the indicator function
\[
 \alpha_i(T)=\begin{cases}1,&T\subseteq I_i,\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
To build $\beta_j$, define the following polynomial in the formal
variables $X_1,\ldots,X_{1200}$ over $\F_p$:
\[
 Q_j(X)=\prod_{k=200}^{599}\frac{\sum_{x\in I_j}X_x-k}{600-k}.
\]
Every denominator lies in $\{1,2,\ldots,400\}$, hence is invertible
in $\F_p$ because~$p>600>400$. Expand $Q_j$ as $\sum_\alpha c_\alpha X^\alpha$
with $c_\alpha\in\F_p$ and multi-indices $\alpha\in\mathbb N^{[1200]}$
(so $X^\alpha=\prod_x X_x^{\alpha_x}$). Define the \emph{square-free
reduction} of $Q_j$ to be
\[
 Q_j^{\mathrm{sf}}(X)=\sum_\alpha c_\alpha\prod_{x:\alpha_x>0} X_x,
\]
in which every positive exponent has been replaced by $1$. Set
$\beta_j(T)$ to be the coefficient of $\prod_{x\in T}X_x$ in
$Q_j^{\mathrm{sf}}$ for each $T\subseteq[1200]$. Because $Q_j$ has
total degree $400$ (a product of $400$ linear forms), only the
$T\in\mathcal T$ can appear. The total degree of $Q_j$ bounds the
support of beta_j exactly to subsets of size at most 400.

\emph{Evaluation on $0$-$1$ vectors.} The square-free reduction does
not change the value of the polynomial at any $0$-$1$ point (because
$0^n=0$ and $1^n=1$ for every $n\ge1$). In particular, evaluate at the
indicator vector $1_{I_i}\in\{0,1\}^{[1200]}$ (entry $1$ at positions
$x\in I_i$, entry $0$ elsewhere). On the one hand,
\[
 Q_j(1_{I_i})
 =\prod_{k=200}^{599}\frac{|I_i\cap I_j|-k}{600-k}.
\]
On the other hand, expanding $Q_j^{\mathrm{sf}}$ and evaluating at
$1_{I_i}$, each monomial $\prod_{x\in T}X_x$ contributes $1$ if
$T\subseteq I_i$ and $0$ otherwise. So
\[
 Q_j(1_{I_i})
 =\sum_{T\in\mathcal T}\alpha_i(T)\,\beta_j(T)=F_{ij}.
\]
Equating the two evaluations gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold-F}
 F_{ij}=\prod_{k=200}^{599}\frac{|I_i\cap I_j|-k}{600-k}.
\end{equation}

\emph{Fit conditions.} If $i=j$ then $|I_i\cap I_j|=600$, so every
numerator $600-k$ cancels the corresponding denominator, giving
$F_{ii}=1$. If $i\ne j$ are nonadjacent then $|I_i\cap I_j|\ge200$ and
$\le 599$, so the factor with $k=|I_i\cap I_j|$ is exactly zero,
forcing $F_{ij}=0$. For adjacent distinct pairs the product is
generally nonzero, but we do not need any particular value; only the
diagonal and the nonedge vanishing matter. This is the threshold-Kneser
polynomial specialization of \cite[Proposition~13]{Haviv2018Theta}
and \cite[Theorem~3.2]{GolovnevHaviv2022}.

\emph{The ratio bound.} Combinatorial estimates. The binomial
coefficients $\binom{1200}{j}$ increase with~$j$ as long as $j\le599$
(because $\binom{n}{j+1}/\binom{n}{j}=(n-j)/(j+1)>1$ when $j<(n-1)/2$
and $n=1200$). So on the sum defining $t$, the largest term is at
$j=400$, and
\[
 t \le 401\binom{1200}{400}.
\]
For $m=\binom{1200}{600}$ we can compare factorials directly:
\[
 \frac{\binom{1200}{400}}{\binom{1200}{600}}
 =\prod_{u=401}^{600}\frac{u}{u+200}
 \le \left(\tfrac 57\right)^{100}.
\]
Here the inequality is a crude but adequate estimate: for
$u\le 500$, the factor $u/(u+200)$ is at most $500/700=5/7$; for
$u\ge501$, the factor is at most $600/800=3/4<1$, so it contributes
less than one. Multiplying all one hundred factors gives the displayed
bound. Then
\[
 \frac tm\le 401\left(\tfrac 57\right)^{100}.
\]
The cube of $5/7$ is $125/343<1/2$, so $(5/7)^{99}<(1/2)^{33}$. Thus
$ (5/7)^{100}<(1/2)^{33} $ as well. Multiplying by $401<40100<2^{33}$
gives
\[
 \frac tm<2^{33}\cdot \frac1{2^{33}}=1,
\]
which is weaker than needed; more precisely,
\[
 100 \cdot \frac tm < 40100\left(\tfrac 57\right)^{100}
 <\frac{40100}{2^{33}}<1,
\]
so $t/m<1/100$.
\end{proof}

Now we have our two finite combinatorial ratios. The first is
$t/m<1/100$: the low-rank fit uses far fewer summands than there are
vertices. The second, coming from the group $H$ below, is
$d/w>1/96$: the idempotent each vertex carries has a regular rank
that is a fixed nonzero fraction of~$|H|$. Section~\ref{op:compression-section}
will combine the two ratios into a matrix with strict inequality
$wt<md$ between its shapes.

\subsection{The finite group $H$}
\label{subsec:group-H}

The third finite ingredient is the local group. It is where the large
idempotent lives, and it is where the crucial ``one-sided annihilation
in each direction'' happens.

Let $P=(\F_p)^3$ be the additive group of three-dimensional column
vectors over $\F_p$. It has $p^3$ elements. Let $M=p^4-1$, as in the
field construction, and let
\[
 A_0=(\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z)^P
\]
be the group of \emph{functions} $a:P\to\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z$, with
addition defined pointwise: $(a+a')(x)=a(x)+a'(x)$ for each $x\in P$.
This is a finite abelian group of order $M^{p^3}$.

The group $P$ acts on $A_0$ by \emph{translation}: for $s\in P$ and
$a\in A_0$, define $s\cdot a\in A_0$ by
\[
 (s\cdot a)(x)=a(x-s), \qquad x\in P.
\]
This is a group action: $0\cdot a=a$ and $(s+t)\cdot a=s\cdot(t\cdot a)$.
Form the semidirect product
\[
 H=A_0\rtimes P, \qquad
 (a,s)(a',s')=(a+s\cdot a',\ s+s').
\]
The identity of $H$ is $(0,0)$. The inverse of $(a,s)$ is
$(-s^{-1}\cdot a, -s)$, i.e., $(-(-s)\cdot a,-s)$ since in characteristic
$p$ subtraction is the same as~$+(-s)$. Concretely: $(a,s)(-(-s)\cdot a, -s)=(a-s\cdot((-s)\cdot a), 0)=(a-s\cdot(-s\cdot a), 0)=(a-0,0)=(0,0)$.
The order of $H$ is
\[
 w:=|H|=|A_0|\cdot|P|=M^{p^3}\cdot p^3.
\]
So $H$ is finite. The group algebra $K[H]$ is thus a
finite-dimensional algebra over $K$, of dimension $w$ as a $K$-vector
space (and $w^2$ as a $K$-algebra of linear maps on $K^w$).

The two subgroups of $H$ that matter now are $U$ and $V$, defined
below. Both are isomorphic to $P$, but they sit inside $H$ in different
ways.

\emph{Translation subgroup.} Define
\[
 u(s)=(0,s),\qquad U=\{u(s):s\in P\}\subseteq H.
\]
Because $(0,s)(0,s')=(0,s+s')$, the map $s\mapsto u(s)$ is an
isomorphism $P\to U$.

\emph{Characteristic element.} Let $c\in A_0$ be the function
\[
 c(0)=1,\quad c(x)=0\text{ for }x\ne0.
\]
The group law gives $(c,0)$ the exact order $M$ in $H$ (as an element
of the abelian normal subgroup $A_0$).

\emph{Conjugate subgroup.} Define
\[
 v(s)=c\,u(s)\,c^{-1},\qquad V=\{v(s):s\in P\}\subseteq H,
\]
where $c$ in the formula denotes $(c,0)\in H$ and $c^{-1}=(-c,0)$
uses the additive inverse in $A_0$. A short computation with the
group law in $H$ gives the explicit form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v(s)-formula}
 v(s)=(c-s\cdot c,\ s),\qquad s\in P.
\end{equation}
Indeed $(c,0)(0,s)=(c,s)$ and $(c,s)(-c,0)=(c+s\cdot(-c),\,s)=(c-s\cdot c,s)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:UV-triv}
The subgroups $U$ and $V$ satisfy $U\cap V=\{1\}$, and $U\cong V\cong P$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $(0,0)=u(s)=v(s')$ for some $s,s'\in P$. Comparing second
coordinates gives $s=s'$. Comparing first coordinates gives
$0=c-s\cdot c$, i.e., $c=s\cdot c$. The left side evaluates at $0$ to
$c(0)=1$; the right side evaluates at $0$ to $(s\cdot c)(0)=c(-s)$.
But $-s\ne 0$ when $s\ne0$, and $c$ vanishes away from the origin, so
$c(-s)=0$. Thus $s=0$, and the only common element is the identity.
The isomorphism statements follow from the displayed definitions:
$s\mapsto u(s)$ is a bijection $P\to U$ preserving the group law, and
$s\mapsto v(s)$ is a conjugate of~$s\mapsto u(s)$ inside the group~$H$,
hence also isomorphic to $P$.
\end{proof}

Why does this matter? In Section~\ref{sec:modular} we shall build an
idempotent~$e$ with
\[
 (u(s)-1)^\ell e = 0,\qquad e\,(v(s)-1)^\ell=0
 \quad\text{for every }s\in P,
\]
where $\ell=(p-1)/2$. The first annihilation uses $u(s)\in U$ on the
left; the second uses $v(s)\in V$ on the right. Because $U\cap V=\{1\}$,
the two sides do not have to agree, and no symmetry forces either one
onto the other. This asymmetry is what makes the later triangular
compression work.

That completes the finite data. In later sections, we always write
$m=\binom{1200}{600}$, $t=|\mathcal T|$, $w=|H|$, and
$d=\dim_KeK[H]$ for the regular rank of the idempotent $e$ we
are about to build in Section~\ref{sec:modular}.

\section{The large idempotent with ordered annihilations}
\label{sec:modular}

This section is the technical heart of the paper. In the previous
section we built three pieces of finite data: a prime $p$, a field
$K$ of order~$p^4$, and a finite group $H=A_0\rtimes P$ with two
different subgroups $U,V\cong P$ meeting trivially. Now we use all that
data to build one particular idempotent $e\in K[H]$ with two properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Large regular rank.} The dimension $d=\dim_K\bigl(eK[H]\bigr)$
      of the image of left multiplication by $e$ satisfies
      $d/w>1/96$, where $w=|H|$.
\item \emph{Opposite annihilations.} Writing $\ell=(p-1)/2$, we have
      $(u(s)-1)^\ell e=0$ and $e\,(v(s)-1)^\ell=0$ for every $s\in P$.
\end{itemize}

The construction proceeds in three stages.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \S\ref{subsec:B-def}: identify a subspace $B\subseteq K^P$ on
      which both $u(s)$-shifts and $v(s)$-shifts have the same
      annihilation behavior. This requires a piece of translation
      calculus (the operators $X_j$) and care with degrees.
\item \S\ref{subsec:admissibility}: use the polynomial identity test of
      Schwartz and Zippel to produce many ``admissible'' characters
      $\lambda\in\widehat{A_0}$, i.e., characters $\lambda$ for which
      a certain Gram matrix~$N_\lambda$ is nonsingular.
\item \S\ref{subsec:idempotent}: assemble these ingredients into an
      idempotent $e$ spread over many matrix-block summands of $K[H]$,
      compute its regular rank exactly, and verify the annihilations.
\end{enumerate}

A last subsection (\S\ref{subsec:edge-zero}) turns the above
annihilations into the ``edge products vanish'' identity used later.

Throughout, we do all linear algebra over~$K$ first, then lift to
$K[H]$ using the Fourier idempotents of the abelian normal subgroup
$A_0\subseteq H$.

\subsection{The annihilated subspace $B\subseteq K^P$}
\label{subsec:B-def}

Let $\ell=(p-1)/2$ and $D=3(p-1)$. Work in the $K$-vector space $K^P$
of functions $P\to K$, with standard basis $\mathbf e_x$ (a $1$ at
$x$ and $0$ elsewhere). We want a linear subspace on which left shifts
and right shifts both annihilate $\ell$-fold.

\emph{Translation calculus.} For $s\in P$ let $L_s:K^P\to K^P$ be the
shift operator $L_s\mathbf e_x=\mathbf e_{x+s}$. Define three operators
\[
 X_j=L_{\varepsilon_j}-1_{K^P}, \qquad j=1,2,3,
\]
where $\varepsilon_j$ are the standard generators of $P=\F_p^3$.

The $L_s$ commute because the group $P$ is abelian. So the $X_j$ commute
as well. A stronger fact: because we are in characteristic~$p$, the
binomial coefficients $\binom pk$ are divisible by $p$ for $1\le k\le p-1$;
so $(1+X_j)^p=1+X_j^p$ and
\[
 X_j^p=(L_{\varepsilon_j}-1)^p = L_{p\varepsilon_j}-1 = L_0-1 = 0.
\]
Thus each $X_j$ is nilpotent of index at most $p$. These are the
``differential operators of order 1'' associated to the three
generators of $P$.

\begin{lemma}[Monomial basis]\label{lem:monomial-basis}
The $p^3$ vectors
\[
 X_1^{n_1}X_2^{n_2}X_3^{n_3}\mathbf e_0, \qquad 0\le n_1,n_2,n_3< p,
\]
form a $K$-basis of $K^P$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The standard basis $\{\mathbf e_x:x\in P\}$ already has cardinality
$p^3=\dim_KK^P$. So it suffices to show every $\mathbf e_s$ is in the
$K$-span of the displayed vectors.

Fix $s=(s_1,s_2,s_3)\in P$. Since $1+X_j=L_{\varepsilon_j}$, we have
$(1+X_j)^{s_j}=L_{s_j\varepsilon_j}$ and
\[
 \mathbf e_s=L_s\mathbf e_0
 =\prod_{j=1}^3 L_{s_j\varepsilon_j}\mathbf e_0
 =\prod_{j=1}^3 (1+X_j)^{s_j}\mathbf e_0
 =\prod_{j=1}^3 \left(\sum_{k=0}^{s_j}\binom{s_j}{k}X_j^k\right)\mathbf e_0.
\]
The last product expands to a $K$-linear combination of the vectors
$X_1^{n_1}X_2^{n_2}X_3^{n_3}\mathbf e_0$ with $0\le n_j\le s_j<p$.
So $\mathbf e_s$ lies in the span of the display. Since $\{\mathbf e_s\}$
spans, the display spans, and it has $p^3$ elements, so it is a basis.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The lemma can be re-read as ``polynomials in $X_1,X_2,X_3$ of degree
$<p$ in each variable are a basis of $K^P$''. This is precisely the
finite-dimensional analogue of the polynomial ring $K[X_1,X_2,X_3]$,
truncated by $X_j^p=0$.
\end{remark}

Define $B$ as the matrix with columns the basis vectors whose total
degree is large enough:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:B-def}
 B := \bigl[X_1^{n_1}X_2^{n_2}X_3^{n_3}\mathbf e_0\bigr]_{
 \substack{0\le n_1,n_2,n_3<p\\ n_1+n_2+n_3\ge D-\ell+1}}.
\end{equation}
So $B$ is a $p^3\times h$ matrix with $K$-linearly independent columns
(they are a subset of a basis). The integer $h$ counts the columns.

\begin{lemma}[Two-sided annihilation of $B$]\label{lem:B-annihilation}
For every $s\in P$,
\[
 (L_s-1)^\ell\,B=0,\qquad B^{\mathsf T}(L_s-1)^\ell=0.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{First identity.} Fix $s\in P$ and write $s=\sum_j s_j\varepsilon_j$.
Expand $L_s-\id_{K^P}$:
\[
 L_s-1 = \prod_{j=1}^3 L_{s_j\varepsilon_j} - 1
 = \prod_{j=1}^3 (1+X_j)^{s_j} - 1.
\]
Expanding the right side, every nonzero monomial has total $X$-degree
at least one (because the constant term $1$ cancels). Therefore the
$\ell$th power $(L_s-1)^\ell$ has the property: applied to any monomial
of total $X$-degree $d$, the result lies in the $K$-span of monomials
of total $X$-degree at least $d+\ell$.

Now take a column $X_1^{n_1}X_2^{n_2}X_3^{n_3}\mathbf e_0$ of $B$,
with $d=n_1+n_2+n_3\ge D-\ell+1$. Applying $(L_s-1)^\ell$ gives a
combination of monomials of total degree at least $D+1$. But every
monomial of total degree greater than $D=3(p-1)$ has some exponent
at least $p$, and $X_j^p=0$ kills it. So each column is annihilated,
proving the first identity.

\emph{Second identity.} Compute the transpose of $L_s$: its matrix entry
$(L_s)_{\mathbf e_x,\mathbf e_y}$ (row $x$, column $y$) is $1$ when
$x+s=y$ and $0$ otherwise. Hence $(L_s^{\mathsf T})_{\mathbf e_y,\mathbf e_x}$
is $1$ when $x-s=y$, so $L_s^{\mathsf T}=L_{-s}$. Consequently
$(L_s-1)^{\mathsf T}=L_{-s}-1$, and
\[
 B^{\mathsf T}(L_s-1)^\ell = \bigl((L_{-s}-1)^\ell B\bigr)^{\mathsf T} = 0^{\mathsf T}=0,
\]
using the first identity with $-s$ in place of $s$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Number of columns]\label{lem:h}
The integer $h$ equals $\binom{\ell+2}{3}$, and
\[
 \frac{h}{p^3}=\frac{(p-1)(p+1)(p+3)}{48\,p^3}>\frac1{48}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Make the substitution $a_j=p-1-n_j$, $j=1,2,3$. Then $a_j\ge0$ and
$\sum a_j=D-\sum n_j$. The condition $\sum n_j\ge D-\ell+1$ becomes
$\sum a_j\le \ell-1$. Each $a_j\le p-1$ is automatic, because
$\ell-1=(p-1)/2-1<p-1$ forces $\sum a_j<p-1$, making each $a_j<p-1$.
Thus $h$ is the number of triples $(a_1,a_2,a_3)\in\mathbb N^3$ with
$\sum a_j\le\ell-1$. Stars and bars (distribute $\le\ell-1$ identical
balls into $3$ labelled bins) gives
\[
 h=\binom{(\ell-1)+3}{3}=\binom{\ell+2}{3}.
\]
Now $\ell=(p-1)/2$, so
\[
 \binom{\ell+2}{3} = \frac{(\ell+2)(\ell+1)\ell}{6}
 = \frac{(p+3)(p+1)(p-1)}{48}.
\]
Divide by $p^3$: $h/p^3=(p+3)(p^2-1)/(48\,p^3)$. To compare with
$1/48$, compute the difference
\[
 (p+3)(p^2-1)-p^3=3p^2-p-3>0 \quad\text{for }p\ge3,
\]
which gives $h/p^3>1/48$.
\end{proof}

To summarize the situation in one line: $B$ is a tall matrix whose
columns are the ``high-degree part'' of $K^P$, and both $u(s)$-shifts
and their transposes kill $B$ after $\ell$ applications. The fraction
$h/p^3$ is at least $1/48$ of the ambient dimension.

We should remark what is special about the value $\ell=(p-1)/2$.
Choosing $\ell$ smaller would shrink $B$'s fraction too much; choosing
$\ell$ larger would not annihilate $B$ at all (the top-degree columns
would survive). The exponent $\ell$ is exactly the right size to
simultaneously annihilate on both sides while keeping $B$ fat.
