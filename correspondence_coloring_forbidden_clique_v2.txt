================================================================
CORRESPONDENCE COLORING OF GRAPHS WITH A FORBIDDEN CLIQUE
a complete proof, written to be read
================================================================

HOW TO READ THIS NOTE
---------------------
This note proves one theorem, stated in Part 2.  Everything else is
here only to serve that statement, and every step is justified.  We go
slowly at the start: Part 1 fixes the words, Part 2 states the result
and the plan, Parts 3 through 9 carry out the proof.  If a step feels
sudden, the sentence just before it usually says why it is there.

Notation is ordinary ASCII math.  "^" is a power.  "<=" and ">=" are
inequalities, and "!=" is "not equal".  "log" is the natural logarithm
throughout.  "sum" is a sum and "prod" a product.  "|S|" is the number
of elements of a set S.  "O(f)" means "at most a constant times f",
where the constant is allowed to depend on the fixed integer r but
never on the graph, its size, its lists, or its matchings.  When we
write "for large enough Delta", the threshold may depend on r only.

================================================================
PART 1.  THREE KINDS OF COLORING
================================================================

1.1  Ordinary coloring.
A graph G has a vertex set V(G) and an edge set E(G).  Edges are
pairs of distinct vertices, at most one edge between any two vertices,
and no loops; this is what "simple" means.  A proper coloring assigns
to every vertex v a color c(v), so that the two ends of every edge get
different colors.  The chromatic number chi(G) is the smallest number
of colors that suffices.  A greedy argument gives chi(G) <= Delta + 1,
where Delta is the maximum degree of G (the largest number of edges at
any one vertex): color the vertices one at a time; when v is colored,
its already-colored neighbors number at most Delta, so one of
Delta + 1 colors is still free at v.

1.2  List coloring.
Suppose each vertex v is handed its own personal list L(v) of allowed
colors, and different vertices may get different lists.  A list
coloring picks, for every vertex v, one color c(v) in L(v), with the
rule that adjacent vertices get different colors.  The list chromatic
number chi_list(G) is the smallest k such that, whenever every vertex
has a list of at least k colors (whatever those colors are), a list
coloring exists.  Giving every vertex the same list of k colors is a
special case of handing out lists, so a list chromatic number at most
k forces an ordinary coloring with k colors; hence chi(G) <= chi_list(G).

1.3  Correspondence coloring (also called DP-coloring).
This is the most flexible of the three, and it is what our theorem is
about.  Here colors are not shared between vertices in a fixed way.
The data are:

  (a) for every vertex v, a finite set L(v) of "entries" (think of
      them as tokens that may be placed at v);
  (b) for every edge uv, a matching M_uv between L(u) and L(v).  The
      pairs in this matching are the "forbidden pairs" of the edge uv.

A pair (a,b) in M_uv means "you may not use token a at u at the same
time as token b at v".  Because M_uv is a matching, each token of L(u)
is forbidden with at most one token of L(v), and vice versa.

A coloring of such an assignment chooses one entry c(v) in L(v) for
every vertex v, with the requirement that for every edge uv the chosen
pair (c(u),c(v)) is NOT a forbidden pair of M_uv.  The correspondence
chromatic number, also called the DP-chromatic number chi_DP(G), is
the smallest k such that every correspondence assignment with
|L(v)| >= k at every vertex admits such a choice.

Two features deserve emphasis.

First, the matchings on different edges need not agree around a cycle:
there may be no way to give the tokens globally consistent names.  That
absence of global names is the whole reason this setting is harder than
list coloring, and it is why, later, we are careful never to assume such
names exist.  Any argument that secretly identifies tokens across lists
would be wrong here.

Second, both earlier notions sit inside this one.

  - To model list coloring: at each vertex give each listed color its
    own separate copy as a token, and along an edge uv place (a,b) in
    M_uv exactly when the two tokens came from the same color name.
    Then a forbidden pair is precisely "same color, adjacent", and a
    correspondence coloring is exactly a proper list coloring.

  - To model ordinary coloring: give every vertex the same list of k
    colors, then take separate copies as above.

Therefore, for every graph G,
        chi(G) <= chi_list(G) <= chi_DP(G) <= Delta + 1,
where the final inequality is greedy: place tokens so that every vertex
has more than Delta entries; then color vertices one at a time, and at
each vertex delete the at most Delta entries forbidden by its already-
colored neighbors.  Since each such neighbor forbids at most one entry
(a matching along that edge), at least one entry survives.

Our theorem shaves a factor of log Delta off this trivial bound,
provided the graph has no large clique.

================================================================
PART 2.  THE THEOREM, A COROLLARY, AND WHY THE ORDER IS RIGHT
================================================================

2.1  The statement.

For an integer r >= 1, a graph is K_r-free if it contains no complete
graph on r vertices as a subgraph (a "clique of size r").  Being
K_3-free is being triangle-free; being K_4-free is having no K_4.

  THEOREM A.
  Fix an integer r >= 4.  There are constants C_r > 0 and Delta_r >= 3
  such that every K_r-free graph G with maximum degree Delta >= Delta_r
  satisfies
        chi_DP(G) <= ceil( C_r * Delta / log Delta ).

The bound is uniform: one constant C_r serves all such graphs, no
matter how many vertices they have, how large their lists are, or how
the forbidden-pair matchings are arranged.  We make no attempt to keep
C_r or Delta_r small; only their existence matters.

2.2  The corollary for an arbitrary forbidden graph.

For a fixed finite graph F, call G F-free if G contains no copy of F
as a subgraph (the copy need not be induced).

  COROLLARY B.
  For every finite graph F there are constants C_F > 0 and Delta_F >= 3
  such that every F-free graph G with maximum degree Delta >= Delta_F
  satisfies
        chi_DP(G) <= ceil( C_F * Delta / log Delta ).

  Proof.  Let r = max(4, |V(F)|).  The clique K_r contains F as a
  subgraph, so every F-free graph is K_r-free.  Now apply Theorem A
  with this r.  (Done.)

The ordinary-coloring version of Corollary B is Conjecture 3.1 of
Alon, Krivelevich, and Sudakov (1999).  So Corollary B settles that
conjecture, in the stronger correspondence-coloring form.

2.3  Why Delta / log Delta is the best possible order.

Nothing smaller can work in general.  If F contains a cycle, then for
every Delta there are graphs of maximum degree at most Delta whose
girth (length of the shortest cycle) exceeds |V(F)|, so they are F-free,
and whose chromatic number is of order Delta / log Delta; this is due
to Bollobas (1978).  Since chi_DP is at least chi, no bound smaller than
a constant times Delta / log Delta can hold for all F-free graphs.
So Theorem A and Corollary B are sharp up to the value of the constant.

================================================================
PART 3.  THE PLAN OF THE PROOF
================================================================

The proof has two halves.

  HALF ONE (Parts 4 to 6) is a self-contained statement about weighted
  graphs: the local weight adjustment lemma (Lemma D, stated in Part 4).
  It is pure combinatorics.  Given a weighted graph with a forbidden
  clique and a bound on the neighborhood weights, it manufactures a
  family of small random edits to the weights whose averaged effect
  cancels the troublesome "triangles".

  HALF TWO (Parts 7 to 9) uses that lemma to run a randomized coloring
  process in rounds.  After enough rounds the uncolored vertices have a
  remaining degree far below the number of usable entries; a final
  greedy pass colors whatever is left.

We spend Part 3 describing the shape of both halves, so that the
technical parts have somewhere to hang.

3.1  The entry graph.

We run the coloring process while keeping a partial coloring: some
vertices already colored, all rules respected.  At each still-uncolored
vertex v, of its allowed entries we keep only those not forbidden by
any color already placed at a neighbor, and call these available.  Put
a nonnegative weight w_i on each available entry i.  Now form the
entry graph H:

  - the vertices of H are the entries i with w_i > 0;
  - two entries are joined in H exactly when, together, they form a
    forbidden pair somewhere (so they sit at the two ends of some edge
    of G).

The restricted sets L(v) partition V(H) into parts.  Two facts about H
drive everything.

(CHAIN) H is K_r-free.  Indeed, a clique in H projects to a set of
vertices of G that are pairwise adjacent: a forbidden pair sits along
an edge of G, so two joined entries are hosted at adjacent vertices of
G, and distinct parts give distinct vertices.  A clique of size r in H
would therefore be a K_r in G, which G does not have.

(PART) Every entry has at most one neighbor in each part.  Indeed, the
forbidden pairs along one edge uv form a matching, so a token at u is
forbidden with at most one token at v.

Facts (CHAIN) and (PART) are exactly the hypotheses of the weight
adjustment lemma.  This is why, from here, we can forget G for a while
and work with a weighted K_r-free graph whose vertices are split into
parts, each vertex meeting each part at most once.  We use these two
facts below without further comment.

3.2  Weights, and what a round must accomplish.

At a still-uncolored vertex v, over the available entries i of its list
L(v), define the total weight
        W_v = sum_{i in L(v)} w_i .
For an entry i, let N_H(i) be its set of neighbors in H, and define
its neighborhood weight
        s_i = sum_{u in N_H(i)} w_u .
We keep two things true at every uncolored vertex and entry:

  (WIN)  K/2 <= W_v <= 2K  for every uncolored v, where K is a fixed
         constant we choose later (it will be K = 16(B+2), with B the
         constant of the adjustment lemma);
  (SMALL)  Delta^{-3} <= w_i <= Delta^{-0.99}  and  s_i <= Lambda,
         where Lambda = 20 log Delta.

The upper bound in (SMALL) is what makes weights "individually tiny".
Its point is combined with (WIN): a fixed positive total W_v >= K/2,
split into pieces each of size at most Delta^{-0.99}, needs at least
        (K/2) / Delta^{-0.99} = (K/2) Delta^{0.99}
positive pieces.  So around every surviving vertex there are at least
about (K/2) Delta^{0.99} available entries.  When that number exceeds
the remaining degree, a greedy completion is available; this is the
endgame of Part 9.  The lower bound Delta^{-3} in (SMALL) is a technical
floor that keeps very tiny positive weights from misbehaving; weights
that dip below it are cleaned up by "trimming" at the end of each round.

3.3  What one round does, and the three difficulties.

A round is a randomized update that (a) colors a decent fraction of the
still-uncolored vertices and (b) keeps (WIN) and (SMALL) in force at
every survivor.  The natural update works along the entries: activate
each entry with probability proportional to its weight, then let an
activated entry become the chosen color of its host if none of its
neighbors in H activated.  When a neighbor's color is chosen, the
entries forbidden with it are deleted (they can never be used).  A
bookkeeping potential measures how much weight is lost and how much
"edge mass" is around:

        D_v = sum_{u in N_G'(v)} D_{vu},
        D_{vu} = sum over forbidden pairs (i,l), i in L(v), l in L(u),
                 of w_i w_l,
        F_v = (entropy term) + gamma log Delta * (K - W_v) + D_v .

Here G' is the graph induced by the uncolored vertices, and gamma is a
fixed small constant (we will take gamma = 0.003).  The entropy term is
the same "relative entropy of the weights" that appears in Johansson's
method; we spell it out in Part 7, where the potential is defined
exactly.  For now, the meaning is:

  - the entropy term is nonnegative and small when the weights within
    a list are spread evenly;
  - the middle term penalizes W_v dropping below K;
  - D_v is the amount of conflicting weight around v; coloring
    neighbors lowers it, which pays for the round.

Three difficulties arise, and the whole architecture is designed to
handle them.

Difficulty 1: triangles.  Suppose we only compensated weight loss "to
first order" along edges.  That works along a single edge, but around
a triangle it does not: if three entries that are mutually adjacent all
get touched by the round, the second-order correction is positive and
accumulates.  Measured at entry i, the accumulation is

        t_i = w_i * sum_{j in N_H(i)} w_j *
                     sum_{z in N_H(i) and N_H(j)} w_z ,

which is twice the weighted mass of triangles through i in H.  A bound
on total triangle mass alone would not repair the individual
inequalities at each entry, because different entries can be hurt by
very different amounts.  What we need is a family of extra random
edits, one per entry, whose averaged contribution offsets t_i at every
entry simultaneously, and which costs only a constant multiple of the
edge mass
        d_i = w_i * s_i = w_i * sum_{j in N_H(i)} w_j
at each entry.  The local weight adjustment lemma is exactly this
machine; its constant is B (to be fixed in Part 4).  It builds edits of
two shapes: multipliers (scale a few nearby weights up or down around
1, on a shared random event) and drops (delete weight at one entry and
charge the incident edges).  The lemma packs these into vectors g_i
with rates a_j and guarantees
        t_i + sum_j a_j g_i^{(j)} <= B d_i
        sum over j with i in support_j of a_j <= Lambda^4
for every entry i.

Difficulty 2: participation within one list.  In the correspondence
setting an edit whose support has small radius in H can still touch many
entries of a single list L(v), because one list may carry many entries.
If an edit touched many entries of one list, the total weight of that
list would become a sum of many quantities that change together, and
the concentration step of Half Two would break.  So the adjustment
lemma must control two things at once: every edit is supported inside a
ball of radius R_0, and every edit touches at most P_0 entries of any
one part.  At our scale these are small: with Lambda = 20 log Delta,
        R_0 = O(log log Delta),      P_0 = Delta^{o(1)}.
Both come out of the same combinatorial construction (Parts 5 and 6),
and both are exactly what the concentration step needs.

Difficulty 3: simultaneous concentration.  After a round, the weights
inside one list are not independent: they are functions of shared
random flags.  So we cannot use a plain sum-of-independent-variables
bound.  Instead (Part 8) we use a concentration inequality for sums of
bounded functions of independent inputs, where each input influences
only a few summands.  The participation bound of Difficulty 2 is what
makes each adjustment input influence few entries of any list.  A
Lovasz local lemma then makes all the many simultaneous estimates hold
at every vertex at once.

3.4  The finish.

Each round shrinks the remaining degree of a vertex by a multiplicative
factor (1 - tau) plus a tiny additive error, with tau = Delta^{-0.10}.
After T_* = ceil(2 tau^{-1} log Delta) rounds the remaining degree at
every vertex is at most
        Delta^{-1} + Delta^{0.96},
which is far below the supply of available entries, at least about
(K/2) Delta^{0.99}.  Each colored neighbor (during the final greedy
pass) forbids at most one entry of the list, by (PART); since there are
more available entries than neighbors left, a choice always survives
and the coloring completes.

That is the whole architecture.  Parts 4 to 6 build the adjustment
lemma; Parts 7 to 9 carry out the rounds and the finish.

================================================================
PART 4.  THE LOCAL WEIGHT ADJUSTMENT LEMMA
================================================================

Part 4 states the lemma and explains what its edits mean.  Parts 5
and 6 prove it.

4.1  The setting.

Fix an integer r >= 4.  Let H be a finite K_r-free graph, with a
partition of its vertex set V(H) into parts; call the partition D, so
each vertex lies in exactly one part D in D.  We require (PART): every
vertex of H has at most one neighbor in each part D in D.

Give each vertex i a positive weight w_i > 0.  Define three quantities
at each vertex:
        s_i = sum_{u in N_H(i)} w_u            (neighborhood weight),
        d_i = w_i * s_i                        (incident edge mass),
        t_i = w_i * sum_{u in N_H(i)} w_u *
                     sum_{z in N_H(u) and N_H(i)} w_z
                                               (twice triangle mass).
So d_i is the total weight of edges out of i, and t_i is twice the total
weight of triangles through i (each triangle contributes its three
pairwise products once at each of its three corners).

We suppose throughout that
        s_i <= Lambda  for every i in V(H),
where Lambda >= 1 is a parameter.  This is the (SMALL) condition of
Part 3, at the graph level.

Define the function f on [0, infinity) by
        f(a) = a log a - a + 1    for a > 0,     f(0) = 1.
It is convex, it equals 0 at a = 1, and f(a) stays close to a quadratic
near a = 1 (a Taylor expansion gives f(1+h) = O(h^2) for small h).
This f is the "entropy increment" of a single weight that is multiplied
slightly.  Its convexity is the reason the linear terms in our
bookkeeping will cancel later.

4.2  The constants of the lemma.

Put, with log the natural logarithm,
        ell_0 = log(8 Lambda),
        R_0   = ceil(4 ell_0) + 10,
        U     = exp(A ell_0^2),
        P_0   = ceil( exp(40 ell_0^2) ),
where A = A(r) > 0 is a constant to be chosen (it will be chosen in
Part 5 to make the local estimate hold; it may be large, since only its
existence matters).  Intuitively:
  - R_0 is the radius within which an edit is allowed to act;
  - U is the largest value a multiplier may take;
  - P_0 is the largest number of entries of a single part an edit may
    touch.
At the scale used in the coloring argument, Lambda = 20 log Delta makes
        ell_0 = O(log log Delta),  R_0 = O(log log Delta),
        P_0 = Delta^{o(1)},
which matches Difficulty 2 of Part 3.

4.3  The two kinds of edit.

A MULTIPLIER OPERATION consists of:
  - a nonempty listed support J, a subset of V(H), contained in a ball
    of radius R_0 in H (that is, there is a center vertex such that
    every point of J is within graph distance R_0 of it), and meeting
    each part D of the partition in at most P_0 vertices;
  - multipliers m_i in [0, U] for i in J.

Extend the multipliers by m_i = 1 for i outside J.  Attach to the
operation the vector g with coordinates
        g_i = w_i f(m_i) + sum_{u in N_H(i)} w_i w_u (m_i - 1)(m_u - 1).
The first term is the change in the entropy part of the weight w_i; the
second term is the change in the edge mass on edges out of i.  Note that
m_i = 1 on part of the support is allowed; those coordinates simply
contribute nothing.  Note also that g_i can be nonzero for vertices i
OUTSIDE the support J, because the second term involves neighbors u
whose multipliers differ from 1.  The "listed support" J records only
where we deliberately act; it does not confine the vector.

A DROP OPERATION AT i has listed support J = {i} and vector g with
        g_i = Lambda w_i - d_i,
        g_u = - w_i w_u        for u in N_H(i),
        g_u = 0                for all other u.
The drop "charges" Lambda w_i for deleting the weight at i (a charge
that the s_i <= Lambda condition makes affordable, since d_i = w_i s_i
<= Lambda w_i), and subtracts the incident edge masses it removes.  A
drop's vector again has nonzero coordinates outside its listed support,
namely on the neighbors of i.

These two families of vectors are our edits.  A "family of operations
with rates" means: finitely many operations j, each either a multiplier
operation or a drop, with an associated vector g^(j), support J_j, and
a nonnegative rate a_j.

4.4  The lemma.

  LEMMA D (local weight adjustment).
  For every integer r >= 4 there are constants A = A(r) > 0,
  B = B(r) >= 1, and Lambda_r >= 1 with the following property.  Let
  Lambda >= Lambda_r, and let H be a finite K_r-free graph whose vertex
  set has a partition D such that each vertex has at most one neighbor
  in each part (property (PART)).  For any positive vertex weights with
  s_i <= Lambda at every vertex, there is a finite family of multiplier
  and drop operations, with vectors g^(j), listed supports J_j, and
  rates a_j >= 0, such that for every vertex i

        t_i + sum_j a_j g_i^(j)  <=  B d_i ,          (V)
        sum over j with i in J_j of a_j  <=  Lambda^4 . (R)

  The constants A, B, Lambda_r depend only on r.

Inequality (V) says the triangle cost t_i is offset, up to a bounded
multiple B of the edge mass d_i, by the averaged edits.  Inequality (R)
says that, at any single vertex, the total rate of edits whose listed
support includes it is at most Lambda^4; this is the rate control that
makes the support bound of Difficulty 2 useful in Half Two (each edit
has rate at most Lambda^4, and fires rarely).

  REMARK D'.
  If we take every part to be a singleton, then the partition condition
  (PART) holds automatically, so Lemma D applies to an arbitrary
  positively weighted K_r-free graph with no partition specified.  We
  state the lemma with a partition only because the correspondence
  setting needs the participation bound (R), which refers to parts.

4.5  Where the vectors come from, to first order.

The point of the vectors g is that they describe the first-order
expected change of an entropy-plus-edge potential, so that a random
round can offset (V) simply by firing each operation with probability
proportional to its rate.  Here is the computation behind the vector
formula, which also motivates the shapes of the edits.

Fix multipliers m_i (extended by 1 off a set J) and a small
probability rho > 0.  On a random event of probability rho, multiply
every weight by m_i; on the complementary event, multiply by
(1 - rho m_i)/(1 - rho).  Each weight's expectation is unchanged:
        rho * m_i + (1 - rho) * (1 - rho m_i)/(1 - rho) = 1.
This "mean-one" construction is what prevents the edits from drifting
the total weight.

Now watch two functionals change under this mean-one multiplicative
edit.  First, the entropy functional sum_i (w_i log w_i - w_i), with
the convention 0 log 0 = 0.  For a single weight w_i multiplied by a
mean-one factor that is m_i with probability rho and close to 1
otherwise, the expected change of the entropy term is
        rho * w_i f(m_i) + O(rho^2),
where f is the function from Section 4.1.  This is exactly the first
term of g_i.  (To see it: the sum of the two possible values of
w log w - w, weighted by their probabilities, differs from the
deterministic value by rho times w (m log m - m + 1), plus a term of
order rho^2 from the compensation factor.)

Second, the edge functional sum over edges iu of w_i w_u.  The expected
change on one edge iu is
        rho * w_i w_u (m_i - 1)(m_u - 1) + O(rho^2),
which is exactly the second term of g_i when summed over u.  So the
vector g_i records the coefficient of rho in the expected change of
"entropy at i plus edge mass out of i".

The triangle trouble appears when we look at the specific edits we
actually want in a coloring round: activating entry z with probability
proportional to its weight w_z, and (on that event) deleting the weights
on the open neighborhood N_H(z), with a compensation restoring the mean
loss.  Such an activation contributes, to first order,
        rho d_i      to the entropy change at i,  and
        rho t_i      to the incident edge change at i.
The rho d_i part is fine (it has the right sign and size relative to
d_i).  The rho t_i part is the positive triangle correlation of
Difficulty 1, and it must be compensated.  Lemma D is the statement
that the multiplier and drop edits can compensate it: (V) says that
after firing the edits with their rates, the leftover coefficient of
rho at i is at most B d_i, uniformly in i.  In Part 7 we fire each edit
with probability proportional to its rate and verify that the total
expected change of the potential is under control.

4.6  Outline of the proof of Lemma D.

The proof occupies Parts 5 and 6, and follows three moves.

  MOVE 1 (duality, Parts 5.1): The inequalities (V) for all vertices i
  together say that a certain vector built from t, d, and the edit
  vectors can be dominated.  By finite-dimensional separation, it
  suffices to check a DUAL statement against arbitrary nonnegative
  "prices": any family of prices obeying a simple test must obey a
  scalar triangle estimate.  This converts "many simultaneous vertex
  inequalities" into "one scalar inequality about triangle mass".

  MOVE 2 (local edge estimate, Parts 5.2 to 5.4): The dual prices
  control the weight of edges between two nearby sets, with a bound
  that folds the rate allowance in.  This is the local cross estimate,
  and it transfers intact when we later split vertices into copies.

  MOVE 3 (triangle bound by splitting, Part 6): Using Move 2, we show
  the priced triangle mass is at most a constant multiple of the priced
  edge mass.  The tool is a vertex-splitting construction, guided by
  random walks inside neighborhoods, that shrinks every neighborhood
  weight while keeping almost all triangle mass.

Parts 5 and 6 carry these out.  The constants are chosen in this order:
Move 2 fixes A and a constant C_1; Move 3 fixes B; finally Lambda_r is
taken large enough that a quantity of order Lambda^2 is swallowed by
the Lambda^4 allowance in (R).

================================================================
PART 5.  DUAL PRICES AND THE LOCAL EDGE ESTIMATE
================================================================

5.1  Reduction to a scalar triangle estimate.

We have many inequalities to satisfy at once, one per vertex i.
Finite-dimensional duality turns them into a single scalar statement.

Introduce nonnegative PRICES: a price x_i >= 0 for each vertex
inequality (V), and a price w_i y_i >= 0 for each rate inequality (R),
so that y_i >= 0 is a "rate price" measured against the weight.  The
claim we will prove is:

  SEPARATION CLAIM.  Suppose the prices satisfy the DUAL TEST

        sum_i x_i g_i + sum_{i in J} w_i y_i >= 0      (DT)

  for EVERY allowed operation (g, J) (that is, every multiplier or
  drop operation whose listed support is J and whose vector is g).
  Then the prices satisfy the DUAL TARGET

        sum_i x_i t_i <= B sum_i x_i d_i
                              + Lambda^4 sum_i w_i y_i .   (DTG)

Once the Separation Claim is proved, Lemma D follows: if no family of
operations achieved (V) and (R), separation would produce prices
satisfying (DT) but violating (DTG), contradicting the claim.

Here is the separation argument in full.  Let n = |V(H)|, and view an
operation as the column vector (g, 1_J) in R^{2n}, where 1_J is the
indicator vector of the listed support J.  Let C be the cone in R^{2n}
generated by all such columns together with the nonnegative orthant
(every coordinate nonnegative).  Two facts about C:

  (i) C is closed.  The possible columns form a compact set: there are
  finitely many possible supports J (each a subset of V(H)), each
  multiplier cube [0,U]^J is compact, and f is continuous on [0,U].
  Also the sum of the last n coordinates of any column is at least 1,
  because the support J is nonempty.  In any convergent sequence of
  elements of C, those last-coordinate sums therefore bound the total
  amount of any single operation column used.  A subsequence of the
  operation contributions converges into a fixed multiple of the
  compact convex hull of the columns and zero, and the residual
  nonnegative-orthant parts converge within the orthant.  So C is
  closed.

  (ii) The target point (B d - t, Lambda^4 1) lies in C if and only if
  there is a family of operations with rates satisfying (V) and (R),
  where d, t, 1 are the vectors (d_i), (t_i), (all ones).  This is just
  the definition of a nonnegative combination of columns plus a
  nonnegative residual.

Now suppose, toward a contradiction, that (B d - t, Lambda^4 1) is NOT
in C.  Because C is closed and convex, separation gives a linear
functional that is nonnegative on all of C and negative at the target
point.  Its coefficients on the second block (the support coordinates)
are prices of the form w_i y_i; since the functional is nonnegative on
the nonnegative orthant, ALL coefficients are nonnegative, so the first
block coefficients may be written as x_i >= 0 and the second block as
w_i y_i >= 0.  Write the functional as
        (p, q)  |-->  sum_i x_i p_i + sum_i w_i y_i q_i ,
where (p,q) runs over R^n + R^n.  "Nonnegative on every operation
column" is exactly (DT).  "Negative at the target" says
        sum_i x_i (B d_i - t_i) + Lambda^4 sum_i w_i y_i < 0,
which is the negation of (DTG).  So prices satisfying (DT) but not
(DTG) would exist.  The Separation Claim rules this out.  Thus the
Separation Claim implies Lemma D, and it remains to prove the Separation
Claim: under (DT), the prices obey (DTG).

Fix nonnegative prices satisfying (DT).  Apply (DT) to the drop at i.
The drop vector has support coordinate 1 at i, so the second sum in (DT)
contributes w_i y_i, and (DT) reads
        sum_v x_v g_v + w_i y_i >= 0,
where g is the drop vector at i: g_i = Lambda w_i - d_i, g_u = -w_i w_u
for u in N_H(i), and g_v = 0 otherwise.  Expanding, and dividing by
w_i > 0 (using d_i = w_i s_i),
        sum_{u in N_H(i)} x_u w_u <= (Lambda - s_i) x_i + y_i
                                   <= Lambda x_i + y_i ,    (DROP)
because s_i >= 0.  Inequality (DROP) is the "drop test"; it is the only
place the drop operations are used.

Now call a vertex GOOD if x_i > y_i, and BAD if x_i <= y_i.  The prices
at bad vertices are small compared with their weights.  We show the
triangles meeting a bad vertex cost little.

At any vertex v, a triangle {v, u, z} contributes
2 (x_v + x_u + x_z) w_v w_u w_z to the total sum_v x_v t_v (because t_v
counts, with weight 2, each triangle through v).  Fix a bad vertex i.
The total contribution of triangles containing i is at most
        2 w_i ( x_i s_i^2 + s_i * sum_{u in N_H(i)} x_u w_u )
        <= O(Lambda^2) w_i y_i ,
by (DROP), x_i <= y_i, and s_i <= Lambda.  Summing over bad vertices,
and allowing overcounting of triangles, gives
        sum over triangles meeting a bad vertex of
          2 (x_i + x_u + x_z) w_i w_u w_z
        <= O(Lambda^2) sum_i w_i y_i .                         (BAD)

So the bad vertices contribute a term of order Lambda^2 times
sum_i w_i y_i, which the Lambda^4 allowance in (DTG) will comfortably
absorb once Lambda_r is taken large.  What remains is the contribution
of triangles all of whose vertices are good.

5.2  Dyadic levels.

We must bound sum_i x_i t_i over good vertices.  The prices x_i can
range over many magnitudes, so we sort the good vertices by the size of
their price into dyadic levels.

Only good vertices (those with x_i > 0) matter.  Consider the dyadic
numbers b = 2^j, j an integer, such that the half-open interval [b, 2b)
contains the price of some good vertex.  There are only finitely many
such levels.  At level b, classify each good vertex by its price:
        LOW     if x_i <  b,
        MIDDLE  if b <= x_i < 2b,
        HIGH    if x_i >= 2b.
Let H_b be the graph on good vertices obtained from H by deleting every
edge that has two LOW endpoints or two HIGH endpoints.  (MIDDLE with
anything is kept; two MIDDLE kept; LOW with HIGH kept; LOW with MIDDLE
kept; HIGH with MIDDLE kept.)

Rescale the weights at level b to
        q_i = w_i * x_i / b    for HIGH i,
        q_i = w_i              for LOW or MIDDLE i.
These q_i, and the words low, middle, high, always refer to the level b
under consideration.

The reason for this bookkeeping is the following clean counting fact.

  Each triangle of good vertices contributes to T_0 at exactly ONE
  level: the level b whose interval [b, 2b) contains the MEDIAN of its
  three prices.  At that level the triangle has at most one LOW and at
  most one HIGH vertex (two would force two below-median or
  two above-median prices), hence at least one MIDDLE vertex, and none
  of its three edges are deleted.  At every other level two of its
  vertices are both LOW (if the level is above the median) or both HIGH
  (if below), so the edge between them is deleted and the triangle is
  gone from H_b.

Define
        T_0 = sum over levels b of
              b * sum over triangles {i,u,z} of H_b of q_i q_u q_z .
We claim this records the good-vertex triangle mass up to a constant
factor 12.  Two comparisons:

  - Rescaling pays for the largest price.  Suppose the prices are
    ordered, at one level, as x_i < b <= x_u < 2b <= x_z.  Then
    q_i = w_i, q_u = w_u, q_z = w_z x_z / b, so
        b q_i q_u q_z = x_z w_i w_u w_z ,
    exactly the product with the largest price in place.
  - A retained triangle has at least one MIDDLE vertex, so its three
    prices are all less than 2b and at least one is at least b.  Hence
    (x_i + x_u + x_z) <= 3 * (2b) and each pair product is covered, so
        the triangle's contribution 2 (x_i + x_u + x_z) w_i w_u w_z
        <= 12 b q_i q_u q_z .

Summing over the unique level of each good triangle gives
        sum over good-only triangles of 2 (x_i + x_u + x_z) w_i w_u w_z
        <= 12 T_0 .
Combined with (BAD) for the bad vertices, and dropping the triangles
that mix good and bad vertices (they are counted in (BAD) at least once,
since they meet a bad vertex),
        sum_i x_i t_i <= 12 T_0 + O(Lambda^2) sum_i w_i y_i .   (RED)
So to prove (DTG) it is now enough to prove
        T_0 <= O_r(M_x),
where M_x = sum_i x_i d_i is the PRICED EDGE MASS, i.e. the edge budget
        M_x = sum_i x_i d_i = sum over edges iu of (x_i + x_u) w_i w_u .
That single scalar inequality, T_0 <= O_r(M_x), is the target of Parts
5.3, 5.4, and 6.

5.2b  The dual inequality in rescaled form.

At a fixed level b, we translate the prices into coefficients on the
rescaled weights q.  For a good vertex i and an original edge iu with
both ends good, put
        alpha_i   = x_i w_i / (b q_i) ,
        beta_i    = y_i w_i / (b q_i) ,
        kappa_iu  = (x_i + x_u) w_i w_u / (b q_i q_u) .
These are the prices per unit of rescaled weight.  They obey
        0 <= beta_i < alpha_i <= 2 ,
        0 < kappa_iu <= 4 ,
        kappa_iu >= 1   whenever iu is an edge of H_b .          (KOEF)
Let us check each bound.  At a HIGH vertex alpha_i = 1; at a LOW or
MIDDLE vertex alpha_i = x_i/b, which is less than 2 and (for a good
vertex) positive.  So 0 < alpha_i <= 2, and beta_i < alpha_i is just
goodness x_i > y_i.  For kappa: if exactly one endpoint of an edge is
HIGH, then kappa is 1 plus the ratio of the other price to the high
price, which lies between 1 and 2; if both are HIGH, kappa is
b/x_i + b/x_u <= 2; if neither is HIGH, kappa is (x_i + x_u)/b <= 4,
and if the edge is retained in H_b with neither endpoint HIGH then one
endpoint is MIDDLE, giving (x_i + x_u)/b >= 1.  This proves (KOEF).

Now apply the dual test (DT) to a multiplier operation whose support J
lies entirely among the good vertices.  Dividing (DT) by b and
substituting the definitions, using that (m_i - 1)(m_u - 1) = 0 unless
both endpoints are off their base value (and the edge term only runs
over original edges of H, including edges that H_b deleted), we get
        0 <= sum_{i good} alpha_i q_i f(m_i)
             + sum_{i,u good, iu in E(H)} kappa_iu q_i q_u
                   (m_i - 1)(m_u - 1)
             + sum_{i in J} beta_i q_i .                       (NDT)
This normalized dual inequality (NDT), holding for every multiplier
operation on good vertices, is the form in which we shall use the dual
test.  Notice the edge sum still runs over ALL original edges, not just
those retained in H_b; the deletions in H_b only remove edges from the
triangle count T_0, and the estimate below accounts for the missing
edges correctly.

Finally, the rescaling preserves a uniform neighborhood bound.  For
every good vertex i,
        sum_{u in N_{H_b}(i)} q_u <= e^{ell_0} = 8 Lambda .    (NBHD)
For a HIGH vertex, all retained neighbors keep their original weights
(the deletion rule ensures a retained neighbor of a HIGH vertex is not
itself HIGH in the same way; at any rate the q-weight of retained
neighbors is at most the original w-weight total, which is s_i <=
Lambda), so the sum is at most Lambda.  For a non-HIGH vertex i, the
HIGH neighbors have total q-weight at most
        (1/b) sum_{u in N_H(i)} x_u w_u <= (Lambda x_i + y_i)/b
        < 2(Lambda + 1),
by (DROP) and goodness x_i > y_i, while the non-HIGH neighbors
contribute at most their original weight sum s_i <= Lambda.  This
proves (NBHD).

5.3  Sparse multipliers.

The next tool is a way to choose random mean-one multipliers on a
weighted K_r-free graph that keep the expected edge mass small, at the
cost of bounding every multiplier.  It is the sparse-subgraph device of
Ajtai, Erdos, Komlos, and Szemeredi, in the weighted form we need.

  LEMMA E (sparse multipliers).
  For every integer r >= 2 there is a constant D_r >= 1 such that the
  following holds.  Let F be a finite K_r-free graph with positive
  vertex weights q_i of total mass s = sum_i q_i.  For every
  0 < epsilon <= 1/2 there are jointly distributed random multipliers
  n_i, one per vertex, such that

        0 <= n_i <= D_r * epsilon^{-(r-2)} ,        (SM1)
        E n_i = 1 ,                                  (SM2)
        E [ sum over edges iu of F of q_i q_u n_i n_u ] <= epsilon s^2 .
                                                     (SM3)

  Here E is expectation and the edge sum is unordered.

  Proof.  If F has no vertices the claim is vacuous, so assume s > 0,
  and we argue by induction on r.

  For r = 2, being K_2-free means having no edge at all, so the edge
  sum in (SM3) is 0 and the constant multipliers n_i = 1 satisfy
  everything.

  Now assume r >= 3 and the lemma known for r - 1.  Keep the initial
  total mass s fixed.  Repeatedly do this: in the current remainder,
  if some vertex has an open neighborhood (inside the remainder) of
  mass greater than epsilon s / 4, remove that open neighborhood as
  one "removed set"; continue until no vertex remains whose open
  neighborhood has mass above epsilon s / 4.  The removed sets are
  disjoint, each removed set induces a K_{r-1}-free graph (if it
  contained a K_{r-1}, then together with the removed vertex, which is
  adjacent to all of it, it would form a K_r in F), and each has mass
  s_j > epsilon s / 4.

  Call the leftover some "remainder".  In the remainder every open
  neighborhood has mass at most epsilon s / 4.  Summing the
  neighborhood masses over all vertices counts each edge at both ends,
  so the remainder's unordered edge mass is at most
        (1/2) * sum over vertices of (mass of its neighborhood)
        <= (1/2) * s * (epsilon s / 4) = epsilon s^2 / 8 .
  If no set was removed, the constant multipliers 1 already give
  (SM3) (with room to spare) and the assertion holds.

  Otherwise, do the following randomized selection.  With probability
  1/2 select the remainder, and with probability
        p_j = s_j / (2 sum_l s_l)
  select removed set j.  (Since every s_j > epsilon s / 4, and the
  s_j sum to at most s, we have p_j > epsilon / 8.)  Set the
  multipliers to 0 on everything not selected.  On the selected
  remainder use the constant multiplier 2.  On the selected removed
  set j use the multipliers supplied by the induction hypothesis for
  parameter epsilon / 4, divided by p_j.  This makes every vertex have
  mean multiplier 1: on the remainder, probability 1/2 times 2; on a
  removed set, probability p_j times (mean 1)/p_j.

  The multiplier bound: on a removed set, the induction hypothesis
  bounds the multiplier by D_{r-1} (epsilon/4)^{-(r-3)}, and dividing
  by p_j > epsilon/8 gives at most
        8 * 4^{r-3} * D_{r-1} * epsilon^{-(r-2)} ,
  so a constant D_r depending only on r bounds all multipliers.  On
  the remainder the multiplier is 2.

  Expected edge mass: edges between different selected pieces have
  zero mass (at least one endpoint selected zero).  The remainder contributes in expectation at most
  (1/2) * 4 * (epsilon s^2/8) = epsilon s^2 / 4 (selection probability
  1/2, each endpoint scaled by 2).  The removed sets contribute in
  expectation at most
        sum_j (epsilon/4) s_j^2 / p_j
        = (epsilon/2) (sum_j s_j)^2
        <= (epsilon/2) s^2 ,
  because (epsilon/4) s_j^2 is the inductive edge-mass bound and we
  divide by the selection probability p_j.  Their sum is at most
  epsilon s^2, proving (SM3).  This completes the induction.  (Done.)

5.4  The local cross estimate.

For sets S, D inside a level graph with rescaled weights q, write
        q(S) = sum_{i in S} q_i ,
        e_q(S, D) = sum_{i in S} sum_{u in D and N(i)} q_i q_u .
The last is a DIRECTED edge sum between S and D: an edge with both ends
in the overlap S and D is counted twice.  When several graphs are in
play, a subscript names the graph.

  LEMMA F (local cross estimate).
  Assume the prices satisfy the dual test (DT), and fix a level b.  Let
  S, D be subsets of V(H_b) such that S union D lies in a ball of radius
  R_0 in H and meets every part of the partition in at most P_0
  vertices.  Suppose that

        32 <= h <= 20 ell_0^2 ,
        q(S) <= e^h ,   q(D) <= e^h ,
        0 <= eta <= 2 ,
        alpha_i <= eta and beta_i <= eta  for every i in S .

  If the constant A in the definition of U (Section 4.2) is sufficiently
  large in terms of r alone, then

        e_q(S, D) <= C_1 ( eta h q(S) + e^{-3h} ) ,        (CROSS)

  where C_1 = C_1(r) depends only on r.

  Remark.  The estimate is a weight bound on the edges between two
  nearby, not-too-heavy sets, with an extra factor eta controlling the
  price on the S side.  The term e^{-3h} is a tiny "exponential cost",
  harmless at the scales we eventually use (h grows like a power of
  log Lambda there).  Lemma F is proved by testing (NDT) against one
  carefully chosen multiplier operation built from Lemma E.

  Proof of Lemma F.  First suppose S and D are disjoint and both
  nonempty.  Apply Lemma E to the induced graph H[S] with its q-weights
  and the parameter
        epsilon = e^{-16h} ,
  obtaining mean-one multipliers n_i on S with
        E sum over edges iu inside S of q_i q_u n_i n_u <= e^{-16h} q(S)^2 .
  Set k' = e^{8h}.  Consider the multiplier operation with listed
  support S union D and multipliers
        m_i = 1 + k' n_i    for i in S ,
        m_i = 0             for i in D .
  By the hypotheses of Lemma F, S union D lies in a ball of radius R_0
  and meets every part in at most P_0 vertices, so this is an allowed
  operation provided every multiplier lies in [0, U].  The multipliers
  on D equal 0.  On S, m_i = 1 + k' n_i >= 1, and
        log m_i = log(1 + k' n_i) <= log(1 + k' D_r e^{16h(r-2)})
                <= C'_r h
  for a constant C'_r depending only on r, using k' = e^{8h}.  Since
  h <= 20 ell_0^2, choosing A >= 20 C'_r gives log m_i <= A ell_0^2 =
  log U, so m_i <= U.  Hence every realization is an allowed operation.

  Write s = q(S), d = q(D).  For EVERY realization of the multipliers,
  (NDT) holds, so it holds after taking expectations.  We bound the
  three pieces of (NDT) in expectation.

  Entropy on S: For a >= 0 we have f(1 + a) <= a log(1 + a) (because
  f(1+a) = (1+a)log(1+a) - a and log(1+a) <= a).  Hence
        E sum_{i in S} alpha_i q_i f(m_i)
        <= eta sum_{i in S} q_i E[ k' n_i log(1 + k' n_i) ]
        <= C'_r eta h k' s ,
  using E n_i = 1 and log(1+k'n_i) <= C'_r h.

  Entropy and support on D, plus support on S:  On D, m_i = 0, so
  f(m_i) = f(0) = 1 and the entropy coordinate is alpha_i q_i, while
  the support coordinate is beta_i q_i < alpha_i q_i <= 2 q_i.  So the
  D-part of the entropy plus the D-part of the support is at most
  4d.  The support on S is sum_{i in S} beta_i q_i <= eta s.

  Quadratic edge term: The expected quadratic term is at most
        4 (k')^2 e^{-16h} s^2  +  2 d^2  -  k' e_q(S, D) ,     (SQ)
  where the estimate records four groups of edges:
    - edges inside S: (m_i - 1)(m_u - 1) = (k')^2 n_i n_u, kappa <= 4,
      and Lemma E gives the first term;
    - edges inside D: (m_i - 1)(m_u - 1) = 1, kappa <= 4, and the
      unordered edge mass inside D is at most d^2 / 2, giving 2 d^2;
    - edges between S and D that are kept in H_b: on these the product
      is (k' n_i)(-1) = -k' n_i, and kappa >= 1, so the expected
      contribution is -k' e_q(S, D) at most (in absolute size);
    - edges between S and D that H_b deleted: the same product -k' n_i
      is at most 0, so these only help and are dropped from the upper
      bound;
    - edges leaving S union D: one endpoint has multiplier 1, so the
      product is 0.
  Since the tested inequality (NDT) holds for every realization,
        E[ entropy ] + E[ quadratic ] + E[ support ] >= 0 .
  Substituting the three bounds and moving -k' e_q(S,D) to the left,
        k' e_q(S, D)
        <= C'_r eta h k' s + eta s + 4d
             + 4 (k')^2 e^{-16h} s^2 + 2 d^2 .
  Divide by k' = e^{8h}.  Since (k')^2 e^{-16h} = 1, s <= e^h, d <= e^h,
  and eta <= 2, the numerator after division is at most
        6 e^{-7h} + 6 e^{-6h} = O(e^{-6h}) ,
  while the leading term is C'_r eta h s.  This proves (CROSS) when S
  and D are disjoint (with C_1 = C_1(r) absorbing all constants).  If
  either set is empty, e_q(S,D) = 0 and (CROSS) holds trivially.

  Overlap:  For possibly overlapping S and D, assign each vertex of
  S union D independently to a left or right class, each with
  probability 1/2.  Let S_L be the vertices of S put on the left and
  D_R the vertices of D put on the right; these are disjoint.  Apply
  the disjoint estimate to S_L and D_R.  Every directed edge of
  e_q(S, D) survives with probability 1/4, while the expected mass
  q(S_L) is q(S)/2, and the coefficient bounds alpha, beta <= eta pass
  to S_L.  Taking expectations,
        (1/4) e_q(S, D) <= C_1 ( eta h (q(S)/2) + e^{-3h} ) ,
  so e_q(S, D) <= C_1 ( 2 eta h q(S) + 4 e^{-3h} ).  Increasing C_1
  proves (CROSS) in general.  (Done.)

5.5  Transfer under vertex splitting.

Part 6 will replace H_b by a graph F whose vertices are COPIES of
vertices of H_b, spreading the heavy neighborhoods apart.  We need the
local cross estimate to survive this.  The bridge is a graph
homomorphism that keeps each original directed edge from being
duplicated.

Say a homomorphism p: F -> H_b is INJECTIVE ON DIRECTED EDGES if the
map (u, v) |--> (p(u), p(v)) is injective on all ordered adjacent pairs
of F.  Vertices of F may have several preimages under p (that is how
copies appear), but each original directed edge has at most one
preimage.  Give every vertex of F the weight q, prices, coefficients
alpha, beta, kappa, and part D of its image under p.

  COROLLARY G (transfer under vertex splitting).
  Let F be a graph with a homomorphism p: F -> H_b injective on
  directed edges, and give its vertices the inherited data as above.
  Then every vertex of F has at most one neighbor in each inherited
  part.  Moreover, (CROSS) holds in F for any S, D in V(F) satisfying
  the mass and coefficient hypotheses of Lemma F, provided S union D
  lies in a ball of radius R_0 in F and meets each inherited part in at
  most P_0 vertices.

  Proof.  Directed-edge injectivity gives
        e_{q,F}(S, D) <= e_{q,H_b}(p(S), p(D)) ,
        q(p(S)) <= q(S) ,    q(p(D)) <= q(D) .
  The first inequality holds because distinct directed edges of F have
  distinct images, so the directed edge sum in F injects into the one in
  H_b, never creating more weight.  The projected union p(S union D)
  lies in a ball of radius R_0 in H (a homomorphism does not increase
  graph distances), and meets each original part in at most P_0
  vertices (each vertex of F meets at most P_0 of the inherited parts'
  vertices, and projection only merges).  The coefficient bounds
  alpha, beta <= eta pass to images.  Lemma F permits overlap of its two
  sets, so it applies to p(S) and p(D) and yields
        e_{q,H_b}(p(S), p(D)) <= C_1 ( eta h q(p(S)) + e^{-3h} )
                              <= C_1 ( eta h q(S) + e^{-3h} ) ,
  giving (CROSS) in F.

  For the part condition: two distinct neighbors of a vertex x of F
  must have distinct images, because otherwise two directed edges of F
  (from x to those two neighbors) would share the same image under p,
  contradicting injectivity on directed edges.  These distinct images
  all lie in the neighborhood in H_b of the single vertex p(x), and at
  most one of them belongs to any one part of the partition.  Hence at
  most one neighbor of x belongs to any one inherited part.  (Done.)

  REMARK.  In Part 6 the split graph F will be built so that p is a
  homomorphism injective on directed edges, and so that inherited
  weights, prices, and parts match those of H_b.  Corollary G is the
  reason all the local estimates (CROSS) remain available there.

================================================================
PART 6.  BOUNDING TRIANGLES BY SPLITTING VERTICES
================================================================

We prove T_0 <= O_r(M_x) (Proposition H below) by repeatedly pushing
neighborhood weights down.  The engine is a vertex-SPLITTING: replace a
vertex by several copies of the same weight, share its edges among the
copies, and check that almost all triangle mass survives.  Random walks
inside neighborhoods choose the sharing.  We give the whole argument,
in the same order as the original.

6.1  Level graphs and the two clean-up deletions.

Throughout, a graph at level b is a graph F_b carrying a homomorphism
into H_b that is injective on directed edges, with all vertices
inheriting weight q, prices, parts, and the low/middle/high label from
their images.  Initially F_b = H_b.  Deleting vertices or edges keeps
this property, and the splitting in 6.3 is built to keep it too.  By
Corollary G, (CROSS) is available on every F_b.

Let
        T = sum over levels b of
            b * sum over triangles {a,u,v} of F_b of q_a q_u q_v .
Initially T = T_0, and we track how T drops as we edit the F_b.

  CHARGE LEMMA.  At every stage,
        sum over b of b * sum over MIDDLE vertices a of F_b of
             q_a q(N_{F_b}(a))  <=  2 M_x .                (MID)

  Why.  An original edge au contributes b q_a q_u to the left side
  exactly when a is middle at that level; but b q_a q_u is at most the
  contribution of its image edge to M_x, since the coefficient kappa on
  that image is at least 1.  An original vertex is middle at exactly one
  level, and a directed edge at a level has at most one preimage.  So
  each original edge is charged at most once from each of its two ends,
  giving the factor 2.

Assume for now that every neighborhood weight in every F_b is at most
e^ell, where 32 <= ell <= ell_0.

FIRST DELETION.  At every level, delete each vertex whose inherited
price satisfies x_i / b < ell^{-4}.  (These are very cheap vertices.)

  Cost in T.  Fix a middle vertex a of F_b before this deletion, let
  D = N_{F_b}(a), and let S be the part of D being deleted.  Then
  S union D = D lies in a ball of radius 1 and meets each part in at
  most one vertex; q(S), q(D) <= e^ell; and alpha_i, beta_i <= ell^{-4}
  on S.  Apply (CROSS) with h = ell: e_q(S, D) is at most
  O_r(q(D)/ell^2).  (For q(D) > ell^{-2} the exponential term is
  absorbed by q(D)/ell^2; for q(D) <= ell^{-2} simply use
  e_q(S,D) <= q(D)^2 <= q(D)/ell^2.)  A deleted triangle has a middle
  vertex (not itself deleted, since a middle vertex has x_a/b >= 1) and
  an edge from S into D in that middle vertex's neighborhood.
  Multiplying by b q_a and summing, then using (MID), bounds the loss in
  T by O_r(M_x / ell^2).

  After this deletion, the total edge mass across levels is bounded:
        sum over b of b * sum over edges uv of F_b of q_u q_v
             <= O(log ell) M_x .                          (EDGE)
  Indeed, for an original edge let x_min be the smaller of its two
  prices.  If it has a preimage at level b, then b ell^{-4} <= x_min
  (deletion rule) and x_min < 2b (no edge with two high ends survives
  into F_b).  There are only O(log ell) dyadic choices for b, and at
  each such level the edge has at most one preimage whose mass
  b q_u q_v is at most the original edge's contribution to M_x.

SECOND DELETION.  Delete an edge uv whenever the q-weight of its common
neighborhood q(N(u) cap N(v)) is less than ell^{-1}, continuing until
none remain.  Deleting uv destroys triangle mass at most b q_u q_v /
ell.  So (EDGE) bounds this further loss by O((log ell) M_x / ell).
Discard isolated vertices too.

After these two deletions, fix a nonempty level graph F = F_b.  All
neighborhoods below are in F, and, with N(u) the F-neighborhood,
        S_u := q(N(u)) in [ell^{-1}, e^ell] ,
        c_{uv} := q(N(u) cap N(v)) >= ell^{-1}    for every edge uv ,
        h_u := sum_{v in N(u)} q_v c_{uv} >= S_u / ell .        (PREP)
The lower bound on S_u holds because u has a neighbor and every edge
has common-neighborhood weight at least ell^{-1}.  Property (PREP) is
what the splitting lemma needs.

6.2  The splitting lemma.

  LEMMA I (splitting a level graph).
  Let 32 <= ell <= ell_0, and let F be a nonempty finite graph with no
  isolated vertices, carrying a homomorphism into some H_b injective on
  directed edges.  Give its vertices the inherited weights, prices, and
  parts, and suppose (PREP) holds.  Then there is a graph F' obtained by
  SPLITTING VERTICES (replacing each vertex by several copies of equal
  weight) and retaining at most one copy of each edge of F, with vertex
  weights unchanged, such that

    (SP1)  every neighborhood of F' has q-weight at most e^{sqrt ell} ;
    (SP2)  sum over triangles of F' of q_a q_u q_v
             >= (1 - C_2 (log ell)/sqrt ell) *
                sum over triangles of F  of q_a q_u q_v ,

  where C_2 depends only on r.  The projection F' -> F is a
  homomorphism injective on directed edges.

6.2.1  How the splitting works, in words.

At each vertex u, and for each neighbor v of u, we must decide which
COPY of u the edge uv attaches to.  We do this by attaching a LABEL in
N(u) to each incidence (u, v): the edge uv attaches to the copy of u
named by that label.  So u is split into copies (u, y), one for each
label y in N(u), each of weight q_u, and every edge of F goes to the
copy at each end that its label names.  Any particular copy of u thus
collects exactly those neighbors v whose incidence (u,v) got the label
that names this copy.

For a triangle {u, v, z} to survive the splitting, its three edges must
land on compatible copies: at each corner, the two incidences of the
triangle at that corner must receive the SAME label.  (If the labels at
some corner differ, the two edges go to different copies of that
corner, and the triangle is broken.)  This is illustrated in the
original figure: an old edge set dividing into two groups at u, with
the "blue" triangles surviving and the "cross" triangle destroyed.

We need two things to hold together:

  (i) at a typical triangle corner, the two labels should AGREE with
      high probability (so most triangles survive);
  (ii) at each copy (u, y), the total weight of attached neighbors
      should be small, at most e^{sqrt ell} (so neighborhoods shrink).

Requirement (i) is handled by choosing the label laws so that nearby
label distributions are close, using an entropy bound (Section 6.3) and
a coupling (Section 6.4).  Requirement (ii) is handled by a
QUALIFICATION rule: we only keep an incidence (u,v) with label y if the
reverse transition probability K_u(y, v) is not too small; this
directly bounds the weight a copy can collect.  Sections 6.3 and 6.4
make both quantitative and show few incidences are discarded.

6.3  Entropy of random walks inside a neighborhood.

Fix a nonempty level graph F = F_b with property (PREP), and let t be
its unordered triangle mass,
        t = sum over triangles {a,u,v} of F of q_a q_u q_v > 0 .
Choose an ORDERED triangle (u, v, z), with each of the six orderings
equally likely once the weighted choice is made: the probability of
(u, v, z) is q_u q_v q_z / (6 t).  Its first two vertices (together
with the conditional law of v given u) have laws
        mu(u, v)   = q_u q_v c_{uv} / (6 t) ,          (TLAW)
        pi_u(v)    = q_v c_{uv} / h_u .
The marginal of u is q_u h_u / (6 t), and mu is symmetric in u, v.

Inside N(u) define transition kernels (probabilities on N(u))
        P_u(v, z) = 1_{vz in E(F)} * q_z / c_{uv} ,
        Q_u       = (I + P_u) / 2 ,
        K_u^{(j)} = P_u Q_u^{j}     (j >= 0).
All denominators are positive by (PREP).  The kernel P_u is reversible
with stationary law pi_u, since
        pi_u(v) P_u(v, z) = q_v q_z 1_{vz in E(F)} / h_u
is symmetric in v, z.  The same holds for Q_u and K_u^{(j)} (polynomials
in P_u).  Writing K_u^{(j)} = Q_u^{j} P_u and using c_{uv} >= ell^{-1}
gives the density bound
        K_u^{(j)}(v, z) <= ell * q_z .                 (DENS)

Since N(u) can have very many vertices even when its total weight is
small, we measure entropy RELATIVE to q: for a probability row p on a
finite set with positive weights q,
        H_q(p) = sum_{z : p(z) > 0} p(z) log( q_z / p(z) ) .
This number may be negative; Jensen's inequality gives H_q(p) <=
log q(support of p).  Define
        H_j = E_{(u,v) ~ mu} H_q( K_u^{(j)}(v, .) ) ,
        R   = ceil(ell) .
The density bound gives H_j >= -log ell.  The next lemma is the crux: it
shows that H_R, though a priori bounded only by ell, is actually small,
so that over R = ceil(ell) steps there is some step with tiny entropy
increase; that tiny step is what lets nearby labels be coupled.

  LEMMA J (walk entropy).
  With the definitions above,
        H_0 >= -log ell ,      H_R <= O_r(log ell) .      (ENT)

  Proof of Lemma J.  Lower bound H_0: at j = 0 the row K_u^{(0)} =
  P_u is supported on N(u) cap N(v) and obeys the density bound, whose
  log gives -log ell; this holds for every (u,v), so H_0 >= -log ell.
  Only the upper bound H_R <= O_r(log ell) needs work.

  Fix a vertex v.  Define expanding sets D_0, D_1, ..., D_R by
        theta = ell^{-6} e^{-ell} ,
        D_0   = N(v) ,
        D_{j+1} = D_j union { z : q(N(z) cap D_j) >= theta } .
  So a vertex z joins the next set when it has at least weight theta of
  neighbors already in D_j.  The sets D_j lie in the ball of radius
  j + 1 about v.

  Mass growth:  Double counting edges between D_j and its newly added
  vertices gives
        theta * q(D_{j+1} \ D_j) <= e^ell * q(D_j) ,
  because each vertex of D_j has total neighborhood weight at most
  e^ell.  Iterating,
        q(D_R) <= e^ell (1 + ell^6 e^{2ell})^R <= e^{20 ell^2} ,  (GROW)
  since 6 log ell <= ell and R <= 2 ell for ell >= 32.

  Part condition:  Fix a part D'.  Each vertex in (D_{j+1} \ D_j) cap D'
  has at least theta weight of neighbors in D_j, and each vertex of D_j
  has at most ONE neighbor in D' (property (PART)).  So
        theta * |(D_{j+1} \ D_j) cap D'| <= q(D_j) .
  Starting from |N(v) cap D'| <= 1 and summing over j < R, together with
  (GROW),
        |D_R cap D'| <= 1 + R theta^{-1} e^{20 ell^2}
                     <= e^{40 ell^2} <= P_0 .               (PARTS)
  Also R + 1 <= R_0 because R = ceil(ell) <= 2 ell_0 for large Lambda.

  Leakage:  For every u in N(v) and 0 <= j <= R,
        K_u^{(j)}(v, N(u) \ D_j) <= j ell^{-4} .            (LEAK)
  At j = 0 the row is supported on N(u) cap N(v) = D_0.  For the step,
  take z in N(u) \ D_{j+1}.  Mass reaching z from D_j uses an
  off-diagonal step of Q_u; by (DENS) and the off-diagonal bound
  Q_u(y, z) <= ell q_z (valid since the I-part of Q_u has no off-diagonal
  term), this mass is at most
        sum_{y in N(z) cap D_j} (ell q_y)(ell q_z) <= ell^2 q_z theta
        = q_z ell^{-4} e^{-ell} .
  Summing over such z gives at most ell^{-4}, as q(N(u)) <= e^ell.  The
  mass arriving from outside D_j is at most the previous leakage, giving
  (LEAK).

  An exceptional neighborhood set:  Define
        J_v = { u in N(v) : q(N(u) cap D_R) > ell^8 } .
  Apply (CROSS) to the sets N(v) and D_R with h = 20 ell^2 and eta = 2.
  The mass, radius (D_R in a ball of radius R+1 <= R_0), and part
  (PARTS) conditions were just verified, and h = 20 ell^2 <= 20 ell_0^2.
  Since S_v = q(N(v)) >= ell^{-1}, the exponential term e^{-3h} is
  absorbed, giving
        ell^8 q(J_v) <= e_q(N(v), D_R) <= O_r(ell^2 S_v) ,
        so  q(J_v) <= O_r(ell^{-6} S_v) .
  Now use (CROSS) a SECOND time, on the pair J_v, N(v), with h = ell and
  eta = 2.  It converts the q-mass bound on J_v into the conditional law
  used by the triangle measure mu (which weights u by c_{uv}):
        P_mu(u in J_v | v) = (sum_{u in J_v} q_u c_{uv}) / h_v
                           = e_q(J_v, N(v)) / h_v
                           <= O_r( (ell q(J_v) + e^{-3ell}) / (S_v/ell) )
                           <= O_r(ell^{-4}) .            (EXC)
  Here the two sets are contained in N(v), so the radius-one, one-per-
  part conditions hold trivially.

  Bounding H_R:  Fix v and consider a row K_u^{(R)}(v, .).  Split it by
  membership in D_R.  Its mass outside D_R is at most R ell^{-4} by
  (LEAK), while the two possible supports have q-weight at most ell^8
  (inside, when u is outside J_v) and e^ell (outside D_R).  The entropy
  of such a mixture is at most
        log 2 + 8 log ell + (R ell^{-4}) ell .
  (Conditioning on the two supports adds at most the binary entropy
  log 2; the conditional entropy is bounded by the log of the support
  weight, giving 8 log ell inside D_R, and the small leakage mass
  contributes the last term.)  Empty supports contribute nothing.  For
  u in J_v use the crude bound ell; by (EXC) these u carry only
  probability O_r(ell^{-4}) under the law of (u,v).  Averaging first over
  u conditional on v and then over v therefore proves
        H_R = O_r(log ell) .   (Done.)

6.4  Coupled labels and the split graph.

We now prove Lemma I.  Throughout F and ell are as in (PREP), the
triangle mass is t, and the kernels and entropies are from 6.3.

  Distance between label laws.  For two probability rows p, a on the
  same finite set, write
        KL(p || a) = sum_{z : p(z) > 0} p(z) log( p(z) / a(z) ) ,
        TV(p, a)   = (1/2) sum_z | p(z) - a(z) | ,
  with KL = +infinity if a vanishes where p does not.  We shall use
        TV(p, a) <= sqrt( KL(p || a) ) .                 (KLS)
  Proof: let rho = sum_z sqrt( p(z) a(z) ).  Jensen gives
  KL(p||a) >= -2 log rho >= 2(1 - rho), and Cauchy-Schwarz gives
  TV(p,a)^2 <= 1 - rho^2 <= 2(1 - rho); the infinite case is immediate.

  Entropy increment.  Since K_u^{(j+1)} = Q_u K_u^{(j)}, the row
  K_u^{(j+1)}(v, .) is the mixture of the rows K_u^{(j)}(z, .) taken
  with z chosen from Q_u(v, .).  The entropy of a mixture minus the
  average entropy of its constituents equals the average divergence of
  the constituents from the mixture, and the log q_z terms cancel in
  this identity.  Averaging over (u,v) ~ mu and using that pi_u is
  stationary and reversible for Q_u (which identifies the average
  constituent entropy as H_j) gives
        H_{j+1} - H_j
          = E_{ (u,v)~mu, z~Q_u(v,.) }
              KL( K_u^{(j)}(z,.) || K_u^{(j+1)}(v,.) )  >= 0 .   (EI)

  A good step.  By Lemma J, H_R - H_0 <= O_r(log ell).  Telescoping
  the nonnegative increments (EI) over the R steps, some step
  0 <= j < R has
        H_{j+1} - H_j <= O_r( (log ell)/ell ) .           (SMALL)
  Fix this j and abbreviate K_u = K_u^{(j)}.

  Corner agreement.  Use the row K_u^{(j+1)}(v, .) as an intermediary in
  the triangle inequality for TV, and that Q_u = (I + P_u)/2, to get
        E_{ (u,v)~mu, z~P_u(v,.) } TV( K_u(v,.) , K_u(z,.) )
          <= 2 sqrt( H_{j+1} - H_j )
          <= O_r( sqrt( (log ell)/ell ) ) .                (CORNER)
  (The two expected distances to the intermediary sum to twice the
  expected distance from the z-row under Q_u; apply (KLS), then
  Cauchy-Schwarz, then (SMALL).)  The law on the left of (CORNER) is
  exactly the ordered triangle law, because
        mu(u,v) P_u(v,z) = q_u q_v q_z / (6 t)
  whenever u, v, z form a triangle.

  Simultaneous coupling.  At each vertex u we must assign to all its
  incidences (u,v) a label Y_{u,v} in N(u), so that Y_{u,v} has
  marginal law K_u(v, .), and do so for all incidences at u at once.
  Here is a finite construction that keeps nearby labels close, with
  the disagreement bound of Angel and Spinka.  Take one shared
  sequence of independent proposals; each proposal is a uniformly
  chosen label together with an independent uniform mark in [0,1].
  Each law accepts the first proposal whose mark is at most its
  probability of that label.  The accepted label then has the
  prescribed law, and every law accepts almost surely.  For two laws at
  total variation distance d, the first proposal accepted by EITHER law
  is accepted by BOTH with probability (1 - d)/(1 + d), because the
  pointwise minimum and maximum of the two probability vectors have
  sums 1 - d and 1 + d.  So the two labels disagree with probability at
  most 2d/(1 + d) <= 2d.  A single proposal sequence delivers this
  bound simultaneously for every pair of laws.  Applying it to the
  rows of K_u and using (CORNER) bounds the AVERAGE probability that
  the labels disagree at a triangle corner by
        O_r( sqrt( (log ell)/ell ) ) .                    (AGREE)
  Couplings at distinct vertices may be chosen independently.

  Qualification.  Agreement controls triangle retention, but we also need
  the copies to have small neighborhoods.  Call an incidence (u,v),
  with label y = Y_{u,v}, QUALIFIED if
        K_u(y, v) >= q_v * e^{-sqrt ell} .                (QUAL)
  The point of using the REVERSE transition K_u(y,v): at a copy of u
  named by y, summing K_u(y,v) over its attached neighbors v sums a
  single probability row, which is what bounds the copy's neighborhood
  weight.  We first show few incidences are unqualified.

  Choose (u,v) ~ mu and then y ~ K_u(v, .).  Conditional on u, the joint
  law of (v, y) is pi_u(v) K_u(v, y), which is symmetric in v, y by
  reversibility.  So the reverse entry K_u(y,v) is positive almost
  surely, and exchanging v and y,
        E log( q_v / K_u(y, v) )
          = E log( q_y / K_u(v, y) )
          = H_j <= H_R = O_r(log ell) .                    (REV)
  The random logarithm on the left is at least -log ell by (DENS).  Add
  log ell and apply Markov's inequality to this nonnegative variable.
  Lemma J then gives
        P( (u,v) is not qualified )
          <= ( H_j + log ell ) / ( sqrt ell + log ell )
          <= O_r( (log ell)/sqrt ell ) .                   (FAILQ)

  Building the split graph.  For each vertex u make copies (u, y),
  indexed by y in N(u), each of weight q_u (so a copy keeps the original
  vertex weight; splitting creates several copies of total weight
  |N(u)| q_u, but each individual copy has the original weight).
  Retain an edge uv only if BOTH incidences are qualified, and then join
  copy (u, Y_{u,v}) to copy (v, Y_{v,u}).  This is a simple graph: each
  old edge contributes at most one edge, and its two ends project to
  distinct old vertices.  The neighbors of a copy (u, y) have total
  weight at most
        sum_{v in N(u) : Y_{u,v} = y, uv retained} q_v
          <= e^{sqrt ell} sum_{v in N(u)} K_u(y, v)
          = e^{sqrt ell} ,                                 (SPLITNB)
  by (QUAL), because the last sum is a full probability row.  This
  proves (SP1).

  Triangle retention.  An old triangle {u,v,z} survives with its
  original mass q_u q_v q_z only if all SIX of its incidences are
  qualified and its two labels AGREE at each of its THREE corners.  In
  the ordered triangle law, each incidence has law mu and each corner
  has the law used in (CORNER).  A union bound over six incidences
  (each unqualified with probability O_r((log ell)/sqrt ell) by (FAILQ))
  and three corners (each disagreeing with average probability
  O_r(sqrt((log ell)/ell)) by (AGREE)), followed by averaging with the
  triangle weights, shows that the expected fraction of triangle mass
  lost is at most
        O_r( (log ell)/sqrt ell + sqrt( (log ell)/ell ) )
          = O_r( (log ell)/sqrt ell ) .                   (LOSS)
  Choose a realization of the couplings that loses no more than this
  expectation.  Then (SP2) holds.  The projection F' -> F sends a copy
  to its original vertex; it is a homomorphism, and it is injective on
  directed edges because each old edge is used at most once.  Composing
  with the old map to H_b preserves injectivity on directed edges, and
  all vertex weights and inherited data are unchanged.  This proves
  Lemma I.  (Done.)

6.5  Iterating the reduction.  The triangle bound.

  PROPOSITION H (triangle bound).
  For the level graphs H_b, rescaled weights q, and prices constructed
  from (DT) with A chosen as in Lemma F, the quantities T_0 and M_x
  satisfy
        T_0 <= O_r(M_x) ,
  with implicit constant depending only on r.

  Proof.  Apply the two deletions of 6.1 to all levels, then Lemma I to
  each nonempty level graph (empty ones need no construction).  The
  resulting graphs have all neighborhood weights at most e^{sqrt ell}
  and still carry homomorphisms into their H_b injective on directed
  edges.  Combining the additive losses from the deletions with the
  retention (SP2) of Lemma I gives
        T_new >= (1 - C_2 (log ell)/sqrt ell) T
                 - C_3 (log ell)/ell M_x ,                (ITER)
  where C_2, C_3 depend only on r.  (If the coefficient of T is
  negative, (ITER) is trivial since T_new >= 0.)

  Now choose a large constant X >= 32 depending only on r.  Starting at
  ell_0, perform the reduction while ell > X, replacing ell by sqrt ell
  each time (so ell shrinks doubly exponentially).  Both losses in
  (ITER) are summable uniformly in the initial value ell_0.  To see
  this, let p(s) = (log s)/sqrt s.  This decreases for large s and
  satisfies p(s^2)/p(s) = 2/sqrt s.  Read in reverse, the successive
  reduction parameters start above X and are squared each time, so
  taking X large makes the total of C_2 p(ell) at most 1/2, while the
  total of C_3 (log ell)/ell is bounded by a constant depending only on
  r.  Iterating (ITER) and using prod_j(1 - a_j) >= 1 - sum_j a_j for
  these nonnegative losses,
        T_final >= (1/2) T_0 - O_r(M_x) .                 (RETAIN)

  At the final stage every neighborhood has weight at most e^X.  Every
  triangle still has a middle vertex.  At a middle vertex a, the
  incident triangle mass (including the factor b) is at most
        b q_a q(N(a))^2 / 2 ,
  so by the charge lemma (MID),
        T_final <= (e^X / 2) * sum over b of b *
                   sum over middle a of q_a q(N(a)) <= e^X M_x .
  This estimate also holds if ell_0 <= X and no reduction was needed.
  Combined with (RETAIN) it gives T_0 = O_r(M_x), proving Proposition
  H.  (Done.)

6.6  Completing the proof of Lemma D.

  By Proposition H, the contribution to sum_i x_i t_i from triangles
  all of whose vertices are good is O_r(M_x) = O_r(sum_i x_i d_i).  By
  (BAD), the contribution from triangles meeting a bad vertex is at most
  O(Lambda^2) sum_i w_i y_i.  Taking B = B(r) sufficiently large and
  then raising the lower threshold Lambda_r so that Lambda^2 is
  swallowed by Lambda^4, we get exactly the dual target
        sum_i x_i t_i <= B sum_i x_i d_i + Lambda^4 sum_i w_i y_i ,
  which is (DTG).  The separation argument of 5.1 now supplies the
  finite family of multiplier and drop operations with rates satisfying
  (V) and (R).  The constants are chosen in this order: A and C_1 from
  Lemma F; then the reduction constants and X from Proposition H; then
  B; finally the threshold Lambda_r so that the O(Lambda^2) bad-vertex
  term is at most Lambda^4.  Every choice depends only on r.  This
  completes the proof of Lemma D.  (Done.)

================================================================
PART 7.  COLORING IN ROUNDS WITH DEPENDENT ENTRY WEIGHTS
================================================================

We now use Lemma D to build a partial correspondence coloring that
increases in small randomized rounds.  This is the entropy-compression
scheme of Johansson, adapted to correspondence coloring: because
weights within a list need not be independent, the PARTICIPATION bound
(R) of Lemma D is what will later supply concentration.

7.1  Constants and parameters.

Fix r >= 4 and let B = B(r) be the constant of Lemma D.  Choose
        K = 16 (B + 2) ,   gamma = 0.003 ,
        C = 100 K / gamma ,
        k = ceil( C Delta / log Delta ) .
We prove Theorem A with C_r = C.  Before starting, RESTRICT every list
to exactly k of its entries and restrict the matchings accordingly;
write L(v) again for the restricted lists.  For large Delta we have
k <= Delta.  Throughout, Delta is the maximum degree of the ORIGINAL
graph, fixed once and for all.  Set
        w_*   = K / k ,
        Lambda = 20 log Delta ,
        tau   = Delta^{-0.10} ,
        xi    = Delta^{-0.14} ,
        zeta  = Delta^{-0.15} .
The parameter tau controls how fast entries activate and how fast edits
fire; xi is the permitted error per round in our bookkeeping; zeta is a
smaller tolerance used in the concentration estimates.  All asymptotic
statements below are uniform over all graphs, lists, matchings, and
states satisfying the stated assumptions, with r fixed; a factor
Delta^{o(1)} is at most Delta^epsilon for every fixed epsilon > 0 once
Delta is large in terms of r and epsilon.  At Lambda = 20 log Delta, the
parameters U and P_0 of Lemma D are Delta^{o(1)} and R_0 =
O(log log Delta), exactly the scales we need for participation.

7.2  The state: partial coloring, entry graph, weights, potentials.

Consider a proper partial correspondence coloring, and let G' be the
subgraph induced by the still-uncolored vertices.  An entry at an
uncolored vertex is AVAILABLE if it conflicts with none of the colors
already chosen at neighboring vertices.  Give each entry i in L(v), for
v in G', a nonnegative weight w_i, requiring that every positive-weight
entry be available.  Let H be the graph on the positive-weight entries
whose edges are the forbidden pairs in the restricted matchings; its
parts are the sets L(v) cap V(H).  As in Part 3, the map sending an
entry to its host vertex is a homomorphism H -> G', a clique in H has
distinct hosts, so H is K_r-free, and each entry has at most one
neighbor in any part.  These are exactly the hypotheses of Lemma D.
Use s_i, d_i, t_i for the neighborhood, incident-edge, and triangle
weights in H, as in Lemma D.  We maintain
        Delta^{-3} <= w_i <= Delta^{-0.99} ,   s_i <= Lambda
        for every positive-weight entry i .                (WB)
For v in G' and vu in E(G'), define
        W_v  = sum_{i in L(v)} w_i ,
        D_vu = sum over forbidden pairs (i,l), i in L(v), l in L(u),
               of w_i w_l ,
        D_v  = sum_{u in N_{G'}(v)} D_vu ,
        F_v  = sum_{i in L(v)} w_* f(w_i / w_*)
               + gamma log Delta * (K - W_v) + D_v .        (POT)
The entropy sum includes zero-weight entries, using f(0) = 1.  Both the
entropy sum and D_v are nonnegative, and D_v = sum_{i in L(v) cap V(H)}
d_i <= Lambda W_v.  So an upper bound on F_v keeps W_v from getting too
small, while coloring neighbors lowers the edge term.

Initially no vertex is colored and every entry has weight w_*.  Since
w_* = Theta(log Delta / Delta) and s_i <= Delta w_* <= (K/C) log Delta,
the bounds (WB) hold.  Moreover,
        W_v = K ,     F_v = D_v <= K^2 Delta / k <= (K^2/C) log Delta .
                                                              (INIT)

7.3  The one-round proposition.

  PROPOSITION K (one coloring round).
  For all sufficiently large Delta depending only on r, the following
  holds.  Suppose a proper partial correspondence coloring and entry
  weights satisfy availability, (WB), and
        K/2 <= W_v <= 2K   for every v in G' (mass window).   (MW)
  Then there is an extension of the partial coloring and a choice of
  weights on the remaining entries that preserve availability and (WB),
  and satisfy, for every vertex v that remains uncolored,
        W_v^new <= W_v + xi ,
        F_v^new <= F_v + xi ,
        deg_new(v)/Delta <= (1 - tau) deg_{G'}(v)/Delta + xi .  (RC)

Proposition K is proved in 7.4 to 7.7.  Once it holds, Part 9 iterates
it and finishes the coloring.

7.4  The random update.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition K; the case G' empty is
immediate.  Fix the operations and rates a_j given by Lemma D on H,
with listed supports J_j.  We use two families of mutually independent
Bernoulli flags:

  - For each entry z in V(H) an ACTIVATION flag, set with probability
    tau w_z.  When set, it touches every neighbor of z in H, pinning
    their multiplier to 0.
  - For each operation j an OPERATION flag, set with probability
    tau a_j.  When set, it touches J_j; a multiplier operation applies
    its prescribed multipliers m_i^{(j)} on J_j, a drop applies
    multiplier 0 on its singleton support.

These probabilities are valid for large Delta: each operation has
nonempty support and rate at most Lambda^4 by Lemma D.

For each i in V(H) put bar w_i = w_i Y_i.  The multiplier Y_i is
defined as follows.  If exactly one set flag touches i, let Y_i be its
designated multiplier.  If at least two set flags touch i, let Y_i = 0.
If no set flag touches i, put
        Y_i = 1 + tau * ( s_i + sum_{j in M} a_j (1 - m_i^{(j)}) ) ,
                                                                 (NOFLAG)
where M indexes the multiplier operations and their multipliers are
extended by 1 off the listed supports.  Zero-weight entries stay at
zero.  The coefficient of tau in (NOFLAG) has absolute value at most
Lambda + Lambda^4 (1 + U), so the no-flag multiplier is positive and
equals 1 + O(tau Delta^{o(1)}).

7.5  Activations that become colors; provisional weights.

An entry ACTIVATES when its activation flag is set.  An activating
entry is SUCCESSFUL if none of its neighbors in H activates.  At every
host with a successful entry, choose one such entry as that host's
color, using any fixed order on the list to break ties.  Two facts:

  - Successful entries cannot be adjacent in H (if they were, each
    would be a neighbor of the other's activated entry, contradicting
    success), and they were available before the round, so this extends
    the proper partial coloring.
  - Every entry that conflicts with a newly chosen color is touched by
    its activation flag (the color came from an activating entry), hence
    receives provisional weight zero, whether or not other flags also
    touch it.

Consequently, after the newly colored vertices are removed, every
remaining POSITIVE provisional weight is available: a positive weight
means no zeroing flag touched it, so it conflicts with no chosen color.
The set of hosts that receive a color depends only on the activation
flags, independently of the choices among successful entries.

The PROVISIONAL weights are bar w_i = w_i Y_i on the whole start-of-
round graph G'.  Until the colored vertices are removed, a bar on any
quantity in (POT) means it is computed with these provisional weights
on all of G'.  We may keep using E(H) in those sums because a zero
weight stays zero.  The compensation term in (NOFLAG) cancels the
first-order mean loss from activations and multiplier operations; what
remains is controlled by Lemma D.  Here is the estimate.

  LEMMA L (expected changes).
  For the random update just defined,
        E bar W_v  <= W_v + o(xi) ,
        E bar F_v  <= F_v + tau (B+1) D_v + o(xi) ,
        E bar D_vu >= (1/2) D_vu      for every vu in E(G') .   (EXP)

  Proof of Lemma L.  Let a_i^- be the sum of the rates of the DROP
  operations at i (so a drop at i fires with probability tau a_i^-).
  Expanding according to the set flags touching one or two entries
  gives, with il an edge of H in the last line,

        E(Y_i - 1) = -tau a_i^- + O(tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}) ,        (M1)
        E f(Y_i)  = tau ( s_i + a_i^-
                     + sum_{j in M} a_j f(m_i^{(j)}) )
                     + O(tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}) ,                   (M2)
        E[(Y_i - 1)(Y_l - 1)]
          = tau * sum_{z in N_H(i) cap N_H(l)} w_z
            + tau * sum_{j in M} a_j (m_i^{(j)} - 1)(m_l^{(j)} - 1)
            + O(tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}) .                            (M3)

  Justification of the errors and first-order terms.  The total
  probability that some flag touches either of two fixed entries is at
  most 2 tau (Lambda + Lambda^4).  Independence bounds both the
  probability of two or more such flags and the total error from
  replacing the exact single-flag probabilities by their marginal
  probabilities by O(tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}); all multipliers and their
  f-values are Delta^{o(1)}, so these errors keep this order.
    (M1): the single flags touching i contribute
    -s_i - a_i^- + sum_{j in M} a_j (m_i^{(j)} - 1) to the coefficient
    of tau, while the no-flag value (NOFLAG) contributes
    +s_i + sum_{j in M} a_j (1 - m_i^{(j)}); the two cancel down to
    -a_i^-.  (Activations and drops have designated multiplier 0, so
    they contribute their term to -s_i and -a_i^-.)
    (M2): use f(0) = 1 for the entries pinned to 0, and f(1+h) = O(h^2)
    at the no-flag value, which is 1 + O(tau Delta^{o(1)}).
    (M3): to first order only flags touching BOTH entries matter.  These
    are (a) activations at a common neighbor z, pinning both to 0, so
    (Y_i - 1)(Y_l - 1) = 1; and (b) shared multiplier flags.  A
    singleton drop cannot touch both ends, and a flag touching only one
    end picks up an extra factor O(tau Delta^{o(1)}) from the other end.

  For every i in V(H), including when Y_i = 0, the entropy change is
        w_* [ f(w_i Y_i / w_*) - f(w_i / w_*) ]
          = w_i log( w_i / w_* ) (Y_i - 1) + w_i f(Y_i) .        (ENTC)
  (Direct expansion of f(a) = a log a - a + 1.)

  Allocate the change in F_v to its positive-weight entries.  The
  contribution of i in L(v) cap V(H) to F_v^bar - F_v is
        w_i [ ( log(w_i/w_*) - gamma log Delta )(Y_i - 1) + f(Y_i) ]
          + sum_{l in N_H(i)} w_i w_l ( Y_i Y_l - 1 ) .       (ENTRY)
  This combines (ENTC) for the entropy, the -gamma log Delta times the
  change of (K - W_v), and the change on the edges out of i (each such
  edge has i in L(v) and the other end outside L(v), since L(v) is an
  independent set in H).  Its expectation is at most
        tau * ( d_i + t_i + sum_j a_j g_i^{(j)} )
          + O( (w_i + d_i) tau^2 Delta^{o(1)} ) .             (EENTRY)

  Indeed, substitute (M1), (M2), (M3) into (ENTRY):
    - The activation edge-correlations in (M3) give tau t_i, and the
      activation entropy in (M2) gives tau d_i.
    - For a multiplier operation j, the compensated linear terms from
      (M1) and the log terms cancel, leaving exactly the entropy piece
      tau w_i f(m_i^{(j)}) and the quadratic edge pieces
      tau w_i w_l (m_i^{(j)} - 1)(m_l^{(j)} - 1); these are precisely
      tau g_i^{(j)}.
    - A drop at i (folded into a_i^-) contributes
      w_i (1 - log(w_i/w_*) + gamma log Delta) <= Lambda w_i to the
      non-edge part (using (WB)) and -d_i to the edge part, matching the
      drop vector of Lemma D.  A drop at a neighbor l contributes
      -w_i w_l, again matching the drop vector.
    - The residuals are O((w_i + d_i) tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}), because
      |log(w_i/w_*)| = O(log Delta) by (WB) and the higher-order errors
      in (M1) to (M3) carry a factor tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}.

  Sum (EENTRY) over i in L(v) cap V(H) and use the key inequality (V) of
  Lemma D, t_i + sum_j a_j g_i^{(j)} <= B d_i.  Since D_v <= Lambda W_v
  and W_v <= 2K, the total error is
        O( (W_v + D_v) tau^2 Delta^{o(1)} ) = o(xi) .
  This proves the potential estimate E bar F_v <= F_v + tau (B+1) D_v +
  o(xi).  The mass estimate E bar W_v <= W_v + o(xi) follows from (M1):
        E bar W_v - W_v = sum_{i in L(v)} w_i E(Y_i - 1)
                        = -tau sum_{i in L(v) cap V(H)} w_i a_i^-
                          + O(k tau^2 Delta^{o(1)}) ,
  which is negative up to o(xi) since a_i^- >= 0 and k <= Delta.  For
  the edge estimate, fix an edge il in the sum defining D_vu.  The
  probability that neither endpoint is touched by any set flag is
  1 - O(tau Delta^{o(1)}); on that event Y_i Y_l = 1 + O(tau
  Delta^{o(1)}), and on every other event w_i w_l Y_i Y_l >= 0.  Hence
        E( w_i w_l Y_i Y_l ) >= w_i w_l / 2
  for large Delta, and summing over i in L(v), l in L(u) gives
  E bar D_vu >= (1/2) D_vu.  This completes the proof of Lemma L.
  (Done.)

================================================================
PART 8.  CONCENTRATION WITH BOUNDED INPUT PARTICIPATION
================================================================

The expectation bounds of Lemma L must be made to hold SIMULTANEOUSLY
at all vertices.  The trouble is that after a round the weights within
one list are not independent; they are functions of shared flags.  To
handle this we need a concentration inequality for sums of bounded
functions of independent inputs in which each input influences only a
few summands.  This is the read-k concentration method of Gavinsky,
Lovett, Saks, and Srinivasan, resting on Finner's generalized Holder
inequality and Hoeffding's exponential-moment bound.  We prove it, so
the argument is self-contained.

  LEMMA M (bounded input participation).
  Let X_1, ..., X_m be bounded real functions of finitely many
  independent inputs.  Suppose X_j has range of length at most b_j, and
  that each input is used by at most p of the functions, where p >= 1
  is real.  If sum_j b_j^2 > 0, then for every a > 0,

        P( sum_j (X_j - E X_j) >= a )  and
        P( sum_j (X_j - E X_j) <= -a )
          <= exp( -2 a^2 / (p * sum_j b_j^2) ) .          (CONC)

  If sum_j b_j^2 = 0, both probabilities are 0.

  Proof.  For nonnegative bounded functions Z_j with these same
  dependence sets, we first prove the generalized Holder inequality
        E prod_j Z_j <= prod_j ( E Z_j^p )^{1/p} .          (HOLD)
  Induct on the number of independent inputs.  Condition on all inputs
  except the last, and let I index the (at most p) functions that use
  the last input.  Apply Holder's inequality with exponent p to the
  factors indexed by I (and, when |I| < p, a constant factor 1 with
  reciprocal exponent 1 - |I|/p).  This bounds the conditional integral
  by prod_j Z~_j, where Z~_j = ( E_last Z_j^p )^{1/p} for j in I and
  Z~_j = Z_j otherwise.  Each Z~_j uses only the remaining inputs of
  Z_j, so the participation bound still holds; the induction hypothesis
  gives E prod_j Z~_j <= prod_j ( E Z~_j^p )^{1/p} = prod_j ( E Z_j^p
  )^{1/p}, proving (HOLD).

  Next, if a random variable X has range of length b, then
        log E e^{ s (X - E X) } <= s^2 b^2 / 8   for all real s .  (HOEV)
  The second derivative of the logarithm on the left is the variance of
  X under the exponentially tilted law.  Any law supported on an
  interval of length b has variance at most b^2/4 (measure squared
  distance from the midpoint).  The logarithm and its first derivative
  vanish at s = 0, which proves (HOEV).

  Now apply (HOLD) to Z_j = e^{ s (X_j - E X_j) } (which for s > 0 are
  nonnegative bounded functions with the same dependence sets), and then
  (HOEV) with the parameter p s to each factor.  The resulting moment
  bound is
        E exp( s sum_j (X_j - E X_j) )
          <= exp( p s^2 sum_j b_j^2 / 8 ) .
  Exponential Markov's inequality with s = 4a / (p sum_j b_j^2) proves
  the upper tail.  Replacing X_j by -X_j proves the lower tail.  (Done.)

8.1  Vertex concentration.

Apply Lemma M first to bar W_v and to the NON-EDGE part bar F_v - bar
D_v of the potential.  Apart from an additive constant, each of these
is a sum of at most k <= Delta functions, one for each entry of L(v),
and each single function has range length at most Delta^{-0.99+o(1)}.
This follows from (WB), (NOFLAG), and U = Delta^{o(1)}; the logarithmic
factors in the entropy term are absorbed in Delta^{o(1)}.  This also
covers arbitrarily small positive provisional weights, because with
0 log 0 = 0 one has sup over 0 <= z <= b of |z log z| = b log(1/b),
which is small when b is small.  Now count how many of these functions
each input influences:

  - a MULTIPLIER flag occurs in at most P_0 of them, by the per-part
    support bound of Lemma D (its listed support meets L(v) in at most
    P_0 entries);
  - a DROP flag occurs in at most one of them (its support is a single
    entry);
  - an ACTIVATION flag occurs in at most one of them, because its
    activating entry has at most one neighbor in L(v) (property (PART)).

So p = P_0 is a valid participation bound.  Therefore each requirement
        bar W_v <= E bar W_v + zeta ,
        bar F_v - bar D_v <= E( bar F_v - bar D_v ) + zeta    (VCON)
fails with probability at most exp(-Delta^{0.5}) for large Delta: with
a = zeta = Delta^{-0.15}, p = Delta^{o(1)}, and sum_j b_j^2 <= k
Delta^{-1.98+o(1)} <= Delta^{-0.98+o(1)}, the exponent in (CONC) is at
least Delta^{0.68-o(1)}.

8.2  Edge concentration.

For an edge vu in E(G'), the matching sum bar D_vu has at most k terms,
each of range length at most Delta^{-1.98+o(1)}.  Every entry at either
endpoint occurs in at most one term, so each flag occurs in at most
2 P_0 terms.  Applying Lemma M with p = 2 P_0 shows that
        | bar D_vu - E bar D_vu | <= Delta^{-1.25}             (ECON)
fails with probability at most 2 exp(-Delta^{0.4}).  Here, with
a = Delta^{-1.25} and sum_j b_j^2 <= k Delta^{-3.96+o(1)} <=
Delta^{-2.96+o(1)}, the exponent is at least Delta^{0.46-o(1)}.

The SEPARATION into non-edge terms and individual matching sums is what
lets the participation bound apply here; no independence among entries
of a list is assumed.  For ordinary list coloring one could instead
choose operations separately within each graph induced by one common
color label, and sampling the flags independently would make distinct
labels independent, giving p = 1.  Arbitrary forbidden-pair matchings
offer no such common labels, and the per-part support bound of Lemma D
replaces that independence.

8.3  Activations that produce colors.

Lemma L allows a potential increase of order tau D_v.  To offset it we
must show that enough neighbors receive colors, measured BOTH by D_vu
and by 1/Delta.  Fix v in G', and take one of the two coefficient
families
        b_u = D_vu    (u in N_{G'}(v)) ,   or   b_u = 1/Delta .   (COEF)
In either case 0 <= b_u <= 2K Delta^{-0.99}: for the first family this
is the matching property D_vu <= Delta^{-0.99} W_v (each entry of L(v)
is forbidden with at most one entry of L(u), each of weight at most
Delta^{-0.99}); for the second it is immediate.

Let A_u indicate that SOME entry at u activates.  The events A_u are
independent over distinct hosts u (they use disjoint activation flags),
and
        P(A_u = 1) >= 1 - e^{-tau W_u} >= tau K / 4              (HOST)
for large Delta, since K/2 <= W_u <= 2K.  Lemma M with p = 1 and
sum_u b_u^2 <= 4K^2 Delta^{-0.98} then gives
        sum_{u in N_{G'}(v)} b_u A_u
          >= (tau K / 4) sum_{u in N_{G'}(v)} b_u - zeta ,
                                                                (ACTMASS)
except with probability at most exp(-Delta^{0.5}).

The loss comes from activations that COLLIDE: a host with an activating
entry but no successful entry gets no color.  The next lemma bounds the
loss.

  LEMMA N (collision bound).
  Let E_v be the set of edges of H with an endpoint hosted at a
  neighbor of v in G', and let X_v count the edges of E_v whose two
  endpoints both activate.  Then
        P( 4K Delta^{-0.99} X_v > zeta )
          <= O(Delta^3) e^{e-1-Delta^{0.01}} + e^{-Delta^{0.8}} .
                                                                (COLLTAIL)
  For either coefficient family (COEF), the sum of b_u over the hosts u
  counted by A_u that receive no color is at most 4K Delta^{-0.99} X_v.

  Proof of Lemma N.  A host that has an activating entry but no
  successful entry must contain an endpoint of an edge counted by X_v:
  its activating entry has an activating neighbor (otherwise it would be
  successful), and that neighbor is joined to it by an edge of E_v.
  Each edge of E_v has at most two hosts, and each coefficient is at
  most 2K Delta^{-0.99}, so the sum of b_u over uncolored hosts counted
  by A_u is at most 4K Delta^{-0.99} X_v.  This is the last assertion.

  Now the tail bound.  First,
        mu_v := E X_v
          <= tau^2 sum_{u in N_{G'}(v)} sum_{i in L(u)} w_i s_i
          <= 2K Delta tau^2 Lambda = Delta^{0.80+o(1)} ,       (MUV)
  since sum_{i in L(u)} w_i s_i <= Lambda W_u <= 2K Lambda and there are
  at most Delta neighbors u.

  For a possible endpoint i of an edge in E_v, the number of its
  activating neighbors is a sum of independent indicators of total mean
  tau s_i <= tau Lambda <= 1.  Its exponential moment at parameter 1 is
  at most exp(e-1), so it exceeds Delta^{0.01} with probability at most
  exp(e-1-Delta^{0.01}).  All possible endpoints are hosted within
  distance two of v in G', and there are at most k <= Delta entries at
  each host, so a union bound over O(Delta^3) endpoints shows that,
  apart from the first error term in (COLLTAIL), the graph formed by the
  edges counted by X_v has maximum degree at most Delta^{0.01}.

  Suppose the bad event of (COLLTAIL) holds, so 4K Delta^{-0.99} X_v >
  zeta, i.e. X_v > Delta^{0.84}/(4K).  In a graph of maximum degree at
  most Delta^{0.01}, a maximal collection of disjoint edges has size at
  least X_v/(2 Delta^{0.01}); for large Delta this is at least
  m = ceil(Delta^{0.82}).  Write q_e = tau^2 w_i w_l for e = il in E_v.
  The events that both endpoints of an edge activate are independent
  along any matching, because a matching has disjoint endpoints and the
  activation flags are independent.  Hence
        P( some size-m matching in E_v has all endpoints activating )
          <= sum_{M subset E_v, M matching, |M|=m} prod_{e in M} q_e
          <= ( sum_{e in E_v} q_e )^m / m!
           = mu_v^m / m!
          <= ( e mu_v / m )^m
          <= e^{-Delta^{0.8}} .                              (MATCHTAIL)
  The second inequality follows by expanding the m-th power: each
  matching appears m! times and all other terms are nonnegative.  The
  last inequality uses (MUV) and m = Delta^{0.82+o(1)} (note
  e mu_v/m = e Delta^{-0.02+o(1)}).  Combining with the first error term
  proves (COLLTAIL).  (Done.)

  Together, (ACTMASS) and Lemma N imply that for EITHER choice in
  (COEF),
        sum over hosts u in N_{G'}(v) that receive a color of b_u
          >= (tau K / 4) sum_{u in N_{G'}(v)} b_u - 2 zeta .   (CMASS)

  Now let E_v be the event that (VCON), or (ECON) for some edge incident
  with v, or (CMASS) for one of the two coefficient families, fails.  A
  union bound (there are at most Delta edges incident with v, and two
  coefficient families) gives, for all sufficiently large Delta,
        P(E_v) <= exp( -Delta^{0.005} ) .                     (FAILV)

8.4  Making all estimates simultaneous: the local lemma.

We must avoid all the bad events E_v at once.  First assemble a
dependency graph.  Assign each multiplier flag to the host of a chosen
center of a radius-R_0 ball in H containing its listed support.
Assign activation and drop flags to the hosts of the activating and
dropped entries, respectively.  By construction, every input that
determines E_v is assigned within distance R_0 + 3 of v in G':

  - the host projection does not increase distances;
  - the provisional weights on an incident matching use flags touching
    entries at its two hosts (distance at most 1 plus the R_0 of a
    support center);
  - whether a neighbor of v receives a color is decided by activation
    flags at distance at most two from v.

So events at distance greater than 2(R_0 + 3) involve disjoint sets of
independent inputs and are independent.  A bound for the maximum degree
of the dependency graph is therefore
        d = (Delta + 1)^{2 R_0 + 10}
          <= exp( O( log Delta * log log Delta ) ) .            (DEPD)

  SYMMETRIC LOCAL LEMMA.  Let finitely many events each have probability
  at most q, with a dependency graph of maximum degree at most d >= 1.
  If q <= 1/(e(d+1)), then all the events can be avoided simultaneously.

  Proof.  Put x = 1/(d+1), so q <= x(1-x)^d (since 1/(e(d+1)) <=
  x(1-x)^d).  We show by induction on the number of conditioned events
  that any event, conditioned on the avoidance of any set of the other
  events, has probability at most x.  Split the conditioning set into
  the NON-neighbors and the at most d neighbors of the event.  By the
  dependency graph, the event is independent of the avoidance of the
  non-neighbors, so the numerator of the conditional probability is at
  most q.  The induction hypothesis bounds, under that conditioning,
  the probability of avoiding each of the at most d neighbors from below
  by 1 - x, hence the probability of avoiding them all from below by
  (1 - x)^d.  The conditional probability is therefore at most
  q / (1 - x)^d <= x.  The same induction guarantees all the
  conditioning probabilities involved are positive.  Ordering the
  events arbitrarily and multiplying the (positive) conditional
  avoidance probabilities shows that avoiding all events at once has
  positive probability, i.e. is possible.  (Done.)

By (FAILV) and (DEPD), the hypothesis of the symmetric local lemma
holds here for large Delta: exp(-Delta^{0.005}) is far below
1/(e(d+1)).  So there is a choice of the flags that avoids every E_v.

8.5  Proof of Proposition K.

  Fix a trial of the flags that avoids every E_v (such a trial exists by
  8.4).  Lemma L, (VCON), and (ECON) summed over the edges incident
  with v give
        bar W_v <= W_v + o(xi) ,
        bar F_v <= F_v + tau (B+1) D_v + o(xi) ,              (PROV)
  because zeta + Delta * Delta^{-1.25} = o(xi).

  Remove the vertices colored in this trial.  At a remaining vertex v
  only the edge part of its potential changes.  Each removed neighbor u
  contributes bar D_vu >= D_vu / 2 - Delta^{-1.25} by (EXP) and (ECON).
  Using (CMASS) with b_u = D_vu, the total decrease is at least
        sum over removed neighbors u of bar D_vu
          >= (tau K / 8) D_v - zeta - Delta^{-0.25} .          (GAIN)
  Since K/8 > B+1, the potential after removal is at most F_v + o(xi).
  The same requirement with b_u = 1/Delta gives the degree bound in
  (RC), because K/4 >= 1 and 2 zeta = o(xi).

  It remains to restore (WB) without losing the mass and potential
  estimates.  To TRIM an entry means to set its weight to zero.  If its
  current positive weight is z, the change in the NON-EDGE part of its
  host's potential is exactly
        c(z) := z ( 1 - log(z / w_*) + gamma log Delta ) .     (TRIMC)

  First trim all entries with z > Delta^{-0.99}.  Their costs c(z) are
  <= 0 for large Delta, since log(Delta^{-0.99}/w_*) = 0.01 log Delta -
  O(log log Delta) and gamma < 0.01.  Next trim all positive entries
  with z < Delta^{-3}.  On this interval c(z) is increasing for large
  Delta and tends to 0 at 0, so each cost is at most
  O(Delta^{-3} log Delta); with at most k <= Delta entries at a host,
  the total cost there is o(xi).  Both operations can only decrease edge
  terms.

  At the resulting weights, mark every positive entry i with s_i >
  Lambda, and trim all marked entries SIMULTANEOUSLY.  For a marked
  entry of weight z, (TRIMC) is at most Lambda z on the restored weight
  interval.  To account for the edge loss without double counting, let
  M be the marked entries and fix a host v.  Let D_v be the edge term
  just before this last trim and D'_v the one after; then
        D_v - D'_v
          = sum_{i in M cap L(v)} w_i s_i
            + sum_{i in (V(H)\M) cap L(v)}
                w_i sum_{l in M cap N_H(i)} w_l
          >= sum_{i in M cap L(v)} w_i s_i .                   (TRIMLOSS)
  Each deleted edge in D_v has exactly one endpoint hosted at v, so it
  occurs in precisely one of the two sums.  The first sum pays for all
  the non-edge costs at v, since w_i s_i > Lambda w_i for marked
  entries, and this stays true when both ends of an edge are trimmed.
  So this last trim does not increase any F_v.  The unmarked entries
  had s_i <= Lambda before the trim, and deleting weights only reduces
  neighborhood weights, so the remaining entries satisfy (WB).

  All trims preserve availability and do not increase W_v.  Combining
  their costs with (PROV) and (GAIN) proves the mass and potential
  bounds in (RC) for sufficiently large Delta.  This completes the
  proof of Proposition K.  (Done.)

================================================================
PART 9.  ITERATION AND COMPLETION
================================================================

We finish the proof of Theorem A by iterating Proposition K.  The
potential keeps a fixed positive amount of weight at each remaining
vertex, while the maximum remaining degree falls below the number of
available entries.

  Proof of Theorem A.  Start from the empty partial coloring and the
  weights in (INIT), and set
        T_* = ceil( 2 tau^{-1} log Delta ) .                  (ROUNDS)
  Perform T_* rounds, stopping early if every vertex receives a color.
  We check inductively that the mass window (MW) stays valid.  After
  j <= T_* completed rounds, every remaining vertex satisfies
        W_v <= K + j xi ,
        gamma log Delta (K - W_v) <= F_v
          <= (K^2/C) log Delta + j xi .                       (ITERPOT)
  The upper bounds follow from (INIT) and (RC); the lower bound on F_v
  follows from the nonnegativity of the entropy and edge terms in
  (POT).  Now T_* xi = O(Delta^{-0.04} log Delta) = o(1), and
  C = 100 K / gamma, so (ITERPOT) gives
        K - K/100 - T_* xi / (gamma log Delta)
          <= W_v <= K + T_* xi .                              (MWITER)
  For sufficiently large Delta both bounds lie in [K/2, 2K].
  Availability and (WB) are preserved by Proposition K, so each
  successive round is justified.

  If some vertices remain after T_* rounds, repeated use of the degree
  inequality in (RC) gives
        deg_remaining(v) / Delta
          <= (1 - tau)^{T_*} + xi * sum_{j=0}^{T_*-1} (1 - tau)^j
          <= Delta^{-2} + xi / tau
          =  Delta^{-2} + Delta^{-0.04} ,                     (FINALDEG)
  where we used tau T_* >= 2 log Delta (so e^{-tau T_*} <= Delta^{-2})
  and the geometric sum xi sum_j (1-tau)^j <= xi/tau =
  Delta^{-0.04}.  So the remaining maximum degree is at most
  Delta^{-1} + Delta^{0.96}.

  On the other hand, the mass lower bound W_v >= K/2 together with the
  upper bound Delta^{-0.99} on each positive entry gives
        | { i in L(v) : w_i > 0 } | >= (K/2) Delta^{0.99} .    (MANY)
  For large Delta this far exceeds the remaining maximum degree
  Delta^{-1} + Delta^{0.96}.

  Color the remaining vertices in any order, choosing at each step a
  positive-weight entry compatible with the colors already chosen.
  Every such entry is available with respect to the partial coloring
  from the rounds.  Each neighbor colored during this final procedure
  forbids at most one entry (the matching condition (PART)), so
  (MANY) guarantees a choice at every step.

  The resulting coloring chooses one entry from each restricted list and
  avoids every forbidden pair, hence is also a coloring for the original
  lists and matchings.  All lower thresholds on Delta in the proof
  depend only on r.  Taking one Delta_r >= 3 above them, together with
  C_r = C from section 7.1, proves Theorem A.  (Done.)

Theorem A is proved, and Corollary B follows from it as in Part 2.  The
whole argument is now complete.

APPENDIX.  A ONE-PAGE SUMMARY AND SOME HISTORY
================================================================

A1.  The proof in a few sentences, for quick recall.

  Build a partial correspondence coloring in rounds.  At any moment the
  still-usable tokens are weighted; the tokens form a K_r-free graph H
  split into one part per list, with at most one neighbor per part.  The
  round activates tokens with probability proportional to weight, keeps
  the "successful" ones (no neighbor activated) as colors, and corrects
  the weight bookkeeping against a potential F_v = entropy + linear
  penalty + incident edge mass D_v.  Edges are handled to first order by
  the mean-one compensation; TRIANGLES are the only obstruction, and the
  local weight adjustment lemma (Parts 4 to 6) produces small random
  edits, itself built from (a) finite-dimensional duality turning the
  many vertex inequalities into one priced triangle bound, (b) a sparse
  multiplier / local cross estimate, and (c) a vertex-splitting walk
  argument that preserves triangle mass while shrinking neighborhoods.
  That lemma both offsets the triangles (to a constant multiple of the
  edge mass) and bounds how many tokens of any one list an edit touches,
  which is what makes the weights concentrate (read-k concentration,
  Lemma M) and lets a Lovasz local lemma run all the per-vertex
  estimates at once.  With tau the per-round activation rate, after
  about tau^{-1} log Delta rounds every survivor has remaining degree
  Delta^{-1} + Delta^{0.96}, far below its (K/2) Delta^{0.99} positive
  tokens, and greedy finishes.

A2.  Some history, kept brief, with the key works named.

  - List coloring: Vizing (1976) and Erdos, Rubin, Taylor (1980).
  - Correspondence (DP) coloring: Dvorak and Postle (2015 preprint,
    published 2018).
  - Logarithmic list bounds under girth or clique restrictions:
    Kim (1995, girth >= 5); Johansson (triangle-free and K_r-free, with
    a log log Delta loss for cliques); Molloy (2019) for leading
    constants and a new clique-free proof; presentations in Molloy and
    Reed (2002) and Bansal, Gupta, Guruganesh (2018).
  - Correspondence versions: Bernshteyn (2016, triangle-free; 2019,
    the Johansson-Molloy bounds, giving O_r(Delta log log Delta / log
    Delta) for fixed r before this paper).
  - Other forbidden-subgraph results: Alon, Krivelevich, Sudakov
    (1999); Vu (2002, list form); Anderson, Bernshteyn, Dhawan (2023,
    2025); Dhawan, Janzer, Methuku (2025, ordinary coloring of
    K_{t,t,t}-free and three-colorable forbidden graphs).
  - Ingredients used here: the sparse-subgraph device of Ajtai, Erdos,
    Komlos, Szemeredi (1981); Angel and Spinka's coupling; the
    concentration of Gavinsky, Lovett, Saks, Srinivasan and Finner's
    generalized Holder inequality; Hoeffding's exponential-moment
    bound; the Lovasz local lemma.  The structural core adapts the
    companion manuscript "A logarithmic independence bound for
    clique-free graphs" (OpenAI, 2026).

  Conjecture 3.1 of Alon, Krivelevich, Sudakov (1999) asked for
  O(Delta/log Delta) coloring when any fixed graph is forbidden; this
  note proves it in the stronger correspondence form, and shows the
  order Delta/log Delta cannot be improved when the forbidden graph has
  a cycle (Bollobas, 1978).
